## Introduction

An air separation unit (ASU) separates air into a gaseous oxygen product, a gaseous nitrogen product and a crude argon product by cryogenic rectification: the air is compressed, purified, cooled to near its dew point and distilled in a pair of coupled columns, the arrangement shown on the diagram in the Process panel. No chemical reaction takes place; the separation is physical, and its cost is the work of compression and refrigeration. This panel derives every relation the page's model computes, in the order in which the model uses them: the component balances that fix the product flow rates, the isothermal equation that gives the compressor power, the entropy-of-mixing sum that gives the minimum work of separation and the second-law efficiency, the Clausius–Clapeyron estimate of the saturation temperatures at the two column pressures, and the capacity-scaling relation that gives the capital cost and the unit cost.

Air separation is the standard route to oxygen and nitrogen at large scale and high purity. Oxygen is used in steelmaking, combustion enrichment and chemical oxidation; nitrogen is used as an inert atmosphere in chemical plants, food packaging and electronics manufacture; argon is used as a shielding gas in welding (Process panel, Process overview). The products are gases that are expensive to transport relative to their value, so the plant is sized to its market, and its economics turn on two quantities: the electric power consumed per tonne of oxygen, which is the largest single operating cost, and the capital cost per tonne of capacity, which falls as the plant grows. The model of this page computes both, and the key points below are stated in terms of them.

## Key points

Quantities in this section are in the units of the App panel: flow rates in kilomoles per hour (kmol/h) or tonnes per day (t/d), power in kilowatts (kW), specific power in kilowatt-hours per tonne of contained oxygen (kWh/t) and cost in United States dollars (USD). The default inputs are a feed flow rate of 1000 kmol/h, an oxygen recovery of 0.943, a nitrogen recovery of 0.638, an argon recovery of 0.90, product purities of 99.0, 99.5 and 90.0 mol percent, a compressor discharge pressure of 600 kPa and an electricity price of 0.07 USD/kWh.

1. Recoveries and purities fix the products; the waste stream closes the balance, and feasibility is the check. Each product flow rate follows from a recovery and a purity, and each waste component flow rate is what remains of the feed. Guided experiment: in the App, raise the oxygen recovery from 0.943 to 0.97. The contained oxygen rate rises from 151.8 t/d to 156.1 t/d and the oxygen product rate from 153.1 t/d to 157.5 t/d, the waste oxygen flow rate falls from 9.0 kmol/h to 3.3 kmol/h, and, because the compressor power does not depend on the recovery, the specific power falls from 250.2 kWh/t to 243.3 kWh/t. Then set both the oxygen and the nitrogen recoveries to 1.0: the waste nitrogen and oxygen flow rates become negative (−2.6 kmol/h and −4.4 kmol/h) and the results carry the infeasibility notice, because the products would withdraw more of each component than the feed contains.
2. Compressor work grows with the logarithm of the pressure ratio, not with the pressure. The discharge pressure also sets the nitrogen saturation temperature at which the main condenser/reboiler of the Process panel operates. Guided experiment: in the App, raise the compressor discharge pressure from 600 kPa to 1200 kPa. The total power rises from 1582 kW to 2198 kW, a factor of 1.39 for a doubling of pressure, and the specific power rises from 250.2 kWh/t to 347.6 kWh/t, moving out of the reported commercial range of 200 kWh/t to 260 kWh/t. The nitrogen saturation estimate rises from 97.4 K to 108.3 K, further above the oxygen saturation estimate of 92.7 K at the low-pressure column pressure: the pressure is what makes the condensation of nitrogen against boiling oxygen possible.
3. A purity change at fixed recovery moves the product total, not the contained oxygen, the power or the unit cost. The purity divides the recovered component flow rate to give the product total, and the remainder of the product is its assigned impurity. Guided experiment: in the App, lower the oxygen product purity from 99.0 mol percent to 95.0 mol percent. The oxygen product flow rate rises from 199.6 kmol/h to 208.0 kmol/h (153.1 t/d to 158.8 t/d) while the contained oxygen rate stays at 151.8 t/d, the specific power stays at 250.2 kWh/t and the unit cost stays at 61.8 USD/t; the minimum work falls from 52.1 kWh/t to 48.9 kWh/t, because a less pure product is a less complete separation. The model computes no purity penalty on the power; the Theory text below states the penalty reported for real plants.
4. The minimum work rises as the separation sharpens, and the plant converts about one fifth of its power into separation work at the default inputs. The minimum work of separation is 330 kW at the default inputs against a total power of 1582 kW, a second-law efficiency of 0.21. Guided experiment: in the App, raise the oxygen recovery from 0.943 to 0.97 and the nitrogen recovery from 0.638 to 0.85 together. The nitrogen product rate rises from 337.0 t/d to 449.0 t/d, the minimum work rises from 330 kW to 345 kW (52.1 kWh/t to 53.1 kWh/t), and the second-law efficiency rises from 0.208 to 0.218: more complete recovery is more separation, and the entropy-of-mixing sum charges for it.
5. Scale dilutes the capital cost and leaves the specific power unchanged. The capital cost grows with capacity to the power 0.70, while the compressor equation is linear in flow rate. Guided experiment: in the App, raise the feed flow rate from 1000 kmol/h to 4000 kmol/h. The contained oxygen capacity rises from 151.8 t/d to 607.1 t/d, the capital cost rises from 16.7 million USD to 44.2 million USD (a factor of 2.65 for a factor of four in capacity), the unit cost falls from 61.8 USD/t to 46.7 USD/t, and the specific power stays at 250.2 kWh/t. The unit-cost-against-capacity chart of the App shows the same dilution as a curve.

## Scope of the model

The page computes a component-balance model of the process shown on the diagram, an isothermal estimate of the compressor power, the minimum work of separation and the second-law efficiency for the product slate the balance specifies, a saturation-temperature estimate for the main condenser/reboiler, and a scaling estimate of cost. The model computes no column internals (no stage-by-stage rectification, no temperature profiles and no hydraulic quantities); product flow rates follow from specified recoveries and purities, and every other quantity follows from those flow rates. The Theory section derives only what the App tab and the Code panel compute. The physical explanation of the separation itself — vapour–liquid equilibrium, rectification and refrigeration — is derived in the Process section, where the claims are made.

## Component balances and product flow rates

The feed flow rate is denoted $F$ (kmol/h), where kmol denotes kilomole and h denotes hour. The feed component molar flow rates (kmol/h) are the products of $F$ and the mole fractions of dry air for nitrogen (N2), oxygen (O2) and argon (Ar): 0.7809, 0.2095 and 0.0093, renormalised to a sum of one after the trace gases are assigned to the nitrogen fraction.

For each product, the recovered component is fixed by a recovery. The oxygen recovery, denoted $R_{O2}$, is the fraction of the feed oxygen flow rate that leaves in the oxygen product; the nitrogen recovery $R_{N2}$ and the argon recovery $R_{Ar}$ are defined in the same way for the nitrogen and crude argon products. A purity fixes the total product flow rate: for the oxygen product, with oxygen purity denoted $x_{O2}$ (mol percent), the oxygen component flow rate is divided by $x_{O2}/100$ to give the product total, and the remainder is assigned to nitrogen, following the impurity assignment of the stream table of the diagram (oxygen product impurity nitrogen; nitrogen product impurity oxygen; crude argon impurity split equally between nitrogen and oxygen).

The waste component flow rates are then determined, not specified: for each component, the waste flow rate equals the feed flow rate minus the three product flow rates. The overall and per-component balances close exactly by construction, and the verification value of the construction is the feasibility test: a negative waste component shows that the specified recoveries and purities are jointly infeasible, because the products would withdraw more of that component than the feed contains.

Worked example. Take a feed flow rate $F$ of 1000 kmol/h, an oxygen recovery $R_{O2}$ of 0.943 and an oxygen purity $x_{O2}$ of 99.0 mol percent. The feed oxygen flow rate is $1000 \times 0.2095 = 209.5$ kmol/h, renormalised in the model to 209.6 kmol/h. The oxygen component flow rate in the oxygen product is $0.943 \times 209.6 = 197.6$ kmol/h. Division by the purity as a fraction, $197.6 / 0.990$, gives an oxygen product flow rate of 199.6 kmol/h. The calculation gives the sample value of the diagram, 200 kmol/h, to the precision of the drawing; the difference is the rounding of the sample basis.

## Mass flow rates and production rates

The mass flow rate of a stream (kg/h), where kg denotes kilogram, is the sum over components of the component molar flow rate multiplied by the component molar mass (kg/kmol): nitrogen 28.013, oxygen 31.999 and argon 39.948. A production rate in tonnes per day (t/d), where t denotes tonne and d denotes day, is the mass flow rate multiplied by 24 and divided by 1000. The contained oxygen rate is the production rate of the oxygen component alone within the oxygen product stream; it is the capacity basis used for the specific power and the unit cost, so that a change of product purity does not disguise itself as a change of capacity.

## Compressor power from the isothermal equation

The main air compressor power is derived from the work of compression per kilomole. For a reversible change at constant temperature, the shaft work input per kilomole, denoted $dw$ (kJ/kmol), where kJ denotes kilojoule and kmol denotes kilomole, equals the pressure $p$ (kPa) multiplied by the decrease of the molar volume $V$ (m3/kmol, on the same kilomole basis as the gas constant below), with the sign taken so that compression is a positive input:

$$dw = -p \, dV$$

For an ideal gas at constant temperature $T_1$ (K), the molar volume is $V = R \, T_1 / p$, where $R$ is the universal gas constant (8.314 kJ/(kmol K), where K denotes kelvin). Substitution gives $dV = -R \, T_1 \, dp / p^2$, and the work per kilomole becomes $dw = R \, T_1 \, dp / p$. Integration from the suction pressure $p_1$ (kPa) to the discharge pressure $p_2$ (kPa) gives the isothermal work per kilomole, $R \, T_1 \ln(p_2/p_1)$, where $\ln$ denotes the natural logarithm. The logarithm is the content of the derivation: work accumulates in equal increments of the logarithm of pressure, not of pressure, so doubling the pressure ratio adds a fixed increment of work rather than doubling the work.

Multiplication by the molar flow rate and division by the isothermal efficiency gives the main air compressor power, denoted $P_{MAC}$ (kW), where kW denotes kilowatt:

$$P_{MAC} = \frac{\dot{n} \, R \, T_1 \, \ln(p_2/p_1)}{\eta_{iso}} \tag{1}$$

where $\dot{n}$ is the molar flow rate (kmol/s) and $\eta_{iso}$ is the isothermal efficiency, defined as the isothermal ideal power divided by the actual power, which accounts for the departure of a real intercooled multi-stage machine from the isothermal ideal. The isothermal equation is the appropriate idealisation for an intercooled compressor, whose stage outlets are returned toward the suction temperature by the intercoolers. The molar flow rate $\dot{n}$ is the feed flow rate $F$ (kmol/h) divided by 3600 s/h, where s denotes second: at the default basis, $\dot{n} = 1000 / 3600 = 0.278$ kmol/s.

Worked example. At the default inputs ($\dot{n} = 0.278$ kmol/s, $T_1 = 293$ K, $p_2 = 600$ kPa, $p_1 = 101$ kPa, $\eta_{iso} = 0.80$), Eq. (1) gives $P_{MAC} = 0.2778 \times 8.314 \times 293 \times \ln(600/101) / 0.80 = 1507$ kW. The calculation gives the value the App tab displays for the compressor power, before the corrections of the next paragraph.

The total plant power, denoted $P$ (kW), is the compressor power corrected by two stated fractions: an auxiliary fraction (default 0.10) for cooling-water circulation, purification regeneration heating and product handling, and an expansion turbine work credit (default 0.05) for the shaft work recovered at the turboexpander. Both fractions are empirical allowances of the model, stated as fractions of the compressor power; neither is computed from the expander stream of the diagram, and a change in either fraction moves the total power in direct proportion. The specific power (kWh/t), where kWh denotes kilowatt-hour, is the total power divided by the contained oxygen production rate (t/h). At the default inputs the total power is 1582 kW and the specific power is 250 kWh/t. Reported values for commercial cryogenic plants lie in the range of approximately 200 to 260 kWh per tonne of oxygen at purities of 95 to 99.5 mol percent [8][9][10] (Economics section); the default inputs give a value at the upper part of that range, because the isothermal efficiency and the pressure are set conservatively. The model computes no purity penalty: the compressor power depends on the feed flow rate alone, so a change of oxygen purity at fixed recovery leaves the specific power unchanged, against the penalty of approximately one fifth between 95 and 99.5 mol percent reported in the cited study [9]. The purity input is used only where the balance uses it, in the product flow rates.

## Minimum work of separation and second-law efficiency

The minimum work of separation is the least work input that can produce the specified products from the feed at the ambient temperature, for an ideal-gas mixture. Mixing is the reverse of separation: the entropy increase on mixing the separated components back into the feed mixture, reversed in sign, sets the minimum work. For one stream, the mixing sum is the sum over components of the component molar flow rate multiplied by the natural logarithm of its mole fraction within that stream. The minimum work, denoted $W_{min}$ (kW), is the gas constant multiplied by the ambient temperature, taken as the suction temperature $T_1$, multiplied by the increase of the mixing sum from feed to products:

$$W_{min} = \frac{R \, T_1}{3600} \left[ \sum_{products} \sum_i n_i \ln x_i \; - \; \sum_i n_{i,feed} \ln x_{i,feed} \right] \tag{2}$$

where $n_i$ is the component molar flow rate (kmol/h) in the stream indicated, $x_i$ is the mole fraction of component $i$ within that stream, the product sum runs over the oxygen, nitrogen, crude argon and waste streams, and the factor 3600 converts hours to seconds. Each product is less mixed than the feed, so each product mixing sum is closer to zero than the feed sum and the bracket is positive.

Worked example. At the default inputs, Eq. (2) gives $W_{min} = 330$ kW, which divided by the contained oxygen production rate of 6.32 t/h gives a minimum specific work of 52.1 kWh/t. The calculation gives the range of approximately 51 to 53 kWh/t reported for cryogenic air separation [8], computed here for the product slate of the model rather than quoted. Complete separation of the same feed into pure components would require 382 kW, which is 60.3 kWh/t on the same contained oxygen basis; the specified products require less because the waste stream remains a mixture and the products are not pure.

The second-law efficiency, denoted $\eta_{II}$ (dimensionless), is the minimum work divided by the total power:

$$\eta_{II} = \frac{W_{min}}{P} \tag{3}$$

At the default inputs, $\eta_{II} = 330 / 1582 = 0.21$. Across the reported commercial range of specific power, 200 to 260 kWh/t, the second-law efficiency of a cryogenic plant on this basis lies between approximately 0.20 and 0.26. The difference between the total power and the minimum work is the sum of the irreversibilities of the plant: compressor inefficiency, heat transfer across finite temperature differences in the main heat exchanger, throttling, pressure drop and heat leak into the cold box. The model does not apportion the difference among those terms.

## Saturation temperature at the condenser/reboiler pressures

The main condenser/reboiler of the Process section operates because the saturation temperature of nitrogen at the pressure-column pressure exceeds the saturation temperature of oxygen at the low-pressure column pressure. The model estimates both temperatures from the integrated Clausius–Clapeyron equation, which relates the vapour pressure $p^*$ (kPa) of a pure component to temperature under the assumptions that the vapour is an ideal gas, the liquid volume is negligible against the vapour volume, and the latent heat of vaporisation, denoted $\Delta H_{vap}$ (kJ/mol), is constant over the interval:

$$\ln \frac{p^*_2}{p^*_1} = -\frac{\Delta H_{vap}}{R_m} \left( \frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1} \right) \tag{4}$$

where $R_m$ is the universal gas constant on a molar basis (8.314 J/(mol K), where J denotes joule) and, in this section only, the subscripts 1 and 2 denote two saturation states and $T_1$ denotes the anchor saturation temperature (77.4 K for nitrogen, 90.2 K for oxygen), not the compressor suction temperature of Eq. (1). Anchoring state 1 at the normal boiling point (nitrogen 77.4 K at 101.325 kPa, $\Delta H_{vap} = 5.57$ kJ/mol; oxygen 90.2 K at 101.325 kPa, $\Delta H_{vap} = 6.82$ kJ/mol [40]) and solving Eq. (4) for the temperature at the column pressure gives the estimate. At 560 kPa the nitrogen estimate is 96.4 K and at 130 kPa the oxygen estimate is 92.7 K, a pure-component difference of 3.7 K. The equation-of-state values are 95.5 K and 92.6 K [40], a difference of 2.8 K; the constant-latent-heat assumption accounts for most of the difference between the two estimates. The operating temperature difference of the exchanger, 1 K to 2 K [26], is smaller than either pure-component difference, because the condensing stream is not pure nitrogen and the boiling sump is not pure oxygen; mixture saturation temperatures lie between the pure values. The App tab reports the two estimates at the compressor discharge pressure and at the low-pressure column pressure of the model (default 130 kPa).


## Capital cost scaling and unit cost

The capital cost estimate, denoted $C$ (USD), where USD denotes United States dollars, scales a reference plant by capacity:

$$C = C_{ref} \left( \frac{Q}{Q_{ref}} \right)^{e} \tag{5}$$

where $Q$ is the contained oxygen capacity (t/d) of the case, $Q_{ref}$ is the reference capacity (2470 t/d), $C_{ref}$ is the reference capital cost (118 million USD in 2018 dollars, the 2018 Lipetsk project of approximately 100 million euros at the 2018 average exchange rate of 1.18 USD per euro [39], for a plant of 72,000 normal cubic metres per hour (Nm3/h)) and $e$ is the capacity exponent (0.70). A normal cubic metre is the volume of a gas at 0 degrees Celsius and 101.325 kPa; at the density of oxygen under those conditions, 1.429 kg/Nm3, the reference capacity is $72{,}000 \times 1.429 \times 24 / 1000 = 2470$ t/d. The exponent expresses that cost grows more slowly than capacity, because vessel wall area, instrumentation, controls and site works do not double when throughput doubles [37][38]. Doubling capacity at an exponent of 0.70 multiplies cost by $2^{0.70} = 1.62$ and reduces capital cost per tonne of capacity by approximately 19 percent. The scaling relation is used only within a single-train configuration; a capacity that requires parallel trains does not follow the relation. The exponent is a modelling assumption of this page, taken from the general chemical-plant range of 0.6 to 0.7 [37][38]: the verified sources collected for the Economics section contain project cost points but no air-separation-specific scaling exponent, and the page states the assumption wherever the estimate is used. All capital costs of the model are in 2018 dollars; escalation to a later year multiplies a cost by the ratio of the Chemical Engineering Plant Cost Index of the later year to that of 2018, and no escalation is applied in the model.

Annual costs combine three terms. The electricity cost is the total power multiplied by the operating hours per year (default 8000 h/yr, an availability of 91.3 percent of the 8760 hours of a year) and the electricity price (USD/kWh). The non-electricity operating cost is taken as a fraction (default 0.04) of the capital cost per year; the fraction is anchored to a commercial report of maintenance at 2 to 4 percent of capital cost per year [17], and its use for maintenance, labour and consumables together is an assumption of the model, stated here and in the Economics section. The capital charge is the capital cost multiplied by the capital recovery factor, which annualises an investment at discount rate $i$ over a plant life of $n$ years (yr). The capital recovery factor, denoted $CRF$ (1/yr), follows from the annuity condition that $n$ equal annual charges $A$ (USD/yr), discounted at the rate $i$, repay the investment $C$: $C = A \sum_{k=1}^{n} (1+i)^{-k}$, where $k$ is the year index. The sum is a geometric series with the closed form $\left[1 - (1+i)^{-n}\right] / i$. Solving for the ratio $A / C$ gives

$$CRF = \frac{i \, (1+i)^{n}}{(1+i)^{n} - 1} \tag{6}$$

At the default inputs ($i = 0.08$, $n = 25$ yr), $CRF = 0.0937$ per year: the annual charge is 9.37 percent of the capital cost, against 4.00 percent for interest-free spreading over the same life, which is the limit of Eq. (6) as $i$ approaches zero, $CRF \to 1/n$.

Worked example. At the default inputs the contained oxygen capacity is 151.8 t/d, and Eq. (5) gives a capital cost of $118 \times (151.8/2470)^{0.70} = 16.7$ million USD. The annual terms are: capital charge $0.0937 \times 16.7 = 1.57$ million USD/yr; electricity $1582 \times 8000 \times 0.07 = 0.89$ million USD/yr; non-electricity operating cost $0.04 \times 16.7 = 0.67$ million USD/yr. Annual contained oxygen production is $151.8 \times 8000 / 24 = 50{,}589$ t/yr. Division of each annual term by the annual production gives the unit cost by term: capital charge 31.0 USD/t, electricity 17.5 USD/t and non-electricity operating cost 13.2 USD/t, a unit cost of 61.8 USD/t in 2018 dollars, with all cost allocated to the oxygen product and no credit for the nitrogen or argon products. The unit cost (USD/t) in general is the sum of the three annual terms divided by the annual contained oxygen production (t/yr). The capacity sweep of the App tab varies the feed flow rate so that the contained oxygen capacity takes each swept value, holding all other inputs fixed; the specific power is invariant along the sweep because the compressor equation is linear in flow rate, and the decline of the unit cost along the sweep is the dilution of the scaled capital cost. In a real plant the specific power also falls with size, because larger compressors and cold boxes are more efficient; the model does not compute that effect, and the sweep understates the benefit of scale on the energy term.

## Validation

The model is validated by construction checks and limiting cases, all executed in the Code panel walkthrough. The closure check asserts that products plus waste reproduce the feed to a maximum error below $10^{-6}$ kmol/h. The anchor check reproduces the sample basis of the diagram: at a feed flow rate of 1000 kmol/h, an oxygen recovery of 0.943 and an oxygen purity of 99.0 mol percent, the oxygen product flow rate is 199.6 kmol/h against the 200 kmol/h of the drawing. The minimum-work check reproduces the reported range: at the default inputs Eq. (2) gives 52.1 kWh/t, within the approximately 51 to 53 kWh/t of the cited sources [8]. The saturation check reproduces the equation-of-state temperatures of the data source [40] to within 1.0 K for nitrogen at 560 kPa and 0.1 K for oxygen at 130 kPa. At an oxygen recovery of zero the oxygen product vanishes. At oxygen and nitrogen recoveries of one, the feasibility flag is false, as the Theory section predicts.

## Conclusion and discussion

The five key points reduce the plant to the quantities that decide its design. The product slate is a balance statement before it is a separation result (key point 1): recoveries and purities fix every product flow rate, the waste stream absorbs the remainder, and a negative waste component is the signal that a specification cannot be met from air. The energy bill is set at the compressor (key point 2): work accumulates with the logarithm of the pressure ratio, and the same pressure level is what allows nitrogen to condense against boiling oxygen in the condenser/reboiler, so the pressure is chosen by the separation and paid for in power. Purity and recovery are not interchangeable levers (key points 3 and 4): at fixed recovery a lower purity only dilutes the product with its impurity, while a higher recovery is a sharper separation, charged for in minimum work; the second-law efficiency of about 0.21 at the default inputs measures how far the real plant, at 250.2 kWh/t, stands above the 52.1 kWh/t minimum. Scale acts on cost alone within this model (key point 5): the unit cost falls from 61.8 USD/t to 46.7 USD/t between 151.8 t/d and 607.1 t/d of contained oxygen because the capital cost grows with the 0.70 power of capacity, while the specific power, linear in flow rate, does not move. The limits of these statements are the limits stated in the scope section: the model computes no column internals and no purity penalty on power, so the guided experiments show the structure of the trade-offs, and the cited plant data of the Economics panel supply the magnitudes the model omits.
