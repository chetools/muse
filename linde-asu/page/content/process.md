## Process overview

An air separation unit (ASU) takes in ordinary air and delivers its three main components separately: oxygen, nitrogen and argon. No chemical reaction takes place anywhere in the plant, and no catalyst is used: air is already a mixture of the products, and the plant's whole task is to unmix it. Customers buy the components apart because each does a job the mixture cannot. Oxygen supports combustion and oxidation, in steelmaking, in combustion enrichment and in chemical oxidation. Nitrogen is valued for the opposite property, that it does not react: it provides an inert atmosphere in chemical plants, in food packaging and in electronics manufacture. Argon, also inert and denser than air, shields welding arcs from the air around them. The unit described here is the Linde double-column plant, the arrangement that has dominated large-scale air separation for a century.

Unmixing air is difficult for one basic reason: at ordinary temperatures the three components are gases, mixed molecule by molecule, and no difference between them that exists at room temperature is large enough to separate them at the scale and purity of this plant. Distillation is the separation used. Air has no liquid phase at ordinary temperatures. It begins to condense only below approximately 80 kelvin (K), so the whole process is a distillation carried out at a temperature no ordinary refrigerator reaches. Air contains, by volume, approximately 78 percent nitrogen (N2), 21 percent oxygen (O2) and 1 percent argon (Ar); the diagram below is drawn for one worked example, called the sample basis, and its exact feed composition is stated with it.

A difference in boiling point is the physical fact the separation rests on. At atmospheric pressure the three components boil at different temperatures: nitrogen at 77.4 K, argon at 87.3 K and oxygen at 90.2 K. Stated the other way, at one temperature the vapour pressure of nitrogen is higher than the vapour pressure of oxygen [41]. Vapour formed from a boiling mixture of the two is therefore richer in nitrogen than the liquid it leaves, and a column of many repeated counter-current contacts — rectification, in the originator's term [41] — accumulates that enrichment until the products are nearly pure. How hard each separation is follows from the size of the difference. Nitrogen and oxygen differ by 12.8 K, a workable difference. Argon and oxygen differ by only 2.9 K, which is why separating argon takes far more contacts than separating nitrogen from oxygen.

Two difficulties follow, and the arrangement of the plant is the answer to both. Cold comes first. Refrigeration at 80 K cannot be bought; the plant must manufacture its own, inside the process. Air itself supplies the refrigeration. Air is compressed and cooled back to ordinary temperature against cooling water. High-pressure air is then cooled far below ordinary temperature in one large heat exchanger (the main heat exchanger), where the cold products leaving the plant absorb its heat. A part of that air, now near the temperature at which it begins to condense, is expanded in a turbine, where the gas does work as it expands and leaves far colder than it entered; the expanded part cools the rest. Exchange against the products also protects the cold: products leave near ordinary temperature, so refrigeration invested in them is recovered instead of thrown away. Everything that operates at these temperatures sits inside one insulated enclosure, the cold box.

A column also needs two things at once: vapour rising, which is made by boiling liquid at the column base, and liquid descending, which is made by condensing vapour at the column top. A first Linde plant, of 1902, rectified in a single column: pure oxygen was delivered, but nitrogen left carrying approximately 7 percent oxygen, because the column had no good source of pure liquid nitrogen for its top [41]. In 1910 the double column replaced the single column, and the arrangement on the diagram dates from that change. Two columns are stacked: a pressure column below, operating at approximately six times atmospheric pressure, and a low-pressure column above it, at little more than atmospheric pressure. Between them sits one heat exchanger doing two jobs, the main condenser/reboiler. Its characterised principle, in Linde's statement, is the combination of the condenser and the evaporator into a common heat exchanger unit, which divides the rectification into two areas at different pressures [41]. A fact from thermodynamics explains why the double column works: temperature at which a liquid boils, or a vapour condenses, rises with pressure. At the pressure of the lower column, nitrogen condenses at a temperature high enough to boil oxygen in the upper column, so the nitrogen vapour at the top of the pressure column condenses against the boiling oxygen at the base of the low-pressure column, in the one shared exchanger. Condensate from the shared exchanger supplies the pure liquid nitrogen the single column lacked: one part returns as reflux (liquid returned to the top of a column) to the pressure column, and another part, after further cooling below its condensation temperature (subcooling), returns as reflux to the low-pressure column. Argon is handled by a third, smaller column at the side, fed from the point in the low-pressure column where the argon content of the vapour is highest.

Nearly all the work of the plant enters at one machine, the main air compressor: the electricity consumed in compression is the dominant operating cost of the process, and the price of electricity and the efficiency of the compressor decide whether a plant is competitive. Capital behaves in the opposite direction: a plant of twice the capacity costs substantially less than twice as much, so the cost per tonne falls as plants grow, which is why the largest plants are very large. Work concentrated in one machine, capital diluted by scale, and refrigeration produced on site and recovered: those three facts are the economics of the process in outline, and each is a consequence of the arrangement described above.

Two parts divide the process: a warm front end, where air is filtered, compressed, cooled and purified at ordinary temperatures, and the cold section inside the cold box, where heat exchange, expansion and rectification take place at temperatures down to approximately 90 K. One preparation in the warm end is not optional: air carries trace amounts of water vapour, carbon dioxide (CO2) and hydrocarbons, and all three would freeze solid at cold-section temperatures and block the passages there, so they are removed by adsorption before the air is cooled; the adsorbent is regenerated thermally and reused, and it is the one consumable separation medium of the process. On the diagram the products carry their trade names: gaseous oxygen (GOX), gaseous nitrogen (GAN), a waste nitrogen stream and crude argon. Equipment tags follow the convention of the diagram: F denotes a filter, C a compressor, E a heat exchanger, V a vessel, X an expansion machine, T a column and VLV a valve, each followed by a number that identifies the item on the diagram. The arrangement on the process flow diagram comprises 15 equipment items and 30 numbered streams on a sample basis of 1000 kilomoles per hour (kmol/h) of feed air. Published Linde accounts of the double-column process are the source of the process description [22][24], and equipment sections follow the order in which air meets the equipment.

## Air filtration (F-101)

Atmospheric air is drawn through a filter that removes dust and particulates that would foul the compressor and the downstream adsorbent. A barrier device (fabric or cartridge elements) forms the filter, with a small pressure drop; filtered air of stream 2 remains at approximately ambient temperature and pressure.

## Main air compression (C-101)

The main air compressor (MAC) raises the air to the pressure column pressure, approximately 560 to 650 kilopascals (kPa) in the sample basis (stream 3 at 625 kPa). Industrial machines are multi-stage centrifugal compressors with interstage cooling, which approaches isothermal compression and is the basis of the power estimate in the Theory section. Compression is the dominant energy input of the plant: the electricity consumed by the compressor train is the largest single operating cost (Economics section). Intercoolers limit the discharge temperature of an intercooled machine. A stage calculation estimates the value, on stated assumptions: equal pressure ratio in each stage, intercooling that returns each stage inlet to the suction temperature of 293 K, and a heat-capacity ratio of 1.4. At an overall pressure ratio of 6.2 in three stages, the stage pressure ratio is 1.84, the isentropic stage outlet temperature is 349 K, and at a stage efficiency of 0.75 the stage outlet temperature is 367 K (94 degrees Celsius). The sample basis of the diagram shows 175 degrees Celsius at the compressor discharge before the aftercooler (stream 3), above the three-stage intercooled estimate; the sample value is retained on the drawing as a sample value and is not a stage calculation.

## Aftercooling (E-101)

The aftercooler cools the compressed air to near ambient temperature (22 degrees Celsius in stream 4) against cooling water and condenses a first part of the water vapour, which is separated and drained. In the Linde arrangement the stage is a direct-contact washing and cooling tower: the water contact also removes soluble contaminants (sulphur dioxide, sulphur trioxide and ammonia) from the air [22]. Cooling at this point reduces the water load on the purification unit by more than half, because the saturation water content of air decreases steeply with temperature at fixed pressure.

## Molecular sieve purification (V-101)

The purification unit removes water vapour, carbon dioxide, nitrous oxide and hydrocarbons by adsorption in two or more vessels operated in turn: one vessel adsorbs while the other is regenerated with heated waste nitrogen. Layering of the bed places an activated alumina layer first, which removes most of the water, followed by a molecular sieve layer (zeolite type 13X), which removes carbon dioxide and the remaining contaminants [25]. The on-line period of a vessel is typically 4 to 10 hours before switchover [25], and purified air is specified at outlet contents of the order of one part per million by volume for both water and carbon dioxide. Removal is a process requirement, not a product specification: water and carbon dioxide freeze at cold-section temperatures and would deposit as solids in the main heat exchanger and the columns, and hydrocarbons (acetylene in particular) concentrate in liquid oxygen and create an explosion hazard (Safety section). Purified air of stream 5 is specified at a carbon dioxide content of the order of one part per million by volume (ppmv, parts per million by volume) and a water dew point of the order of minus 65 to minus 75 degrees Celsius, low enough that residual water freezes out only in passages designed for periodic deriming (a warm-up of a cold-section item to melt and remove deposited solids) rather than in the column packing. Adsorption is a physical, reversible uptake in the zeolite pore structure; no reaction and no catalyst are involved, and regeneration restores the adsorbent by heating and purging.

## Main heat exchanger (E-102)

The main heat exchanger (MHE) is a brazed aluminium plate-fin exchanger in which purified air is cooled to approximately its dew point (stream 6 at minus 171 degrees Celsius, 568 kPa) against the three cold product streams leaving the cold section (oxygen, nitrogen and waste nitrogen, streams 19 to 21). A single exchanger performs the heat integration of the whole plant: the returning products, still at low temperature, cool the feed in counter-current flow, absorbing its heat and leaving near ambient temperature (streams 22 to 24). Without the recovery, the products would be vented cold and the feed would have to be refrigerated from ambient temperature by expansion alone, at a multiple of the compressor power. Products leave a few kelvin below the entering air temperature; temperature approach at the warm end is a few kelvin, and a larger approach is a direct loss of refrigeration and of compressor power. The size of the loss follows from the feed rate: a warm-end slip of 3 K on 1000 kmol/h of air, at a molar heat capacity of approximately 29 kJ/(kmol K) [40], discards approximately 24 kW of refrigeration. That refrigeration must be produced again by expansion at temperatures near 100 K, where each watt of refrigeration costs several watts of shaft work, so a few kelvin at the warm end costs a multiple of 24 kW at the compressor. A brazed aluminium plate-fin unit serves as the exchanger because several streams at cryogenic temperature must exchange heat in one compact block, and aluminium retains ductility at those temperatures (Safety section). Passages for the condensing and vaporising streams operate close to the phase boundaries, which is why freeze-out of residual contaminants is a design concern (Safety section).

## Expansion turbine (X-101)

Refrigeration is produced by expansion of a part stream of the cooled air (stream 8, 200 kmol/h of the 1000 kmol/h basis) in an expansion turbine (a turboexpander) to the low-pressure column pressure (stream 9 at 138 kPa, minus 182 degrees Celsius). Expansion is approximately isentropic; the temperature decrease of the expanded stream, together with the work recovered at the turbine shaft, supplies the refrigeration that balances heat leak into the cold box, the warm-end approach loss of the main heat exchanger and the withdrawal of cold products. Together, front end and cold section form one refrigerator, and the sequence answers the apparent paradox that compression, which heats the air and consumes most of the plant power, is the route to cold. Compression raises the pressure at ambient temperature, where the heat of compression is rejected to cooling water at low cost. Heat exchange against the cold products then cools the high-pressure air to near its dew point at almost no additional work. Expansion from that state, in the turbine or across a valve, produces temperatures that no cooling-water system can reach, because the temperature decrease is produced by the gas itself giving up energy, not by contact with a colder utility. At steady state the refrigeration produced by expansion balances the heat leak into the cold box, the warm-end approach loss of the main heat exchanger and the cold withdrawn in any liquid products, and no external refrigerator operates. Start-up begins from ambient temperature with the same sequence: expansion of high-pressure air cools the equipment progressively, the first liquid forms in the columns, and rectification begins once liquid levels are established.

Turbine and throttling valves both reduce pressure, and the two devices are not interchangeable. Expansion in the turbine is approximately isentropic: the gas does shaft work, energy leaves with the work, and the temperature decrease is large. Throttling across a valve is isenthalpic: no work leaves, and a gas cools only by the Joule–Thomson effect, the temperature change at constant enthalpy, which for air below its inversion temperature of approximately 620 K is a cooling of the order of 0.2 K per bar of pressure drop [40]. A valve in place of the turbine, the arrangement of the earliest Linde plants, produces a fraction of the refrigeration per unit of flow and recovers no work. A gas stream, where the recoverable work is large, goes to the turbine, and liquid streams, where the volume change and the recoverable work are small, go to the valves.

Expanded air is not wasted: it enters the low-pressure column as a feed stream. The front-end sequence of compression, direct-contact cooling, adsorption and main heat exchange follows the standard Linde plant description [23]. Remaining air (stream 7, 800 kmol/h) enters the pressure column directly.

## Pressure column (T-101)

The pressure column (also called the high-pressure column) performs a partial rectification at approximately 560 kPa. Rising vapour becomes richer in nitrogen (the more volatile component); descending liquid becomes richer in oxygen. Reflux and boilup (the vapour generated at a column bottom) supply the two phases of every contact in every column of the plant. At each contact, separation is set by the volatility ratio, denoted alpha, the ratio of the vapour pressures of two components at the column temperature: for nitrogen against oxygen at 90 K, alpha is approximately 3.6 [40], so each contact enriches the vapour in nitrogen by a substantial factor; for argon against oxygen at the same temperature, alpha is approximately 1.34 [40], so each contact enriches the vapour in argon only slightly, which is the quantitative reason the crude argon column needs many stages and a high reflux ratio.

Two streams leave the column: an overhead vapour of approximately pure nitrogen (stream 10, 97.0 mol percent nitrogen in the sample basis, with oxygen at 2.0 mol percent and argon at approximately 1.0 mol percent, near its feed content) and a bottom liquid, the rich liquid, enriched to approximately 40 mol percent oxygen (stream 16). No reboiler of its own, in the usual sense, belongs to the pressure column: entering air vapour provides the boilup, and its condenser is the shared main condenser/reboiler.

## Main condenser/reboiler (E-103)

The main condenser/reboiler is the thermal link that defines the double-column process. It sits between the two columns: pressure-column overhead nitrogen condenses inside it (stream 10 to stream 11), and the heat released vaporises liquid oxygen in the sump of the low-pressure column, the pool of liquid collected at its base. The pressure difference between the columns makes the arrangement possible: the condensation temperature of nitrogen at the higher pressure rises above the boiling temperature of oxygen at the lower pressure. At equal pressures the heat could not flow at all: at atmospheric pressure nitrogen condenses at 77.4 K while oxygen boils at 90.2 K, so the condensing side would be 12.8 K colder than the boiling side and heat would flow in the wrong direction. Raising the nitrogen pressure reverses the difference. The Clausius–Clapeyron estimate of the Theory section gives a nitrogen saturation temperature of 96.4 K at 560 kPa and an oxygen saturation temperature of 92.7 K at 130 kPa, a pure-component difference of 3.7 K in the favourable direction; equation-of-state values are 95.5 K and 92.6 K [40]. The exchanger operates with a temperature difference of only 1 to 2 kelvin between the condensing and boiling sides [26], smaller than either pure-component difference because the condensing stream is not pure nitrogen and the boiling sump is not pure oxygen. The pressure stage exists to create that difference: at one pressure for both columns, the double column would need an external condenser colder than 90 K and an external reboiler, which is a second refrigerator. Condensed liquid nitrogen (stream 11, 400 kmol/h) is divided: one part returns to the pressure column as reflux (stream 12, 200 kmol/h) and the other part is sent, after subcooling, to the top of the low-pressure column as its reflux (streams 13 to 15). No external heating or cooling medium is involved in the main separation duty.

## Subcoolers (E-104, E-106) and throttling valves (VLV-101, VLV-102)

Subcoolers cool the two liquid streams leaving the pressure-column system (liquid nitrogen, stream 13 outlet temperature minus 186 degrees Celsius; rich liquid, stream 17) against cold waste nitrogen before throttling. Subcooling suppresses flash gas formation when the streams are throttled to the low-pressure column pressure in the throttling valves (streams 15 and 18): liquid that flashes at the valve would pass through the column as vapour without contributing reflux or feed liquid at the intended entry point. Throttling is an isenthalpic expansion across a valve. For a liquid stream the temperature decrease is not a Joule–Thomson effect of the liquid, whose coefficient is small: the liquid reaches the saturation temperature at the lower pressure by flash evaporation, in which a fraction of the liquid vaporises and the latent heat of that fraction cools the remainder. An enthalpy balance at the valve gives the flash fraction: the fraction equals the excess of the inlet enthalpy over the saturated-liquid enthalpy at the outlet pressure, divided by the latent heat at the outlet pressure. Subcooling reduces the inlet enthalpy, and the flash fraction falls in direct proportion, which is the quantitative statement of the purpose of the subcoolers.

## Low-pressure column (T-102)

The low-pressure column completes the separation at approximately 120 to 140 kPa. It receives four feeds: subcooled liquid nitrogen at the top (stream 15, the column reflux), throttled rich liquid at an intermediate point (stream 18), expanded air at a lower intermediate point (stream 9) and the returning liquid from the crude argon column (stream 30). Three products leave: gaseous nitrogen of 99.5 mol percent purity at the top (stream 21, 500 kmol/h), waste nitrogen drawn a few stages below the top (stream 20, 290 kmol/h, carrying most of the argon not recovered and the bulk of the remaining oxygen loss) and gaseous oxygen of 99.0 mol percent purity from the sump (stream 19, 200 kmol/h), vaporised in the main condenser/reboiler. The argon content of the column vapour rises to a maximum (the argon belly, of the order of 8 to 10 mol percent argon in the vapour) at an intermediate height. A maximum exists because argon is intermediate in volatility: against oxygen it is the more volatile component and is carried upward, and against nitrogen it is the less volatile component and is returned downward, so argon accumulates where the two tendencies balance. Without a draw at the maximum, argon would leave with the oxygen and nitrogen products and limit the purity of both. The side draw to the crude argon column is taken at the maximum (stream 29, 100 kmol/h at 9.0 mol percent argon).

## Crude argon column (T-103) and condenser (E-105)

The crude argon column rectifies the argon-enriched side draw against its own bottom product. Because argon (boiling point 87.3 K) is less volatile than oxygen, the column separates oxygen downward: the bottom liquid, nearly pure oxygen, returns to the low-pressure column (stream 30, 90 kmol/h), and the overhead vapour, enriched to approximately 90 mol percent argon, is condensed in the crude argon condenser (E-105), which is cooled by a part of the rich liquid (drawn on the diagram as a short line that ends without a terminal symbol, because the rich liquid is not consumed in the condenser: it continues to the low-pressure column after absorbing the heat of condensation and warming in the exchange). The condensate divides into reflux (stream 27, 20 kmol/h) and the crude argon product (stream 28, 10 kmol/h). For its size, the crude argon column is the most stage-intensive column of the plant: 40 to 50 equilibrium stages are typical for crude product, at a reflux ratio of the order of 30, because the argon-oxygen separation is the closest-boiling in the process [27]. Commercial crude argon generally contains 95 to 98 mol percent argon [27]; the 90 mol percent of the sample basis of the diagram is at the low end of practice and is retained on the drawing as a conservative sample value. Crude argon still contains nitrogen and oxygen; purification to commercial pure argon (removal of oxygen by reaction with hydrogen over a catalyst, then drying and removal of nitrogen in a further column) is a separate downstream process outside the boundary of this unit, and is the only catalytic step associated with argon production. Within the boundary of this unit there is no catalyst and no reaction.

## Products and battery limits

'Battery limits' is the plant term for the boundary of the unit: the streams of the diagram cross it at the states stated there, and everything outside it — the argon refining train, for example — is not part of this unit. Gaseous products leave through the main heat exchanger at ambient temperature: gaseous nitrogen at 110 kPa (stream 24), gaseous oxygen at 115 kPa (stream 22) and waste nitrogen at 105 kPa (stream 23), of which a part is used, after heating, to regenerate the purification adsorbent before discharge. Plants that deliver products at higher pressure either compress the gaseous product or use internal compression (pumping the liquid product and vaporising it in the main heat exchanger), a variant discussed in the Economics section. Liquid products (liquid oxygen and liquid nitrogen drawn from the columns for storage and for merchant sale, sale as liquid to customers supplied by tanker rather than by pipeline) are outside the sample basis of this unit, which is an all-gaseous plant with a crude argon draw.
