## Production scale

Cryogenic air separation covers a wider capacity range than any other industrial gas process. Linde's packaged (standardised) plants reach approximately 850 tonnes per day (t/d) of oxygen or 2000 t/d of nitrogen per train in one current product description [1]; engineered (customised) plants from the same supplier span 1000 to 5500 t/d of oxygen per train [2]. Largest single trains in service are of the order of 5000 t/d: five units at Jamnagar, India are each designed for 5250 t/d of oxygen [2]. Multi-train complexes exceed any single train by a wide margin; the eight units at Ras Laffan, Qatar produce 30,000 t/d of oxygen in total [2]. At the small end, merchant plants (plants that liquefy gas at a central plant and deliver liquid by tanker to customers in a region) of approximately 250 t/d serve regional liquid markets [3], and below approximately 500 t/d the capital cost and energy penalty of cryogenic separation become uncompetitive against pressure swing adsorption (a cyclic adsorption separation in which the more strongly adsorbed component of a gas mixture is taken up on a bed at pressure and released when the bed pressure is reduced) for gaseous oxygen, according to a United States Department of Energy assessment [4].

World and United States production totals for elemental oxygen and nitrogen gas are not published as an authoritative statistical series. Secondary sources quote approximately 100 to 150 million tonnes per year of oxygen worldwide, but the two figures are mutually inconsistent and neither comes from a statistical agency; the page reports no world total [5]. United States Geological Survey nitrogen series is not a substitute: it measures fixed nitrogen as ammonia, not air separation nitrogen gas [6]. The industry is supplied by a small number of producers, principally Linde, Air Liquide, Air Products and Chemicals, Taiyo Nippon Sanso (Nippon Sanso Holdings), Messer and Yingde Gases; commercial estimates of revenue market share differ between firms (Linde approximately 22 to 31 percent) and are not verified against filings [7].

## Energy consumption

Electricity is the dominant operating cost of an air separation unit (ASU), and the specific power consumption is the controlling economic variable. Reported values for cryogenic production cluster between approximately 200 and 260 kilowatt-hours per tonne of oxygen (kWh/t) for gaseous product at 95 to 99.5 mol percent purity, against a thermodynamic minimum of approximately 51 to 53 kWh/t [8][9]. A National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) low-pressure design at 99.5 mol percent purity reports 204.5 kWh/t [10]; an oxy-combustion study reports 200 kWh/t at 95 mol percent purity rising to 240 kWh/t at 99.5 mol percent, which is the cleanest published statement of the purity penalty [9]. Vendor figures for internal-compression oxygen generators of 1000 to 15,000 normal cubic metres per hour (Nm3/h) at 90 to 98 mol percent purity are 0.40 to 0.55 kWh/Nm3 [11], which is consistent with the per-tonne range at those purities. The model of this page gives 250 kWh/t at its default (conservative) settings, within the reported range.

Plant size sets the scale of the energy term. A train of 5000 t/d at a specific power of 250 kWh/t draws approximately 52 megawatts (MW) of electric power continuously; a complex of 30,000 t/d draws approximately 313 MW. No commercial plant approaches the thermodynamic minimum for its product slate, and the reported range of 200 to 260 kWh/t corresponds to second-law efficiencies of approximately 0.20 to 0.26 on the basis of the Theory section.

The electricity share of cost is reported between 60 and 70 percent of total production cost in commercial market research [12], between 65 and 75 percent of life-cycle operating expenses in a vendor technical guide [13], and up to 70 to 80 percent of operating cost in a regional market report [14]. Sources differ in scope and in the denominator of the share, and the model of this page gives a different value on a stated denominator: at the model defaults, electricity is 57 percent of the operating cost excluding the capital charge, and 28 percent of the unit cost including the capital charge (worked example in the Capital cost section). A change in electricity price moves the unit cost in the proportion of the electricity term: at the model defaults a 10 percent change in electricity price changes the unit cost by approximately 3 percent, which the App tab demonstrates through its electricity price input.

## Capital cost

No peer-reviewed capital cost correlation and no air separation specific cost-scaling exponent were found in the sources verified for this page; the 0.70 exponent used in the App model is a stated modelling assumption, taken from the general chemical-plant capacity exponent range of 0.6 to 0.7 [37][38] and anchored to published project points, not a sourced air-separation correlation. Verified project points are: a Linde investment of approximately 100 million euros in a unit of approximately 72,000 Nm3/h of gaseous oxygen for the Lipetsk steel site in Russia, announced in 2018 [15]; and an investment of approximately 130 million euros for two plants of 55,000 Nm3/h of oxygen each at Yantai, China, announced in 2011 [16]. Normalised to capacity, the two points are approximately 1389 euros per Nm3/h (Lipetsk, 2018) and approximately 1182 euros per Nm3/h (Yantai, 2011, two plants); the points are announcements, not final costs, their scopes are not stated in the announcements, and the earlier point is not escalated to 2018 in this comparison. The model converts the Lipetsk point at the 2018 average exchange rate of 1.18 USD per euro [39], giving a reference capital cost of 118 million United States dollars (USD) in 2018 dollars at a reference capacity of 2470 t/d (Theory section). All capital costs and unit costs of the model are in 2018 dollars; escalation to a later year multiplies by the ratio of the Chemical Engineering Plant Cost Index of that year to the index of 2018 [37], and no escalation is applied in the model. A commercial market report places large cryogenic facilities in the range of 100 to 500 million USD depending on capacity and configuration [12]. An itemised operating cost breakdown beyond the electricity share was not found in authoritative sources; a commercial report gives maintenance at 2 to 4 percent of capital cost per year [17], which is the basis of the 0.04 non-electricity operating fraction used, as an assumption covering maintenance, labour and consumables together, in the model.

Worked example (model defaults, 2018 dollars). The contained oxygen capacity is 151.8 t/d and the scaled capital cost is 16.7 million USD. The unit cost of 61.8 USD/t consists of a capital charge of 31.0 USD/t (50 percent), an electricity term of 17.5 USD/t (28 percent) and a non-electricity operating term of 13.2 USD/t (21 percent); the full derivation is in the Theory section. Unit cost is gross: all cost is allocated to the oxygen product, with no credit for the nitrogen products (303 t/d of gas and 34 t/d of liquid at the defaults) or the crude argon product. The analyst base case of approximately 100 USD per tonne for oxygen [20] exceeds the model value at every scale the sweep covers (61.8 USD/t at 151.8 t/d, 36.7 USD/t at the reference capacity of 2470 t/d, both recomputed at the corrected reference cost); the analyst figure's scope, year and co-product treatment are not stated in its source, and the two values are not reconciled beyond that statement. At the model defaults the plant sits below the approximately 500 t/d threshold at which cryogenic separation becomes uncompetitive against pressure swing adsorption for gaseous oxygen [4]: the model value at that scale is a cost estimate for a configuration a buyer would not normally choose, and secondary figures for pressure swing adsorption at 10 to 50 t/d are of the order of 200 USD/t at 300 to 500 kWh/t (research record of this page).

## Business models

Large plants are normally built on site at the consumer's fence line under sale-of-gas contracts. Air Products' 2022 annual report describes on-site supply under contracts of typically 10 to 15 years containing fixed monthly charges and/or minimum purchase requirements with price escalation provisions, while merchant supply (liquid delivered by tanker, and packaged gas) has no minimum purchase requirement and contracts of generally five years or less [18]. In plain terms, the sale-of-gas model is a utility arrangement: the gas producer builds, owns and operates a plant at the customer's fence line, the boundary of the customer's site, and sells gas by the tonne under a long contract. The merchant model is delivery: the producer liquefies gas at a central plant and delivers liquid by tanker, or supplies packaged gas, under short contracts. The fixed monthly charge of a sale-of-gas contract is the mechanism by which the producer recovers the capital charge, which is 50 percent of the unit cost at the model defaults, whether or not the customer takes gas; the minimum purchase requirement and the price escalation provision allocate the energy risk between producer and customer. The sale-of-gas model transfers the capital and energy risk to the gas producer and prices the gas as a utility; the merchant model monetises liquid production, for which argon is a significant credit. The argon credit is not quantified in the model: at the model defaults the crude argon rate is 8.70 t/d, and a calculation at the refined-argon spot price of 675 USD per tonne [19] would give approximately 39 USD per tonne of oxygen, which overstates the credit because the product of this unit is crude argon at 90 mol percent, not refined argon, because refining and liquefaction costs are excluded, and because the argon market is small against the oxygen rate of a large plant. Argon prices retrieved for 2026 are of the order of 675 USD per tonne in China and 725 to 763 USD per tonne in India, with sharp regional spot movements, against an analyst base-case production cost of approximately 100 USD per tonne for oxygen and 20 USD per tonne for nitrogen (an analyst's cost estimate, not a verified market price) [19][20].

## Process trade-offs

Product pressure is obtained either by compressing the gaseous product or by internal compression: pumping the liquid product from the low-pressure column and vaporising it in the main heat exchanger, which removes the oxygen compressor (a machine with demanding safety requirements, Safety section) at the cost of a higher air-side pressure [11]. Purity has the energy penalty quantified in the energy section: specifying 99.5 mol percent oxygen for a consumer that accepts 95 mol percent raises the specific power by approximately one fifth in the cited study [9]. This page's model does not compute the penalty (Theory section): the purity input changes the product flow rates of the balance and leaves the specific power unchanged, so the App tab understates the cost of a purity increase by the amount of the cited penalty. Pumping a liquid product in the internal-compression variant costs little work against compressing the same product as a gas, because a liquid is nearly incompressible and its volume, against which the pump works, is smaller than the gas volume by a factor of the order of the vaporisation expansion ratios of the Safety section. Liquid production raises the refrigeration load and the specific power per unit of gaseous product, but provides storage, supply backup and access to the merchant market, and allows production to be shifted toward periods of low electricity price; a Linde modular plant in Germany is operated explicitly in that load-shifting mode [21]. Argon recovery requires the additional side column and condenser shown on the diagram and constrains the operation of the low-pressure column, but at large scale the argon credit is a material offset to the oxygen cost; argon supply is rigidly coupled to air separation operating rates, which is the structural cause of its price volatility [19].
