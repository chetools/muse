## Hazard inventory

The air separation unit (ASU) combines four hazard families that are each manageable and jointly demanding: an oxidising inventory (liquid and gaseous oxygen), a cryogenic inventory (liquefied gases at temperatures down to approximately 90 kelvin (K)), a large rotating-machine energy input, and an asphyxiant inventory (nitrogen, which is odourless and gives no physiological warning). The issues of special concern below are the ones that have shaped the design rules of the industry bodies for industrial gases, principally the European Industrial Gases Association (EIGA) and the Compressed Gas Association (CGA).

## Hydrocarbon accumulation in liquid oxygen

The hazard specific to this process is the accumulation of hydrocarbons in the liquid oxygen of the main condenser/reboiler (E-103 of drawing PFD-102). Air contains trace hydrocarbons, of which acetylene is the most dangerous because its solubility in liquid oxygen is very low (of the order of a few parts per million by mole): acetylene that enters with the feed air and escapes the purification unit is concentrated by evaporation in the condenser/reboiler sump, where the liquid oxygen is continuously boiled. Beyond its solubility limit it precipitates as a solid, and solid acetylene (and heavy hydrocarbon deposits generally) in contact with liquid oxygen can detonate under mechanical shock or local energy input. Total hydrocarbon accumulation is the companion mechanism: even soluble hydrocarbons concentrate as the liquid boils, so the condenser/reboiler is operated with a continuous liquid oxygen purge (a small blowdown stream that carries accumulated hydrocarbons out of the sump), the feed air hydrocarbon content is controlled at the purification unit, and the liquid oxygen in the sump is analysed for total hydrocarbons and acetylene against alarm and shutdown limits set by industry guidance. Explosions in air separation plants attributed to hydrocarbon accumulation in liquid oxygen are a documented historical cause of cold box losses, and the control set (purification, purge, analysis, limits) exists because of that record.

## Freeze-out of nitrous oxide, carbon dioxide and water

Nitrous oxide (N2O), emitted by combustion and agricultural sources and present in air at sub-part-per-million levels, behaves like the hydrocarbons in one respect: it is only partly removed by the purification unit, it has limited solubility in liquid oxygen, and it concentrates in the cold section, where it can also deposit as a solid in heat exchanger passages. Carbon dioxide and water that break through a degraded adsorbent bed freeze in the main heat exchanger (E-102), raising pressure drop and, in the limit, forcing a plant shutdown and warm-up (deriming). The operational controls are adsorbent condition monitoring, breakthrough alarms on carbon dioxide analysers at the purification outlet, and exchanger pressure-drop trending.

## Oxygen enrichment and materials compatibility

Oxygen is not flammable, but it makes other materials flammable and lowers ignition energies by orders of magnitude. Hydrocarbon contamination (oil, grease, thread sealants) in oxygen service is a classical ignition source, and oxygen systems are therefore cleaned for oxygen service, assembled without hydrocarbon lubricants, and operated with velocity limits in piping to limit ignition by particle impact and adiabatic compression. Materials for oxygen service are selected for ignition resistance; aluminium, widely used in the cold box plate-fin exchangers, is acceptable only within its qualified conditions. Oxygen enrichment of the local atmosphere (from leaks or vents) turns clothing and ordinary materials into fire hazards at concentrations well below those reached near a vent, and oxygen deficiency from nitrogen release is the mirror hazard: a nitrogen leak in an enclosed space or a cold box entry can reduce the oxygen content below the level that sustains consciousness without any warning sensation. Cold box entry and work in trenches and pits (where cold nitrogen accumulates) are therefore controlled by permit, atmosphere monitoring and rescue arrangements.

## Cryogenic and pressure hazards

Contact with liquefied gases or uninsulated cold equipment causes cryogenic burns, and carbon steels lose ductility at cold-section temperatures (ductile-to-brittle transition), so cold-service equipment uses aluminium or austenitic stainless steels and the cold box design limits the spread of a liquid spill. The pressure hazard specific to cryogenic plant is blocked-in liquid: a volume of liquefied gas trapped between closed valves warms toward ambient temperature and generates pressures far beyond piping ratings, so every liquid-containing section that can be isolated is provided with thermal relief protection. Vacuum-jacketed and perlite-insulated enclosures add the hazard of insulation-space pressure rise on a large internal leak, which the cold box relief system must accommodate.

## Rotating equipment and operational issues

The main air compressor is the largest single energy and noise source and a major fire initiator if its lubrication system fails in the presence of an oxygen-enriched stream downstream; machine protection (vibration, surge control, bearing temperature) is a safety system, not only an availability system. Molecular sieve vessels cycle thermally and mechanically: adsorbent attrition (dusting) carries zeolite fines into the cold section if the bed support or the cycling valves degrade, and a regeneration heater fault can pass heat into the cold box. The crude argon column operates with a small temperature driving force and a composition profile that is sensitive to the low-pressure column operation; purity excursions in the argon system are an availability issue rather than a safety issue, except that oxygen-rich liquid returned to the low-pressure column must remain within the column's stable operating range. Energy intensity is also an environmental issue of special concern: at approximately 200 to 260 kilowatt-hours of electricity per tonne of oxygen, the indirect carbon dioxide emissions of a large unit are set almost entirely by the carbon intensity of its electricity supply.

## Codes and industry guidance

Design and operation follow the industrial gas industry's own guidance families (EIGA documents for air separation, oxygen systems and hydrocarbon limits; CGA publications for compressed gas handling and oxygen service), pressure equipment codes of the jurisdiction (the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code in the United States), and site rules for confined space entry and oxygen-deficiency monitoring. The applicable document numbers and current editions are project-specific and are verified at design stage; this section names the families, not editions.
