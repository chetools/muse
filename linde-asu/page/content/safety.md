## Hazard inventory

The air separation unit (ASU) combines four hazard families that are each manageable and jointly demanding: an oxidising inventory (liquid and gaseous oxygen), a cryogenic inventory (liquefied gases at temperatures down to approximately 90 kelvin (K)), a large rotating-machine energy input, and an asphyxiant inventory (nitrogen, which is odourless and gives no physiological warning). The issues of special concern below are the ones that have shaped the design rules of the industry bodies for industrial gases, principally the European Industrial Gases Association (EIGA) and the Compressed Gas Association (CGA).

## Hydrocarbon accumulation in liquid oxygen

The hazard specific to this process is the accumulation of hydrocarbons in the liquid oxygen of the main condenser/reboiler (E-103 of drawing PFD-102). Air contains trace hydrocarbons, of which acetylene is the most dangerous because its solubility in liquid oxygen is very low (of the order of a few parts per million by mole): acetylene that enters with the feed air and escapes the purification unit is concentrated by evaporation in the condenser/reboiler sump, where the liquid oxygen is continuously boiled. Beyond its solubility limit it precipitates as a solid, and solid acetylene (and heavy hydrocarbon deposits generally) in contact with liquid oxygen can detonate under mechanical shock or local energy input. Total hydrocarbon accumulation is the companion mechanism: even soluble hydrocarbons concentrate as the liquid boils. The concentration factor for a plant whose only liquid draw is the purge stream (typically 0.2 percent of the air) can reach approximately 500, against approximately 5 for an internal-compression plant drawing approximately 20 percent of the air as liquid product [28]. The control set is specified in the industry guidance for reboilers and condensers [28]: maximum allowable concentrations in the liquid oxygen of a downflow reboiler at 1.2 bar absolute, in ppm by volume, of acetylene 0.5, total hydrocarbons (as methane) 500, ethylene 15, propylene 1, carbon dioxide 0.5 and nitrous oxide 5; a continuous liquid oxygen purge of 0.1 to 0.2 percent of the incoming air (or an equivalent purge at least every 8 hours); continuous analysis of the sump liquid for total hydrocarbons, with the carbon dioxide analyser at the purification outlet as the breakthrough marker; and maintenance of sump level and reboiler submergence so that no passage boils dry. Two documented explosions define the hazard. At Fushun, China, on 16 May 1997, an explosion in the column of a 200 tonnes per day (t/d) oxygen plant killed 4 employees and injured 31; the recorded cause was an exceptional hydrocarbon pollution peak from a neighbouring plant venting, together with a temporarily low liquid level in the main vaporiser, with the aluminium column internals burning in liquid oxygen as the fuel [29]. At Bintulu, Malaysia, on 25 December 1997, an explosion destroyed the major elements of a 2500 t/d oxygen plant and injured 12 people; the investigation found hydrocarbon ingress as carbonaceous aerosol from forest fires, with nitrous oxide crystal fouling of the vaporiser creating the deposits in which hydrocarbons accumulated, and aluminium combustion driving the rupture [30].

## Freeze-out of nitrous oxide, carbon dioxide and water

Nitrous oxide (N2O), emitted by combustion and agricultural sources and present in air at sub-part-per-million levels, behaves like the hydrocarbons in one respect: it is only partly removed by the purification unit, it has limited solubility in liquid oxygen, and it concentrates in the cold section, where it can also deposit as a solid in heat exchanger passages; its maximum allowable concentration in reboiler liquid oxygen is 5 ppm by volume, and that of carbon dioxide is 0.5 ppm by volume [28]. Nitrous oxide fouling was a contributory cause at Bintulu [30]. Carbon dioxide and water that break through a degraded adsorbent bed freeze in the main heat exchanger (E-102), raising pressure drop and, in the limit, forcing a plant shutdown and warm-up (deriming). The operational controls are adsorbent condition monitoring, breakthrough alarms on carbon dioxide analysers at the purification outlet, and exchanger pressure-drop trending.

## Oxygen enrichment and materials compatibility

Oxygen is not flammable, but it makes other materials flammable and lowers ignition energies by orders of magnitude. Hydrocarbon contamination (oil, grease, thread sealants) in oxygen service is a classical ignition source, and oxygen systems are therefore cleaned for oxygen service, assembled without hydrocarbon lubricants, and operated with velocity limits in piping to limit ignition by particle impact and adiabatic compression. Materials for oxygen service are selected for ignition resistance; aluminium, widely used in the cold box plate-fin exchangers, is acceptable only within its qualified conditions. Oxygen enrichment of the local atmosphere (from leaks or vents) turns clothing and ordinary materials into fire hazards at concentrations well below those reached near a vent [31], and oxygen deficiency from nitrogen release is the mirror hazard: a nitrogen leak in an enclosed space or a cold box entry can reduce the oxygen content below the level that sustains consciousness without any warning sensation. The confined-space entry window is 19.5 percent oxygen by volume minimum and 23.5 percent maximum [32]; below 19.5 percent, breathing rate and heartbeat rise and attention and coordination are impaired, and below approximately 6 percent convulsions and cessation of breathing can occur immediately [33]. Cold box entry and work in trenches and pits (where cold nitrogen and argon accumulate) are therefore controlled by permit, atmosphere monitoring and rescue arrangements. A leak of process gas into the cold box insulation space is itself an issue of special concern: perlite lodged inside process equipment can create pockets where oxygen and hydrocarbons collect together, and a cold box leak that enriches the insulation is a shutdown criterion rather than a monitor-and-run condition. At Yima, China, on 19 July 2019, an explosion in one unit of a gasification plant air separation installation killed 15 people; the reported sequence (secondary reporting of the official investigation, partially verified) is continued operation after a cold box leak had been detected and a crack had appeared in the cold box surface [34].

## Cryogenic and pressure hazards

Contact with liquefied gases or uninsulated cold equipment causes cryogenic burns, and carbon steels lose ductility at cold-section temperatures (ductile-to-brittle transition), so cold-service equipment uses aluminium or austenitic stainless steels and the cold box design limits the spread of a liquid spill. The pressure hazard specific to cryogenic plant is blocked-in liquid: a volume of liquefied gas trapped between closed valves warms toward ambient temperature and generates pressures far beyond piping ratings. The expansion ratios on vaporisation are 696 for nitrogen, 847 for argon and 862 for oxygen [35], so every liquid-containing section that can be isolated is provided with thermal relief protection, with relief inlets and outlets protected against ice and solid carbon dioxide blockage, which can defeat the device. Vacuum-jacketed and perlite-insulated enclosures add the hazard of insulation-space pressure rise on a large internal leak, which the cold box relief system must accommodate.

## Rotating equipment and operational issues

The main air compressor is the largest single energy and noise source and a major fire initiator if its lubrication system fails in the presence of an oxygen-enriched stream downstream; machine protection (vibration, surge control, bearing temperature) is a safety system, not only an availability system. Molecular sieve vessels cycle thermally and mechanically: the adsorbent particles (typically 1 to 5 millimetres) are prone to breakdown and dusting if the unit is incorrectly operated, so an after-filter is required to keep the dust out of the cryogenic equipment, and liquid-water carryover into a bed can damage the adsorbent and drive bed temperatures above 100 degrees Celsius [36]. The crude argon column operates with a small temperature driving force and a composition profile that is sensitive to the low-pressure column operation; purity excursions in the argon system are an availability issue rather than a safety issue, except that oxygen-rich liquid returned to the low-pressure column must remain within the column's stable operating range. Energy intensity is also an environmental issue of special concern: at approximately 200 to 260 kilowatt-hours of electricity per tonne of oxygen, the indirect carbon dioxide emissions of a large unit are set almost entirely by the carbon intensity of its electricity supply.

## Codes and industry guidance

Design and operation follow named industry documents, each verified for this page: the Safe Practices Guide for Cryogenic Air Separation Plants (EIGA Doc 147, harmonised with the Compressed Gas Association (CGA) P-8 family) [36]; Safe Operation of Reboilers/Condensers in Air Separation Units (EIGA Doc 65) for hydrocarbon, nitrous oxide and carbon dioxide limits and purge practice [28]; Oxygen Pipeline and Piping Systems (EIGA Doc 13 / CGA G-4.4) for oxygen piping materials and velocity limits; Cleaning of Equipment for Oxygen Service (EIGA Doc 33 / CGA G-4.1); Fire Hazards of Oxygen and Oxygen Enriched Atmospheres (EIGA Doc 04) [31]; Hazards of Oxygen-Deficient Atmospheres (EIGA Doc 44) [33]; Safe Location of Oxygen, Nitrogen and Argon Vents (EIGA Doc 154) [32]; and Bulk Liquid Oxygen, Nitrogen and Argon Storage Systems at Production Sites (EIGA Doc 127) for storage, oil exclusion and cryogenic materials (austenitic stainless steels, 9 percent nickel steel, copper alloys and aluminium). The aluminium-specific documents CGA G-4.8 (aluminium structured packing) and CGA G-4.9 (brazed aluminium heat exchangers) were written after the Fushun and Bintulu events. General codes are the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section VIII for vessels, ASME B31.3 for process piping, the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) recommended practice 53 for oxygen-enriched atmospheres, and the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) G04 family for materials compatibility in oxygen service. Applicable editions are confirmed at design stage.
