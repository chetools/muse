# Textbook style [reference — load for instructional or detailed explanatory, step-by-step text]

This section does not supersede the sections above. It applies when writing instructional or detailed explanatory, step-by-step phrases, sections, and sentences — a derivation, a definition, an assumption list, a caveat, a worked calculation. The STEM and business sections above govern the bigger picture: a document's structure, its claims, its openings and closings. Where a step-by-step explanatory sentence's textbook form differs from a rule above, the textbook form governs that sentence.
Activation: this section is in force whenever a document is written under this skill, and it applies sentence by sentence. A sentence takes the textbook forms when its job is to let the reader reproduce or verify a step: it states a definition, a derivation step, an assumption, a procedure step, a caveat, or a worked calculation. A sentence whose job is to state what was done, found, or decided takes the STEM or business rules above. Where one sentence would do both — a result sentence that also derives its value — the derivation is moved into its own sentence in the textbook forms and the result sentence keeps the frame style. A closing sentence that rests a claim or a decision on grounds stated before it is a frame sentence: its inference is not marked with "thus", "therefore", "hence", "accordingly", or a causal "so" (trigger test, 2026-11-01: all three frame-side errors in eight test documents were inference markers of this kind in closings). Completion check for any passage that contains detail sentences: every derivation step is marked by a connective or opened by its source; operation verbs are chosen by route; assumptions stand before the first equation that uses them; every evaluative word carries its ground in its own sentence or the one before; no frame sentence carries an imperative, a step connective, or a where roster.



## Step by step

1. **Name the object first.** A detail sentence carries its object's full name — the law, equation, quantity, or step it is about — even mid-document.
2. **Fix every name at first use.** Copula forms: "is defined as", "is called", "is known as"; a name the field already uses takes "is referred to as", with its source or reason in the same sentence where one is given. A mathematical symbol, as against a term, is fixed by "denotes" or "is denoted by". Chemical species notation takes no such fixing for a chemical-engineering audience: an element symbol (N, H) and the formula of a common species are read directly, appear in no "denotes" clause, and take no entry in a where roster. After a displayed equation, the "where" roster is the default definition site for the equation's mathematical symbols.
3. **Mark each derivation step at the step.** "Thus" where the premises stand in the immediately preceding sentences; "therefore" is a routine variant in two books; "hence" is a secondary tier. A because-clause attaches the ground to the step, result, or comparison it supports.
4. **Open a step with its source** where the source is not the preceding line: "From (11-6), …"; "By an overall material balance, …". A criterion got by setting two relations side by side is stated by the comparison: "Comparison of Eqs. (x) and (y) shows that…".
5. **Narrate a transformation with the equation as subject:** "becomes" (substitution, limiting case), "reduces to" (special case under a stated condition), "simplifies to", "can be written", "rearranges to", "can be replaced with".
6. **Choose the operation verb by the route to the result:** determine (balance, criterion, specification), calculate (numerical evaluation, instrument named), obtain from (data source, table, earlier result), find from (relation or data set), estimate (judgement). Equations and procedures take "gives/yields/shows"; methods and designs take "provides". Names of objects keep one form; these verbs are exempt from that rule, and the same verb repeats for the same route.
7. **In a worked example:** imperative or question task carrying every datum; imperative at each choice point ("Assume a pressure of 74 psia"; "Take 1 g … as a basis"); assumptions before the first equation that uses them — task-side "Assume that…", solution-side "is assumed" with value or grade attached, or a numbered list with the model or equation as the subject of "assume". Where an expected limiting behaviour selects the law, state the expectation first and check it at the close.
8. **Close the example with a check:** benchmark the result against experiment, an estimate, a predecessor, or a rival method, with the difference stated; or name the idealizations with the direction of error each causes. A "Thus" sentence converts the number into the statement the example exists to make.
9. **State change in the matching frame.** Covariation: "As X increases, Y increases, but Z decreases", one change verb per variable, closed by a trade-off sentence where the pair sets one. Imposed step: "changes from X to Y". Range: "varies from X to Y". A named producer takes the transitive frame (a section, a stream, a salt increases or reduces a quantity). "Fall" is not used of a quantity; "drop" is a noun.
10. **Omit by naming.** The omitted effect is named, with its ground: "can be neglected because…", or, most often in running prose, the adjective — the effect is "negligible". An approximation's adequacy is fitness for a named purpose or a number, never a bare grade.
11. **Grade only with the ground attached.** "Important", "key", "significant", "acceptable", "reasonable" stand where what the thing limits or decides, a comparison, a practice statement, or the justifying number stands in the same sentence or the one before. A general claim carries "usually" or "typically" in its own sentence, attached to its number, range, or configuration where it has one, and inside a definition at the point of definition.
12. **Flag what the reader can get wrong, at the point of risk.** "Note that" (Seborg variant "Notice that") heads a sign convention, a validity caveat, or a hidden assumption, with the substance in the same sentence.
13. **Close a chapter or section with its fixed summary frame:** a restatement of the unit's content — Seborg's past-tense inventory with a forward pointer, Seader's numbered present-tense result items, Smith–Van Ness's frame with bare infinitive objectives — with no new derivation, no new claim, and no task imperatives.


## What to look out for

- A connective with no premise behind it: "thus" earns its place only from the sentences just before.
- "Note that" with nothing checkable after it; the books' form always carries a condition, a sign, or a consequence.
- Synonym discipline applied to the wrong words: one name per object, free choice among the operation verbs by route.
- Change verbs that invent a cause where no producer is named, and a covariation left without its closing trade-off where the pair sets one.
- Field terms mistaken for grading words in a mechanical scan: "key component" and "pressure drop" are terminology, not evaluations.
- "Scope" and "carry" in limit and inclusion statements: validity takes the law as subject ("applies only at low pressures"), omission names the item.
- A "denotes" clause spent on chemical species notation ("where N denotes nitrogen"): element symbols and common species formulae are the chemical-engineering audience's working notation and take no definition.
- Splits the corpus does not settle: "compared with" against "compared to" — one form per document, held; the reference-frame phrase ("with respect to" against "in terms of") splits the same way; solution tense is past for a simulator run narrated as an event, present elsewhere.


## Validity and omission forms

23. A validity statement names the law, equation, relation, model, or criterion as its subject: "applies to" or "applies only to", "is valid for" or "valid only for", "holds for" or "does not hold for", "is limited to". The condition stands in the same sentence, and the case outside is stated as what changes. A result, a value, or a reported figure is not the subject of "holds": a result or figure that is bounded takes "is valid at" a point or "is valid for" a range.
24. An omission statement names the omitted term or effect: the omitted item as subject of "can be neglected" or "is neglected", an author subject with "neglect" or "omit", or the item "is not included in" a named equation, formula, or model, or "no [named] term is included". The reason for the omission stands in the same sentence or the next. An omission is never stated with "carry": an equation does not carry, or fail to carry, a term.
25. A closing restatement of a limit restates the conditions themselves, or names the limited thing as subject of "is limited to". An abstract noun — "scope", "basis", "extent" — is never the subject or the frame of a limit statement. "Scope" stands only in that excepted use, or as a defined project term ("scope of work") in object position.
26. "Carry" states physical transport (a stream, phase, or unit carries a quantity), or "carry out" for a procedure. "Carry" does not state that a text, equation, figure, quantity, or category includes, presents, or accounts for something. A comparison result is stated with "is" or "has" and its comparator; a figure's basis is stated as how the figure was computed ("is computed on that basis"); the quantities that define a comparison are introduced with "define".
