# Writing style

Rule numbers cite the detailed predecessor skills a rule comes from (STEM rules and patterns by their numbers; business rules as B-numbers). Evidence, corpus sizes, and limits for every rule are summarised in `EVIDENCE.md` in this directory. Single-corpus patterns held out of the main rules are in `APPENDIX.md`.

Use: the shared sections and the STEM sections govern all writing by default. The business section governs a document, or the non-technical sections of a mixed document, under the activation condition stated in that section. The equations section governs documents that carry displayed equations, in either domain.

## Register constraints

Journal-formal, unemotional register. Plain words. Each descriptive word is the most specific word that fits the observation: a change in a quantity is named by the word that matches what was observed (a reduction, where a procedure produced the change; a decrease, where a recorded value went down), not by a general or figurative word (a fall). Titles are short. Brevity by cutting words and clauses, not qualifications, ranges, or evidence. Define every symbol and every abbreviation before first use, with SI units where applicable. Headings are nominal and impersonal ("Computational Methods", not "How the numbers are computed"); no hype phrases or clickbait-style titles, no conversational fragments as headings, no anthropomorphizing, no second-person asides outside the instructional forms in rules 11–14. In STEM and instructional writing: explain, never persuade, with no argumentative framing and no rhetorical connectives. In business documents the persuasion switch in the business section governs in place of the preceding sentence. [Test amendment, combined test T3, T5, and D2] No fact, consequence, or causal link is stated that the source material for the document does not state; a derived figure is stated as derived, with its derivation.

## Sentences and paragraphs [shared]

1. Open the sentence with the noun the sentence is about. A participle frame ("Using …") is a minority method-first opening. "We" is used sparingly.
2. Keep explicit connectives sparse and spend them on contrast; "However" is the default marked transition. Addition and sequence stay unmarked unless the relation would otherwise be missed.
3. Carry continuity inside a paragraph by repeating the term. One object keeps one name; no synonym cycling, no signpost chains. A sentence that must stand on its own (a validity statement, a closing, the first sentence of a section or a paragraph) restates the object's name rather than carrying the object with a bare category noun: "Fick's law holds for a stagnant medium", not "The law holds for a stagnant medium"; the bare noun leaves the reader to re-identify the object. Where possible, "The" before a generic noun is replaced by a brief descriptive noun, a technical noun above all ("Fick's law" for "the law", the Antoine equation for "the equation" where the equation is the object). Repetition is not excessive where the occurrences stand far apart; closeness is the constraint, not repetition itself, and distance licenses the full restated form, openings included. Reference forms are consistent: an object introduced as "this skill" stays "this skill" through the passage, and "this" and "the" do not alternate for one referent. A new paragraph opens a new unit. Repetition of the term is not repetition of the sentence opening: sentence openings vary across a passage, and multiple sentences do not open with the same word (most often "The") unless the repetition is a deliberate emphasis. Where a sentence can be better written opening with words other than "The", that opening is used; "The" opens a sentence only where no better opening serves. A completed passage is checked by listing the first word of each sentence. The check crosses items: in a document with several items of one type (five tutorials, a set of abstracts), items of the same type do not open with the same word or phrase; the first words of peer items are listed alongside the first words within each item.
4. Vary phrase lengths within a sentence and sentence lengths within a paragraph. A paragraph mixes short and long sentences and closes on shorter sentences than it develops with. A results passage is not fragmented into single-datum sentences. Sentence and paragraph structures vary as well, within reason: a passage does not run on one construction, one clause pattern, or paragraphs of one shape and length where a varied structure is available. Structural variety is subordinate to the other rules: variety is not bought at the cost of a definition in place, a bundled number, a named owner, or claim strength, and where variety and another rule conflict, the other rule governs.
5. Put data values in parentheses inside the sentence rather than in a clause of their own.

## STEM documents: structure

6. Opening order: setting, established knowledge, gap, aim, method preview. The gap is stated as a missing piece of knowledge where that is the case.
7. Abstract order: setting, aim or approach, method, principal result (qualitative, then quantitative), interpretation, implication. No limitation is named in the abstract; limitations and scope conditions are named in the closing.
8. Interpretation stands next to the result it interprets. Results sentences carry a number by default.
9. Figures are referred to in the order the results need them. The caption is a noun phrase followed by conditions and sample size, and states the finding when the figure carries the claim.
10. The closing claim is held at or below the abstract claim. Strength is raised only where the closing combines evidence strands explicitly. The closing carries the principal claim at its tested scope, with the limitations named; a closing that states a limitation alone is used only where no scoped claim is available.

## STEM documents: instructional text [tutorials and explainers]

11. A tutorial states its goal in its opening sentences and shows or describes the outcome near the start.
12. A worked example has three parts: inputs, result, and what the result shows or how it is checked.
13. Tutorials address the reader; explainers withhold direct address. Audience and prerequisites are stated where the item assumes them.
14. Checkpoints, expected outputs, and warnings stand at the point of risk. A code block is preceded by a purpose sentence and followed by a statement of the output.

## Claim, number, and definition patterns [shared]

15. The limit stands inside the claim sentence: a concession clause first and the bounded claim as the main clause, or the exception, condition, or scope statement in the same sentence as the claim, placed before the number it qualifies.
16. The number is bundled with its qualifier in the same sentence: denominator or cohort, interval, and named baseline or contrast, in parentheses where the syntax requires it. A denominator is fixed in the sentence that defines the quantity. [Test amendment, combined test T1] In a worked example, the sentence that reports a prediction or a scored result carries the baseline the result is scored against in the same sentence, in parentheses where required.
17. A null result is stated as a detection statement with its conditions: what was not detected, under what conditions, over what range, or against which reference. A null is not stated as an absence.
18. A term is defined in place, at its first dependent use: parenthetical or appositive definition, an operational definition attached to the first reported value, or a henceforth sentence that fixes a short name. A composite notation is defined symbol by symbol, in symbol order. A quantity named as a model output or as part of a result (a prediction and its uncertainty, a value and its spread) is a term under this rule and is defined at first use; a worked example is checked at completion for a named quantity that no sentence defines.
19. The caption fixes what the text leaves open: method, threshold, baseline, error-bar provenance, replication count, conditions, spread, or the computation that produced the plotted quantity.
20. A choice or a threshold is keyed to its condition in a condition-first sentence: the condition opens the sentence and the recommendation follows.
21. A confirmation sentence names the independent route: the second device, technique, phenomenon, or laboratory that makes the route independent.
22. [analogy in use] An analogy or simplification is followed, within one sentence or the next, by a statement of where it stops holding.

## Business documents

Activation: this section governs when the user asks for business or consulting writing, or assigns a business document type (a recommendation memo or report, an executive summary, a slide storyline). Content type alone does not activate this section. In a mixed document the classification is by section: technical sections follow the STEM sections, and non-technical sections follow this section with the shared sections.

Overrides, business documents only. O1: rules 6 and 7 do not govern the opening; rules B1 and B2 govern in their place. O2: the register constraint on persuasion is replaced by screened persuasion: recommendation and framing devices measured in the business corpus are used, and devices that misstate evidence are not used; the prohibitions in B9 state the screen. O3: rule 9 governs journal-form exhibits; consulting-form exhibits follow B6. O4: rule 10 governs claim strength; a business document that cannot restate its claim closes by a move under B5.

B1. The bottom line stands before the evidence. A summary block, a deck sentence, or an opening paragraph states the finding or the recommendation before the evidence sections.
B2. The opening carries a frame, performed and not labelled: a situation, a complication, and the question the document answers, in that order, or a named alternative frame (a failure base rate, a valuation puzzle, a challenge catalogue). The frame label does not appear in the document. [Test amendment, combined test T3] The frame is a required move, not an alternative to the bottom line: B1 and B2 are both performed in the opening, the frame in the opening paragraph or paragraphs. Situation material stated after the recommendation does not perform the frame. A business document is checked at completion: bottom line present, frame performed, owner class named where a recommendation is made, evidence base named where the finding is stated; a document missing one of these is not complete.
B3. The evidence base is named where the finding is stated: the survey, sample, or use-case base, with its count, in the summary or in the exhibit notes, not only in a methodology section.
B4. A recommendation is written as an action with a named owner class. Timing and quantified benefit stand in the evidence sections or in a closing passage, not in the recommendation sentence.
B5. Closing moves where a claim is not restated: next steps, questions for the reader's team, a checklist, or an invitation. A formal decision ask, with a named decision maker and a date, is not a corpus form (0 of 22 coded documents) and is used only where the user asks for one.
B6. Exhibit titles, consulting form: the title states the finding, with its number or comparison, and a source line under the exhibit names the data base and the respondent count. Journal form: the title names the subject and the caption states the finding, as in rule 9. [Test amendment, combined test T5] In a slide storyline, the opening title states the recommendation complete, with every element of it (action, scope, held items, decision point), and each support line belongs to the title it follows; the title–support pairing is checked line by line at completion.
B7. A trade-off is stated in one sentence with both numbers present: benefit and cost, or gain and risk. Neither side of the trade-off is stated without the other.
B8. A period result is stated as one sentence carrying its components, or a headline figure is replaced in the next sentence by its signed components at the same scale, so the reader can recompute the total.
B9. Prohibitions under the persuasion screen: a closing claim stronger than the opening claim with no evidence added between the two; a superlative with no comparison set stated in the text; a certainty opener with no evidence pointer in the same sentence; an analogy in closing position with no boundary stated.

## Equations [documents with displayed equations; STEM patterns 28–31]

E1. Symbol definitions follow a preference order. [Test amendment, diagnostic case D1; consistent with slim-test case S6] Where the set-up is a complete sentence naming the quantities, all symbols of the display are defined in the set-up sentence, in symbol order, with units in parentheses. Where the set-up is a lead-in clause, the central object is defined in prose before the display and the remaining symbols in the sentence immediately after the display, as a where-clause roster in equation order. A table carries the roster only where the text states the deferral. One sentence per symbol, ahead of the display, is not the corpus form and fragments the passage.
E2. The set-up is a lead-in clause that the display completes, or a complete sentence that names the quantities the equation relates. A set-up states an assumption or a derivation from a named prior relation where either is the equation's basis.
E3. The sentence immediately after the display is the definition sentence. An interpretation follows the roster, not the display directly.
E4. A boundary or case (a piecewise threshold, an endpoint case, an interpretation boundary) stands adjacent to the equation: in the set-up, the display, or the following text. [Test amendment, diagnostic case D1] Where the boundary is a case on the equation's own variable (a threshold value of that variable), the boundary stands in the sentence immediately after the display. A numbered display is referred back to by its number; an unnumbered definitional display is referred back to by name. The validity of a law or an analysis is stated with the law named as subject and the conditions in the same sentence, in the textbook form: "holds for" a stated case, "applies when" a condition is met, "is valid for" a stated range; the case outside is stated as what changes (a moving medium adds a convection term to the flux). "Scope ends at" is not the form for a law's validity.

## What not to do [shared, unless marked]

1. Synonym cycling for the central object or quantity. A new name forces the reader to re-identify the object.
2. A number in a sentence of its own, with its baseline, denominator, or conditions in a separate sentence or later. A value separated from its comparison has no stated meaning at the point of reading.
3. Inferential connectives that tell the reader the conclusion ("therefore", "thus", a causal "so"). The result and its evidence are stated; the inference is the reader's. [STEM and instructional documents; in business documents the persuasion screen in B9 governs framing devices, and a connective that misstates evidence remains prohibited.]
4. Meta-signposts that announce the text's own moves. The interpretation, the example, or the limit is stated, not announced.
5. Restatement of a result in a further sentence that adds no scope, condition, or limit. A result is stated once; a restatement stands only where the added material changes the claim's scope or strength.
6. [business documents] A finding or recommendation held back to the close. The bottom line stands before the evidence (B1), and the close restates it at or below its opening strength.
7. Multiple sentences in a passage opening with the same word, most often "The", and a "The" opening where a better opening exists. Repetition of a term carries continuity (rule 3); repetition of an opening word flattens the passage, and stands only as deliberate emphasis.
8. A general or figurative word where a specific word names the observation: "fall" for a change in a quantity that a procedure reduced or a record shows decreased. The word is chosen for the observation, not for variety.
9. Alternating reference forms for one object ("this skill" in one sentence, "the skill" in the next). One referent keeps one form of reference through a passage (rule 3).
10. Peer items opening alike: every tutorial in a set opening "Your goal", every abstract opening with the same word. Items of one type in a document open differently from each other (rule 3).
11. One construction repeated through a passage: sentences of one shape and paragraphs of one shape and length, where a varied structure was available at no cost to the other rules (rule 4).
12. A bare category noun carrying a named object in a sentence that must stand on its own ("The law holds…", "The model predicts…"), where the object's name is available and is restated instead (rule 3).

Caution: conditions, replication, and spread belong in the caption in full, and a trend sentence carries its values with it.

## AI-writing patterns [all writing, chat replies included]

Source: Wikipedia, "Signs of AI writing", a WikiProject AI Cleanup advice page; the page is descriptive, not prescriptive. The fault is density and formula, not a single item: one listed word, correct grammar, a formal register, or one transition word is not a defect. Each draft is checked for clusters.

(1) Content. State specific facts. Importance is not inflated with claims about significance, legacy, or broader trends ("stands as a testament", "pivotal moment", "reflects broader", "setting the stage for", "evolving landscape") unless the claim is sourced and is itself the subject. No trailing present-participle clause that adds vague analysis ("highlighting", "underscoring", "ensuring", "reflecting", "contributing to", "fostering"). No promotional wording ("boasts", "vibrant", "groundbreaking", "renowned", "nestled", "in the heart of", "commitment to"). An opinion is attributed to a named source; vague attributions ("experts argue", "industry reports", "observers have cited") are not used. No stock closing of the form "Despite its …, it faces challenges … Despite these challenges …", and no unsourced "Future outlook" section.
(2) Word choice. The density of the overused set is kept low: "additionally" at the start of a sentence, "align with", "bolstered", "crucial", "delve", "emphasizing", "enduring", "enhance", "fostering", "garner", "highlight", "interplay", "intricate", "key" as an adjective, "landscape" as an abstract noun, "meticulous", "pivotal", "robust", "showcase", "tapestry" as an abstract noun, "testament", "underscore" as a verb, "valuable", "vibrant". The plain word is used when it says the same thing: "used" for "utilized", "wrote" for "authored", "tried" for "attempted", "moved" for "relocated".
(3) Sentence form. "Is", "are", and "has" are used where they fit, not replaced by "serves as", "stands as", "marks", "represents", "boasts", "features", or "offers". A relation is stated directly, with "of", "for", "by", "in", or "with", not hidden in "in connection with" or "associated with". Stock negative parallelisms ("not just X, but Y"; "not X, it is Y"; "X rather than Y") and lists of three used to sound complete are avoided.
(4) Filler and framing. "It is important to note", "worth noting", "in summary", "in conclusion", and "overall" restatements are omitted. Chat filler is kept out of documents and low in chat ("Certainly", "Of course", "I hope this helps", "let me know"). No canned assurances about a revision ("improved clarity and flow", "refined for neutrality", "while preserving the original meaning"); the actual change is named. When information was not found, what was checked and what was not verified is stated; "while specific details are limited", "based on available information", and knowledge-cutoff disclaimers are not used, and the gap is not filled with speculation.
(5) Format. No mechanical boldface, no "**Term:** description" list as a default form, no emoji in headings or bullets, no thematic break between every section, no small table where prose is clearer. The heading case of the target style is used; title case is not the default. Em dash use is kept low; a comma, a colon, parentheses, or a full stop stands in its place. No placeholder or template text in a delivered draft.
