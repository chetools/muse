Writing skill: paired samples

This skill corrects defects that recur in AI-written prose: claims stronger than the evidence, numbers apart from their comparisons and conditions, terms used before definition, findings held to the final paragraph. A draft written with this skill states each claim at the strength of its evidence, where a reader can check the claim. Development came from analysis of published writing: highly cited papers in Nature and Science, tutorials and explainers from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and DeepMind, and business reports from Harvard Business Review, MIT Sloan Management Review, McKinsey, Boston Consulting Group and Bain. The samples below state the same facts with and without this skill.

Highlighting: pale red marks a weak form in the without-skill passage. Pale green marks the corresponding form in the with-skill passage. The line after each pair names the differences shown.

Sample 2 (STEM) — Tutorial: heat-transfer coefficient from a cooling curve

Package load for the with-skill version: core + textbook reference + ai-check reference (revision pass).

Shared fact base. An estimate of the overall heat-transfer coefficient, denoted U (W m−2 K−1), comes from fitting a cooling model to measured liquid temperatures. A residual is a measured temperature minus the model temperature at the same time; the sum of squared residuals has units of K2. Standard error of an estimate is the spread of estimates the same fit would give across repeated measurement sets with the same measurement noise. Worked example: 6 measurements over 30 min; starting guess U = 420 W m−2 K−1 gives a sum of squared residuals of 18.6 K2; the fit gives U = 512 W m−2 K−1 with a standard error of 24 W m−2 K−1 and a sum of squared residuals of 2.1 K2; iterations stop when the relative change in the sum of squared residuals falls below 0.1%. Room temperature is held constant in the model, and no evaporation is admitted.

Without the skill

Estimating an overall heat-transfer coefficient from a cooling curve begins with measured liquid temperatures and a cooling model fitted to those measurements. The fitted quantity is U, expressed in W m−2 K−1. At each measurement time, the residual is the measured temperature minus the model temperature. Squaring and adding the residuals gives the sum of squared residuals, which has units of K2 and shows how closely the model follows the measurements.

The standard error describes the spread of estimates that the same fit would produce across repeated measurement sets having the same measurement noise. It indicates how much the fitted U might vary if the measurements were collected under the same noise conditions.

In the worked example, six measurements are taken over 30 min. A starting guess of U = 420 W m−2 K−1 gives a sum of squared residuals of 18.6 K2. The fit gives U = 512 W m−2 K−1, with a standard error of 24 W m−2 K−1 and a sum of squared residuals of 2.1 K2. Iterations stop when the relative change in the sum of squared residuals falls below 0.1%. The model holds room temperature constant and admits no evaporation. The comparison between the starting and fitted residual sums shows the improvement produced by fitting.

Sample 8 (STEM) — Equation passage: Fick's first law of diffusion

Package load for the with-skill version: core + equations reference + textbook reference + ai-check reference (revision pass).

Shared fact base and notation for this sample. The diffusive flux J (mol m−2 s−1) through a stagnant, dilute medium is computed from the diffusivity D (m2 s−1) and the concentration gradient dC/dx; C is concentration (mol m−3) and x is position (m). Fick's first law: J = −D dC/dx. A negative sign in the law states that diffusion runs from high concentration to low concentration. Worked values: oxygen in water, D = 2.1 × 10−9 m2 s−1; concentration decreases by 0.25 mol m−3 across a film 0.5 mm thick, giving dC/dx = −500 mol m−4 and J = 1.05 × 10−6 mol m−2 s−1. In a stagnant medium, Fick's first law applies; a moving medium adds convection, which is not included in the equation.

With the skill

Determine the total pressure drop and the required pump power for water pumped through a straight pipe run: the result is a total pressure drop of 46.9 kPa and a required pump power of 1.34 kW.

Take the data as given: water at 293 K (density ρ, 998 kg/m³; viscosity μ, 1.00 × 10⁻³ Pa·s); volumetric flow rate Q of 0.020 m³/s; pipe length L of 60 m; pipe inside diameter D of 0.102 m; pipe roughness ε of 0.045 mm; four elbows at a loss coefficient of 0.9 each, an entrance at 0.5, and an exit at 1.0 (sum of loss coefficients ΣK, 5.1); pump efficiency η of 0.70.

Mean velocity u (m/s) is calculated from flow rate and diameter:

u = 4Q/(πD²) (1)

Substitution in Eq. (1) thus gives u = 2.45 m/s. The Reynolds number Re follows from density, velocity, diameter, and viscosity:

Re = ρuD/μ (2)

Eq. (2) thus gives Re = 2.49 × 10⁵, and the flow is turbulent. The Haaland equation, with the Reynolds number and the relative roughness, gives the Darcy friction factor f = 0.0180.

Friction pressure drop Δp_f (kPa) is calculated from friction factor, length, diameter, density, and velocity, Eq. (3):

Δp_f = f (L/D)(ρu²/2) (3)

Δp_f is thus 31.6 kPa. Fittings pressure drop Δp_K (kPa) is calculated from the sum of loss coefficients, density, and velocity:

Δp_K = ΣK (ρu²/2) (4)

Eq. (4) thus gives Δp_K = 15.2 kPa, and the total pressure drop Δp is 46.9 kPa. Required pump power P (kW) is calculated from flow rate, total pressure drop, and efficiency, Eq. (5):

P = QΔp/η (5)

P is thus 1.34 kW.

Fittings contribute 15.2 kPa of the 46.9 kPa total, about one third. Shortening the run or removing fittings changes the answer more than a small change in the friction factor would.
