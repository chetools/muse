Writing skill: paired samples

This skill corrects defects that recur in AI-written prose: claims stronger than the evidence, numbers apart from their comparisons and conditions, terms used before definition, findings held to the final paragraph. A draft written with this skill states each claim at the strength of its evidence, where a reader can check the claim. Development came from analysis of published writing: highly cited papers in Nature and Science, tutorials and explainers from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and DeepMind, and business reports from Harvard Business Review, MIT Sloan Management Review, McKinsey, Boston Consulting Group and Bain. The samples below state the same facts with and without this skill.

Highlighting: pale red marks a weak form in the without-skill passage. Pale green marks the corresponding form in the with-skill passage. The line after each pair names the differences shown.

Sample 1 (STEM) — Research abstract: structured catalyst for methanol synthesis

Shared fact base. Carbon dioxide (CO2) and hydrogen (H2) react over a copper–zinc oxide catalyst to give methanol. Space-time yield is the mass of methanol produced per unit catalyst volume per hour, in kilograms per litre per hour (kg L−1 h−1). Test conditions: 250 °C, 50 bar, feed ratio H2/CO2 = 3. Structured catalyst: space-time yield 0.84 kg L−1 h−1, pressure drop 0.18 bar, activity loss 6% after 100 h. Pellet catalyst: 0.61 kg L−1 h−1, 1.25 bar, 14% after 100 h. One feed composition was tested; no sulfur-bearing feed was tested. Yield and pressure drop are seldom reported for the same catalyst charge.

Without the skill

Methanol synthesis from CO2 is a game-changing route and the catalyst is the heart of the process. The material was tested at 250 °C and 50 bar with a feed ratio H2/CO2 = 3. The yield was excellent. It reached 0.84. The pellets gave 0.61. Pressure drop was also much better for the new system, and activity loss after 100 h was lower. Therefore, the structured catalyst clearly outperforms the conventional catalyst on every axis. The reactor packing lost 6% activity, while the older bed lost 14%. Pressure drop values were 0.18 bar and 1.25 bar respectively. In summary, this breakthrough catalyst will transform methanol production, highlighting its robust and vibrant performance. One feed composition was used in the tests.

With the skill

Methanol synthesis from CO2 and H2 over copper–zinc oxide catalysts is limited in fixed beds by pressure drop across the pellet bed. Yield and pressure drop are seldom reported for the same catalyst charge, and the charge form that holds yield at low pressure drop is the missing comparison. A structured catalyst and a pellet catalyst of the same composition were tested at 250 °C and 50 bar with a feed ratio H2/CO2 = 3. Space-time yield for the structured catalyst was 0.84 kg L−1 h−1 at a pressure drop of 0.18 bar (pellet catalyst: 0.61 kg L−1 h−1 at 1.25 bar), and activity loss after 100 h was 6% for the structured catalyst against 14% for the pellet catalyst. Over the 100 h test, the structured charge held the higher yield at the lower pressure drop. At the tested feed composition, with no sulfur-bearing feed tested, the structured catalyst is the higher-yield, lower-pressure-drop form of the two charges.

Differences: the with-skill abstract follows the setting, gap, method, result, closing order; values stand with their comparators and conditions in one sentence; one name is kept for each catalyst; no connective announces the conclusion; the closing claim stays at the tested scope and carries its limits. In the without-skill version, values stand separated from comparators, names cycle (catalyst, material, system, packing, bed), the activity result is restated, and the closing rises above the evidence.

Sample 2 (STEM) — Tutorial: heat-transfer coefficient from a cooling curve

Shared fact base. An estimate of the overall heat-transfer coefficient, denoted U (watts per square metre per kelvin, W m−2 K−1), comes from fitting a cooling model to measured liquid temperatures. A residual is a measured temperature minus the model temperature at the same time; the sum of squared residuals has units of kelvin squared (K2). Standard error of an estimate is the spread of estimates the same fit would give across repeated measurement sets with the same measurement noise. Worked example: 6 measurements over 30 min; starting guess U = 420 W m−2 K−1 gives a sum of squared residuals of 18.6 K2; the fit gives U = 512 W m−2 K−1 with a standard error of 24 W m−2 K−1 and a sum of squared residuals of 2.1 K2; iterations stop when the relative change in the sum of squared residuals falls below 0.1%. Room temperature is held constant in the model, and no evaporation is admitted.

Without the skill

This section will walk through the fitting procedure. A model is fitted to the temperatures. The fit adjusts the coefficient. It reports uncertainty. Six measurements were taken. They covered 30 minutes. The starting guess was 420. The residuals were 18.6. The fit then gave 512, and the uncertainty was 24. The residuals fell to 2.1. Therefore the fit is good. Iterations stop at a relative change below 0.1%. The fitted value of 512 shows that the procedure works well. Room temperature is held constant in the model, and evaporation is not included.

With the skill

Your goal is an estimate of the overall heat-transfer coefficient U from a measured cooling curve: at the end of the fit you hold U = 512 W m−2 K−1 in place of the starting guess of 420 W m−2 K−1. You record the liquid temperature at stated times and adjust U until the sum of squared residuals is smallest; a residual is a measured temperature minus the model temperature at the same time. U is reported with its standard error, the spread of estimates the same fit would give across repeated measurement sets with the same measurement noise. In the worked example, 6 measurements over 30 min give U = 512 W m−2 K−1 with a standard error of 24 W m−2 K−1 and a sum of squared residuals of 2.1 K2 (starting guess U = 420 W m−2 K−1: 18.6 K2). Reduction of the sum of squared residuals from 18.6 K2 to 2.1 K2 is the check that the fitted curve tracks the measured curve; iterations stop when the relative change in the sum of squared residuals falls below 0.1%. Model conditions bound the estimate: room temperature constant, and no evaporation.

Differences: the with-skill tutorial states the goal and the outcome in its opening sentences, addresses the reader, defines the standard error at first use, and carries the starting-guess baseline inside the sentence that reports the fitted result. In the without-skill version, the procedure is announced in place of a stated goal, uncertainty stays undefined, the baseline and the fitted value stand in separate fragments, and the closing restates the result.

Sample 3 (STEM) — Equation passage: Reynolds number and flow regime in a pipe

Shared fact base and notation for this sample. Re (dimensionless), the Reynolds number, is computed from the fluid density ρ (kilograms per cubic metre, kg m−3), the mean velocity v (metres per second, m s−1), the pipe diameter D (metres, m), and the dynamic viscosity μ (pascal seconds, Pa s). Flow is laminar below Re = 2,300 and turbulent above Re = 4,000; between the two values the regime is transitional and is not assigned by the number alone. Worked values: water in a pipe of diameter 0.05 m at a mean velocity of 1.2 m s−1 gives Re = 60,000.

Without the skill

The Reynolds number is given by

Re = ρ v D / μ

Re is the Reynolds number. ρ is density. v is velocity. D is diameter. μ is viscosity. Therefore, a big Re means turbulence. For water in a 0.05 m pipe at 1.2 m s−1, Re = 60,000, so the flow state is turbulent. The flow condition changes with the number: the regime is laminar under 2,300 and turbulent over 4,000, with a transition zone in between.

With the skill

Reynolds number Re (dimensionless), fluid density ρ (kg m−3), mean velocity v (m s−1), pipe diameter D (m), and dynamic viscosity μ (Pa s) are related by

Re = ρ v D / μ

Flow is laminar below Re = 2,300 and turbulent above Re = 4,000; between the two values the regime is transitional and is not assigned by the number alone. For water in a pipe of diameter 0.05 m at a mean velocity of 1.2 m s−1, Re = 60,000, and the flow is in the turbulent range as bounded in the preceding sentence.

Differences: the with-skill passage defines all symbols in the set-up sentence, in symbol order, before the display, and places the boundary values in the sentence immediately after the display. In the without-skill passage, symbols are defined one per sentence after the display, three names cycle for the regime (flow state, flow condition, regime), and the boundary values wait for the final sentence.

Sample 4 (Business) — Recommendation memo: predictive maintenance sensors on pumps

Shared fact base. 214 pumps run at the plant. Maintenance records for 12 months show 1,180 h of unplanned downtime; bearing failures account for 46% of the downtime hours. A sensor set for all 214 pumps is quoted at $420k installed, plus $68k per year. In a vendor trial on a comparable unit, the sensors cut downtime by 22%; the figure is a vendor figure and was not measured at this plant. Pilot cost on 18 pumps is $36k over three months. Spare-part lead times are unchanged by the sensors. Responsibility for running the pilot and reporting before the March capital review sits with the reliability engineering group.

Without the skill

Situation: the plant runs 214 pumps and downtime is a growing burden. Complication: bearing failures cause 46% of the downtime hours. Question: should sensors be installed? Records cover 12 months. Unplanned downtime reached 1,180 h. Installation is quoted at $420k. The yearly charge is $68k. A vendor trial reports 22%, which proves the technology works. A pilot would cost $36k. This game-changing programme is clearly the best available option, and leadership should seize it. The recommendation is to approve a three-month pilot on 18 pumps, run by the reliability engineering group, before the March capital review.

With the skill

214 pumps run at the plant on a maintenance regime that records failures after they occur, and bearing failures stop pumps without warning; the question for the capital review is whether detection before failure is worth a plant-wide sensor installation. A three-month pilot of the sensors on 18 pumps, at a cost of $36k, is recommended by the reliability engineering group, with the decision on full installation held until pilot results are reported. Evidence comes from the plant's maintenance records for 12 months: 1,180 h of unplanned downtime, of which bearing failures account for 46%. Full installation is quoted at $420k plus $68k per year; the pilot carries the trade-off in one comparison: $36k buys three months of detection data on this plant's pumps, against $420k committed on a vendor figure of 22% downtime reduction measured at another plant. Spare-part lead times are unchanged by the sensors. As stated, the pilot recommendation stands: detection performance at this plant is the untested quantity, and the March capital review decides full installation on the pilot report.

Differences: the with-skill memo performs the opening frame without labels, states the recommendation before the evidence with its owner class, names the evidence base with its count where the finding is stated, carries the trade-off in one sentence with both figures, and holds the closing at the opening strength. In the without-skill memo, the frame is labelled, the yearly charge stands separated from the installation figure, the vendor figure is presented as proof, and the recommendation sits in the final sentence after a stronger closing claim.

Sample 5 (STEM/business mix) — Carbon capture retrofit at a cement kiln: technical note and investment recommendation

Shared fact base. Carbon dioxide is denoted CO2. Kiln emissions are 780 kilotonnes (kt) of CO2 per year. An amine absorption unit, tested at pilot scale at 12 tonnes (t) of CO2 per day over 60 days, captured 86% of the CO2 in the treated gas; steam demand was 3.4 gigajoules per tonne of CO2 (GJ t−1) and solvent loss 1.8 kilograms per tonne (kg t−1). Beyond 60 days, the effect of kiln dust on the solvent is untested. Full-scale cost: capital $210 million, operating cost $38 million per year, capture cost $64 per tonne. At an assumed carbon price of $70 per tonne (a scenario value, not a forecast), the payback is 9.6 years. Ownership is split: the process engineering group owns the design; the finance group owns the investment case.

Without the skill

The amine unit is a breakthrough for decarbonisation and will capture the kiln's emissions. In the pilot it captured 86%, with steam at 3.4 and solvent loss at 1.8. Therefore the technology is proven at scale. The kiln emits 780 kt per year, so the retrofit transforms the plant's footprint. On the money side, the project costs $210 million. Operating costs are $38 million per year. The capture cost works out at $64 per tonne and payback is under a decade. Carbon is priced at $70. Experts agree this is the right moment to act. One point is that kiln dust effects past 60 days have not been tested. The recommendation, finally, is a staged design study, with process engineering and finance involved.

With the skill

Technical note. At pilot scale (12 t per day over 60 days), the amine absorption unit captured 86% of the CO2 in the treated gas, at a steam demand of 3.4 GJ per tonne of CO2 captured and a solvent loss of 1.8 kg per tonne. Over 60 days only, the capture figure is a pilot figure; the effect of kiln dust on the solvent beyond 60 days is untested, and no full-scale capture rate is stated. Investment recommendation. Kiln emissions of 780 kt of CO2 per year stand against a capture cost of $64 per tonne at full scale, and the investment question is whether a design study is justified before any construction decision. A staged design study, owned jointly by the finance group and the process engineering group, is recommended, with construction held until the solvent question is settled by a longer pilot. Capital cost is $210 million and operating cost $38 million per year; at the scenario carbon price of $70 per tonne, stated as an assumption, the payback is 9.6 years. Recommendation scope stays as stated: a study and a longer pilot, decided on solvent behaviour beyond 60 days.

Differences: the with-skill version classifies by section. In the technical note, the pilot result stands with its scale and duration in the claim sentence, the untested item is named, and no full-scale rate is stated. Recommendation order in the investment section puts the recommendation before the cost evidence; both owner classes are named, and the carbon price is carried as a labelled scenario value in the payback sentence. Promotional wording in the technical note, a carbon price separated from the payback, a vague attribution, and a recommendation deferred to the final sentence mark the without-skill version.

Sample 6 (STEM) — Research abstract: pervaporation membranes for ethanol dehydration

Shared fact base. Pervaporation is a membrane separation in which a liquid feed contacts one side of a membrane and vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through as vapour. Flux is the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour (kg m−2 h−1). The separation factor is the water-to-ethanol ratio in the permeate divided by the water-to-ethanol ratio in the feed (dimensionless). Feed: 85 wt% ethanol in water, at 60 °C. Polymer membrane: flux 1.9 kg m−2 h−1, separation factor 180, flux fell 11% over 500 h. Zeolite membrane: flux 1.2 kg m−2 h−1, separation factor 420, flux unchanged over 500 h within measurement error. One feed composition was tested; no test ran beyond 500 h; no fouling feed was tested. Seldom are flux, selectivity, and stability reported together for the same membrane.

Without the skill

Pervaporation is a membrane separation in which a liquid feed contacts one side of a membrane and vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through as vapour. Two membranes were compared for dehydrating ethanol at a single feed composition of 85 wt% ethanol in water at 60 °C. Performance is reported as flux, the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour (kg m−2 h−1), and as the separation factor, the water-to-ethanol ratio in the permeate divided by the water-to-ethanol ratio in the feed. The polymer membrane gave a flux of 1.9 kg m−2 h−1 with a separation factor of 180, but its flux fell by 11% over 500 h. The zeolite membrane gave a lower flux of 1.2 kg m−2 h−1 with a higher separation factor of 420, and its flux was unchanged over 500 h within measurement error. Flux, selectivity, and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane. The results cover one feed composition only; no test ran beyond 500 h and no fouling feed was tested.

With the skill

Ethanol dehydration by pervaporation separates water from ethanol across a membrane: a liquid feed contacts one side of the membrane, and vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through as vapour. Reports seldom give flux, selectivity, and stability together for the same membrane. A polymer membrane and a zeolite membrane were compared on one feed (85 wt% ethanol in water) at 60 °C over 500 h. Two quantities carry the comparison: flux, the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour (kg m−2 h−1), and the separation factor, the water-to-ethanol ratio in the permeate divided by the water-to-ethanol ratio in the feed (dimensionless). Higher flux came from the polymer membrane, and higher selectivity from the zeolite membrane. Polymer membrane flux was 1.9 kg m−2 h−1 at a separation factor of 180, and flux decreased 11% over 500 h (zeolite membrane: 1.2 kg m−2 h−1 at a separation factor of 420, flux unchanged over 500 h within measurement error). Choice between the two membranes, at the tested feed composition, rests on the weight given to flux against selectivity and stability; no test ran beyond 500 h, and no fouling feed was tested.

Differences: the with-skill abstract states the gap before the method, defines flux and the separation factor before the results, carries stability inside the result sentence, and closes on a scoped choice between the two membranes. In the without-skill version, the gap stands after the results, each membrane takes a separate sentence, and the closing lists limits with no scoped claim.

Sample 7 (STEM) — Tutorial: vapour pressure from the Antoine equation

Shared fact base. An estimate of the vapour pressure of ethanol at a stated temperature is the goal. Vapour pressure is the pressure at which a liquid and its vapour stand in equilibrium at that temperature. The Antoine equation relates vapour pressure P (mmHg) to temperature T (°C): log10(P) = A − B/(T + C), where A, B, C are constants for the liquid (dimensionless, dimensionless, °C). Ethanol constants: A = 8.20417, B = 1642.89, C = 230.300, holding from −57 °C to 80 °C. Worked example at T = 60 °C: T + C = 290.300 °C; B/(T + C) = 5.659; log10(P) = 2.545; P = 351 mmHg (0.468 bar). Check at T = 78.4 °C: P = 762 mmHg, against 760 mmHg for boiling at atmospheric pressure. As an empirical fit, the equation's constants do not apply outside the stated range.

Without the skill

This tutorial estimates the vapour pressure of ethanol at a stated temperature. The vapour pressure is the pressure at which a liquid and its vapour stand in equilibrium at that temperature. The Antoine equation relates vapour pressure P, in millimetres of mercury (mmHg), to temperature T, in degrees Celsius (°C): log10(P) = A − B/(T + C). Here A, B and C are constants for the liquid: A and B are dimensionless, and C is in degrees Celsius. For ethanol, A = 8.20417, B = 1642.89 and C = 230.300, and the constants hold from −57 °C to 80 °C. In the worked example at T = 60 °C, T + C = 290.300 °C and B/(T + C) = 5.659, so log10(P) = 2.545 and P = 351 mmHg, or 0.468 bar. A check at the normal boiling point, T = 78.4 °C, gives P = 762 mmHg from the equation, against 760 mmHg for boiling at atmospheric pressure. The Antoine equation is an empirical fit, and outside the stated temperature range the constants do not apply.

With the skill

An estimate of the vapour pressure of ethanol at a stated temperature comes from the Antoine equation: at 60 °C, P = 351 millimetres of mercury (mmHg), equal to 0.468 bar. Vapour pressure is the pressure at which a liquid and its vapour stand in equilibrium at that temperature. Calculation uses the Antoine equation, log10(P) = A − B/(T + C), relating vapour pressure P (millimetres of mercury, mmHg) to temperature T (degrees Celsius, °C) through constants A (dimensionless), B (dimensionless), and C (degrees Celsius, °C) fitted for the liquid. Ethanol constants are A = 8.20417, B = 1642.89, and C = 230.300, and the constants hold from −57 °C to 80 °C. You substitute T = 60 °C: T + C = 290.300 °C and B/(T + C) = 5.659, giving log10(P) = 2.545 and P = 351 mmHg. A check at the normal boiling point tests the result: at T = 78.4 °C the equation gives P = 762 mmHg, against 760 mmHg for boiling at atmospheric pressure. Close agreement with the boiling value supports the estimate at 60 °C. Outside the stated range the constants do not apply, and the equation is an empirical fit.

Differences: the with-skill tutorial states the goal and the numerical outcome in its opening sentence and names the boiling-point calculation as a check on the result. In the without-skill version, the opening announces the passage itself, the outcome appears only inside the worked example, and no sentence names the check as a check.

Sample 8 (STEM) — Equation passage: Fick's first law of diffusion

Shared fact base and notation for this sample. The diffusive flux J (mol m−2 s−1) through a stagnant, dilute medium is computed from the diffusivity D (m2 s−1) and the concentration gradient dC/dx; C is concentration (mol m−3) and x is position (m). Fick's first law: J = −D dC/dx. A negative sign in the law states that diffusion runs from high concentration to low concentration. Worked values: oxygen in water, D = 2.1 × 10−9 m2 s−1; concentration falls by 0.25 mol m−3 across a film 0.5 mm thick, giving dC/dx = −500 mol m−4 and J = 1.05 × 10−6 mol m−2 s−1. Scope: a stagnant medium; a moving medium adds convection, which the equation does not carry.

Without the skill

Diffusion moves a species from regions of high concentration to regions of low concentration, and Fick's first law gives the rate of that movement through a stagnant, dilute medium. The diffusive flux J (moles per square metre per second, mol m−2 s−1) is computed from the diffusivity D (square metres per second, m2 s−1) and the concentration gradient dC/dx, where C is concentration (moles per cubic metre, mol m−3) and x is position (metres, m). The law is:

J = −D dC/dx

The negative sign states the direction of the process: diffusion runs from high concentration to low concentration. Where concentration falls as position increases, the gradient is negative and the flux comes out positive.

Oxygen in water provides a worked example. The diffusivity is D = 2.1 × 10−9 m2 s−1, and the concentration falls by 0.25 mol m−3 across a film 0.5 mm thick, so dC/dx = −500 mol m−4. Substitution into the law gives J = 1.05 × 10−6 mol m−2 s−1.

A scope condition closes the passage. The law as stated covers a stagnant medium; a moving medium adds convection, which the equation does not carry.

With the skill

Fick's first law computes the diffusive flux J (moles per square metre per second, mol m−2 s−1) of a species through a stagnant, dilute medium from the diffusivity D (square metres per second, m2 s−1) and the concentration gradient dC/dx, where C is the concentration (moles per cubic metre, mol m−3) and x is position (metres, m):

J = −D dC/dx

A negative sign carries the direction of transport: diffusion runs from high concentration to low concentration. Worked values for oxygen in water use D = 2.1 × 10−9 m2 s−1. Across a film 0.5 mm thick, a concentration difference of 0.25 mol m−3 gives dC/dx = −500 mol m−4, and substitution into the displayed relation gives J = 1.05 × 10−6 mol m−2 s−1. The law holds for a stagnant medium; in a moving medium, convection adds a term to the flux that the equation as written does not carry.

Differences: the with-skill passage defines every symbol in the set-up sentence, in symbol order, before the display, and closes by stating the conditions under which the law holds. In the without-skill version, the opening sentence carries no quantity, a bare lead-in introduces the display, and the closing sentence announces the scope condition instead of only stating the condition.

Sample 10 (STEM/business mix) — Compressed-air leak repair at a plastics plant: technical note and investment recommendation

Saving from the 41 repairs is estimated at $17.5k per year (an estimate, not a metered value), a payback of 6.7 months on the repair cost. Maintenance would carry out the repairs and owns the repair programme; the plant manager owns the decision. A meter can be fitted after repair to measure the saving.
