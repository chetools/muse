Writing skill: paired samples

This skill corrects defects that recur in AI-written prose: claims stronger than the evidence, numbers apart from their comparisons and conditions, terms used before definition, findings held to the final paragraph. A draft written with this skill states each claim at the strength of its evidence, where a reader can check the claim. Development came from analysis of published writing: highly cited papers in Nature and Science, tutorials and explainers from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and DeepMind, and business reports from Harvard Business Review, MIT Sloan Management Review, McKinsey, Boston Consulting Group and Bain. The samples below state the same facts with and without this skill.

Highlighting: pale red marks a weak form in the without-skill passage. Pale green marks the corresponding form in the with-skill passage. The line after each pair names the differences shown.

Sample 1 (STEM) — Research abstract: structured catalyst for methanol synthesis

Package load for the with-skill version: core only.

Shared fact base. Carbon dioxide and hydrogen react over a copper–zinc oxide catalyst to give methanol. Space-time yield is the mass of methanol produced per unit catalyst volume per hour, in kilograms per litre per hour (kg L−1 h−1). Test conditions: 250 °C, 50 bar, feed ratio H2/CO2 = 3. Structured catalyst: space-time yield 0.84 kg L−1 h−1, pressure drop 0.18 bar, activity loss 6% after 100 h. Pellet catalyst: 0.61 kg L−1 h−1, 1.25 bar, 14% after 100 h. One feed composition was tested; no sulfur-bearing feed was tested. Yield and pressure drop are seldom reported for the same catalyst charge.

Without the skill

Methanol synthesis from CO2 is a game-changing route and the catalyst is the heart of the process. The material was tested at 250 °C and 50 bar with a feed ratio H2/CO2 = 3. The yield was excellent. It reached 0.84. The pellets gave 0.61. Pressure drop was also much better for the new system, and activity loss after 100 h was lower. Therefore, the structured catalyst clearly outperforms the conventional catalyst on every axis. The reactor packing lost 6% activity, while the older bed lost 14%. Pressure drop values were 0.18 bar and 1.25 bar respectively. In summary, this breakthrough catalyst will transform methanol production, highlighting its robust and vibrant performance. One feed composition was used in the tests.

With the skill

Methanol synthesis from CO2 and H2 runs over copper–zinc oxide catalysts, in structured and pellet charge forms. Yield and pressure drop are seldom reported for the same catalyst charge, and the charge form that holds yield at low pressure drop is the missing comparison. A structured catalyst and a pellet catalyst of the same composition were tested at 250 °C and 50 bar with a feed ratio H2/CO2 = 3. Space-time yield, the mass of methanol produced per unit catalyst volume per hour, for the structured catalyst was 0.84 kg L−1 h−1 at a pressure drop of 0.18 bar (pellet catalyst: 0.61 kg L−1 h−1 at 1.25 bar), and activity loss after 100 h was 6% for the structured catalyst against 14% for the pellet catalyst. At the tested feed composition, with no sulfur-bearing feed tested, the structured catalyst is the higher-yield, lower-pressure-drop form of the two charges.

Differences: the with-skill abstract follows the setting, gap, method, result, closing order; values stand with their comparators in one sentence, with the test conditions in the sentence before; one name is kept for each catalyst; no connective announces the conclusion; the closing claim is held to the tested conditions and states its limits. In the without-skill version, values stand separated from comparators, names cycle (catalyst, material, system, packing, bed), the activity result is restated, and the closing rises above the evidence.

Sample 2 (STEM) — Tutorial: heat-transfer coefficient from a cooling curve

Package load for the with-skill version: core + textbook reference.

Shared fact base. An estimate of the overall heat-transfer coefficient, denoted U (W m−2 K−1), comes from fitting a cooling model to measured liquid temperatures. A residual is a measured temperature minus the model temperature at the same time; the sum of squared residuals has units of K2. Standard error of an estimate is the spread of estimates the same fit would give across repeated measurement sets with the same measurement noise. Worked example: 6 measurements over 30 min; starting guess U = 420 W m−2 K−1 gives a sum of squared residuals of 18.6 K2; the fit gives U = 512 W m−2 K−1 with a standard error of 24 W m−2 K−1 and a sum of squared residuals of 2.1 K2; iterations stop when the relative change in the sum of squared residuals falls below 0.1%. Room temperature is held constant in the model, and no evaporation is admitted.

Without the skill

This section will walk through the fitting procedure. A model is fitted to the temperatures. The fit adjusts the coefficient. It reports uncertainty. Six measurements were taken. They covered 30 minutes. The starting guess was 420. The residuals were 18.6. The fit then gave 512, and the uncertainty was 24. The residuals fell to 2.1. Therefore the fit is good. Iterations stop at a relative change below 0.1%. The fitted value of 512 shows that the procedure works well. Room temperature is held constant in the model, and evaporation is not included.

With the skill

Your goal is an estimate of the overall heat-transfer coefficient U from a measured cooling curve: at the end of the fit you hold U = 512 W m−2 K−1 in place of the starting guess of 420 W m−2 K−1. You record the liquid temperature at stated times and adjust U until the sum of squared residuals is smallest; a residual is a measured temperature minus the model temperature at the same time. U is reported with its standard error, the spread of estimates the same fit would give across repeated measurement sets with the same measurement noise. In the worked example, 6 measurements over 30 min give U = 512 W m−2 K−1 with a standard error of 24 W m−2 K−1 and a sum of squared residuals of 2.1 K2 (starting guess U = 420 W m−2 K−1: 18.6 K2). Reduction of the sum of squared residuals from 18.6 K2 to 2.1 K2 is the check that the fitted curve tracks the measured curve; iterations stop when the relative change in the sum of squared residuals falls below 0.1%. Model conditions bound the estimate: room temperature constant, and no evaporation.

Differences: the with-skill tutorial states the goal and the outcome in its opening sentences, addresses the reader, defines the standard error at first use, and states the starting-guess baseline inside the sentence that reports the fitted result. In the without-skill version, the procedure is announced in place of a stated goal, uncertainty stays undefined, the baseline and the fitted value stand in separate fragments, and the closing restates the result.

Sample 3 (STEM) — Equation passage: Reynolds number and flow regime in a pipe

Package load for the with-skill version: core + equations reference + textbook reference.

Shared fact base and notation for this sample. Re (dimensionless), the Reynolds number, is computed from the fluid density ρ (kg m−3), the mean velocity v (m s−1), the pipe diameter D (m), and the dynamic viscosity μ (Pa s). Flow is laminar below Re = 2,300 and turbulent above Re = 4,000; between the two values the regime is transitional and is not assigned by the number alone. Worked values: water in a pipe of diameter 0.05 m at a mean velocity of 1.2 m s−1 gives Re = 60,000.

Without the skill

The Reynolds number is given by

Re = ρ v D / μ

Re is the Reynolds number. ρ is density. v is velocity. D is diameter. μ is viscosity. Therefore, a big Re means turbulence. For water in a 0.05 m pipe at 1.2 m s−1, Re = 60,000, so the flow state is turbulent. The flow condition changes with the number: the regime is laminar under 2,300 and turbulent over 4,000, with a transition zone in between.

With the skill

Reynolds number Re (dimensionless), fluid density ρ (kg m−3), mean velocity v (m s−1), pipe diameter D (m), and dynamic viscosity μ (Pa s) are related by

Re = ρ v D / μ

Flow is laminar below Re = 2,300 and turbulent above Re = 4,000; between the two values the regime is transitional and is not assigned by the number alone. For water in a pipe of diameter 0.05 m at a mean velocity of 1.2 m s−1, Re = 60,000, and the flow is in the turbulent range as bounded in the preceding sentence.

Differences: the with-skill passage defines all symbols in the set-up sentence, in symbol order, before the display, and places the boundary values in the sentence immediately after the display. In the without-skill passage, symbols are defined one per sentence after the display, three names cycle for the regime (flow state, flow condition, regime), and the boundary values wait for the final sentence.

Spare-part lead times are unchanged by the sensors. Responsibility for running the pilot and reporting before the March capital review sits with the reliability engineering group.

Without the skill

This game-changing programme is clearly the best available option, and leadership should seize it. The recommendation is to approve a three-month pilot on 18 pumps, run by the reliability engineering group, before the March capital review.

With the skill

214 pumps run at the plant, and bearing failures account for 46% of the downtime hours in the plant's maintenance records; the question for the capital review is whether detection before failure is worth a plant-wide sensor installation. Differences: the with-skill memo performs the opening frame without labels, states the recommendation before the evidence with its owner class, names the evidence base with its count where the finding is stated, states the trade-off in one sentence with both figures, and holds the closing at the opening strength. In the without-skill memo, the frame is labelled, the yearly charge stands separated from the installation figure, the vendor figure is presented as proof, and the recommendation sits in the final sentence after a stronger closing claim.

Sample 5 (STEM/business mix) — Carbon capture retrofit at a cement kiln: technical note and investment recommendation

Package load for the with-skill version: core + business reference (business sections).

Shared fact base. Kiln emissions are 780 kilotonnes (kt) of CO2 per year. Beyond 60 days, the effect of kiln dust on the solvent is untested. Full-scale cost: capital $210 million, operating cost $38 million per year, capture cost $64 per tonne. At an assumed carbon price of $70 per tonne (a scenario value, not a forecast), the payback is 9.6 years. Ownership is split: the process engineering group owns the design; the finance group owns the investment case.

Without the skill

Therefore the technology is proven at scale. The kiln emits 780 kt per year, so the retrofit transforms the plant's footprint. On the money side, the project costs $210 million. Operating costs are $38 million per year. The capture cost works out at $64 per tonne and payback is under a decade. Carbon is priced at $70. Experts agree this is the right moment to act. With the skill

Technical note. The recommendation is limited to a study and a longer pilot, decided on solvent behaviour beyond 60 days.

Differences: the with-skill version classifies by section. In the technical note, the pilot result stands with its scale and duration in the claim sentence, the untested item is named, and no full-scale rate is stated. Recommendation order in the investment section puts the recommendation before the capital and operating cost figures; both owner classes are named, and the carbon price stands as a labelled scenario value in the payback sentence. Promotional wording in the opening sentence, a carbon price separated from the payback, a vague attribution, and a recommendation deferred to the final sentence mark the without-skill version.

Sample 6 (STEM) — Research abstract: pervaporation membranes for ethanol dehydration

Package load for the with-skill version: core only.

Flux is the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour (kg m−2 h−1). The separation factor is the water-to-ethanol ratio in the permeate divided by the water-to-ethanol ratio in the feed (dimensionless). Feed: 85 wt% ethanol in water, at 60 °C. Polymer membrane: flux 1.9 kg m−2 h−1, separation factor 180, flux fell 11% over 500 h. Zeolite membrane: flux 1.2 kg m−2 h−1, separation factor 420, flux unchanged over 500 h within measurement error. One feed composition was tested; no test ran beyond 500 h; no fouling feed was tested. Seldom are flux, selectivity, and stability reported together for the same membrane.

Sample 7 (STEM) — Tutorial: vapour pressure from the Antoine equation

Package load for the with-skill version: core + textbook reference + equations reference.

Shared fact base. An estimate of the vapour pressure of ethanol at a stated temperature is the goal. Vapour pressure is the pressure at which a liquid and its vapour stand in equilibrium at that temperature. The Antoine equation relates vapour pressure P (mmHg) to temperature T (°C): log10(P) = A − B/(T + C), where A, B, C are constants for the liquid (dimensionless, dimensionless, °C). Ethanol constants: A = 8.20417, B = 1642.89, C = 230.300, holding from −57 °C to 80 °C. Worked example at T = 60 °C: T + C = 290.300 °C; B/(T + C) = 5.659; log10(P) = 2.545; P = 351 mmHg (0.468 bar). Check at T = 78.4 °C: P = 762 mmHg, against 760 mmHg for boiling at atmospheric pressure. As an empirical fit, the equation's constants do not apply outside the stated range.

Without the skill

Here A, B and C are constants for the liquid: A and B are dimensionless, and C is in degrees Celsius. For ethanol, A = 8.20417, B = 1642.89 and C = 230.300, and the constants hold from −57 °C to 80 °C. In the worked example at T = 60 °C, T + C = 290.300 °C and B/(T + C) = 5.659, so log10(P) = 2.545 and P = 351 mmHg, or 0.468 bar. A check at the normal boiling point, T = 78.4 °C, gives P = 762 mmHg from the equation, against 760 mmHg for boiling at atmospheric pressure. The Antoine equation is an empirical fit, and outside the stated temperature range the constants do not apply.

With the skill

An estimate of the vapour pressure of ethanol at a stated temperature comes from the Antoine equation: at 60 °C, P = 351 millimetres of mercury (mmHg), equal to 0.468 bar. Vapour pressure is the pressure at which a liquid and its vapour stand in equilibrium at that temperature. Calculation uses the Antoine equation, log10(P) = A − B/(T + C), relating vapour pressure P (millimetres of mercury, mmHg) to temperature T (°C) through constants A (dimensionless), B (dimensionless), and C (°C) fitted for the liquid. In the without-skill version, the opening announces the passage itself, the outcome appears only inside the worked example, and the boiling-point calculation is named a check, but no sentence states what the check shows; the two values stand side by side with no interpretation.

Sample 8 (STEM) — Equation passage: Fick's first law of diffusion

Package load for the with-skill version: core + equations reference + textbook reference.

Shared fact base and notation for this sample. The diffusive flux J (mol m−2 s−1) through a stagnant, dilute medium is computed from the diffusivity D (m2 s−1) and the concentration gradient dC/dx; C is concentration (mol m−3) and x is position (m). Fick's first law: J = −D dC/dx. A negative sign in the law states that diffusion runs from high concentration to low concentration. Worked values: oxygen in water, D = 2.1 × 10−9 m2 s−1; concentration falls by 0.25 mol m−3 across a film 0.5 mm thick, giving dC/dx = −500 mol m−4 and J = 1.05 × 10−6 mol m−2 s−1. Scope: a stagnant medium; a moving medium adds convection, which the equation does not carry.

Without the skill

Diffusion moves a species from regions of high concentration to regions of low concentration, and Fick's first law gives the rate of that movement through a stagnant, dilute medium. The diffusive flux J (moles per square metre per second, mol m−2 s−1) is computed from the diffusivity D (square metres per second, m2 s−1) and the concentration gradient dC/dx, where C is concentration (moles per cubic metre, mol m−3) and x is position (metres, m). The law is:

J = −D dC/dx

The negative sign states the direction of the process: diffusion runs from high concentration to low concentration. Where concentration falls as position increases, the gradient is negative and the flux comes out positive.

Oxygen in water provides a worked example. The diffusivity is D = 2.1 × 10−9 m2 s−1, and the concentration falls by 0.25 mol m−3 across a film 0.5 mm thick, so dC/dx = −500 mol m−4. Substitution into the law gives J = 1.05 × 10−6 mol m−2 s−1.

A scope condition closes the passage. The law as stated covers a stagnant medium; a moving medium adds convection, which the equation does not carry.

With the skill

Fick's first law computes the diffusive flux J (mol m−2 s−1) of a species through a stagnant, dilute medium from the diffusivity D (m2 s−1) and the concentration gradient dC/dx, where C is the concentration (mol m−3) and x is position (m):

J = −D dC/dx

A negative sign states the direction of transport: diffusion runs from high concentration to low concentration. Worked values for oxygen in water use D = 2.1 × 10−9 m2 s−1. Across a film 0.5 mm thick, a concentration difference of 0.25 mol m−3 gives dC/dx = −500 mol m−4, and substitution into the displayed relation gives J = 1.05 × 10−6 mol m−2 s−1. Fick's law holds for a stagnant medium; in a moving medium, convection adds a term to the flux that is not included in the equation.

Differences: the with-skill passage defines every symbol in the set-up sentence, in symbol order, before the display, and closes by stating the conditions under which the law holds. In the without-skill version, the opening sentence states no quantity, a bare lead-in introduces the display, and the closing sentences announce their own move ('A scope condition closes the passage') before stating the condition, and state the moving-medium case with 'carry'.

First-order decay in a batch reactor

A batch reactor holds a reacting liquid while reaction A → products proceeds. The rate of disappearance of A is r = kC, where r is the rate of reaction (mol L−1 min−1), k is the rate constant (min−1), and C is the concentration of A (mol L−1). Four assumptions precede any relation: the volume is constant, the temperature is constant, the kinetics are first-order, and no reverse reaction occurs.

At constant volume, the design relation for the batch reactor is

dC/dt = −kC

The left side gives the rate of change of concentration with time, and the right side states that A disappears in proportion to the concentration. Separate the variables by dividing both sides by C and multiplying by dt, which gives dC/C = −k dt. Integrate from C0 at t = 0 to C at time t. The integrated form is

ln(C0/C) = kt

where C0 is the initial concentration of A (mol L−1) and t is time (min). Conversion connects concentration to progress: X = 1 − C/C0, so C = C0(1 − X) and the integrated form can be solved for the time at any conversion.
