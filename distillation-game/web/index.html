Theory

Introduction

This page operates a distillation column separating a liquid feed of n-pentane, n-hexane, and n-heptane. Pentane is the light key (the component concentrated overhead), heptane is the heavy key (the component concentrated in the bottoms), and hexane is the middle component. The column has 12 trays (a tray is one equilibrium stage: liquid and vapour leaving it are taken to be in equilibrium), the feed enters on tray 6, and the nominal feed rate F (molar feed flow, in mol/s) is 10 mol/s with feed mole fractions 0.40 pentane, 0.30 hexane, 0.30 heptane. The nominal column pressure P (absolute pressure, in kPa) is 150 kPa; the playable range is 100 kPa to 300 kPa.

The separation matters beyond this game because distillation is the default industrial method for separating liquid mixtures whose components boil at different temperatures, and its operating cost is dominated by the heat supplied at the reboiler (the vessel that boils the column bottoms) and removed at the condenser (the exchanger that condenses the overhead vapour). Every control action in the game — opening a valve, warming the cooling water, raising the pressure — moves product purity and energy use together, through the relations set out below.

Key points the game demonstrates:

  • Separation per tray is set by the relative volatility, which falls as pressure rises, so the same column separates less well at high pressure (see Relative volatility; try it in the Pressure control unit).
  • A drum level is controlled by the sum of its outflows; the separation is controlled by their split (see Reflux ratio, level, and split; try it in the Reflux, distillate, and bottoms unit).
  • The condenser in this plant is also the pressure-control device: how flooded it runs sets how much condensing area is exposed (see Flooded-condenser pressure control; try it in the Condenser unit).
  • Condensate reaches the drum only if the condenser-to-drum pressure difference beats the riser lift head, and only without flashing if the liquid is subcooled (see Riser lift; try it in the Riser and reflux drum unit).
  • Two valves in series behave in two different ways: the condenser pair are passive throttles sharing one pressure drop, while the steam regulator holds a fixed pressure upstream of the steam valve, so the chest pressure follows the balance of steam in and steam condensed (see Valve law; try it in the Valves unit).
  • Boilup is bought with steam: the reboiler duty follows the steam chest saturation temperature, which follows the chest pressure (see Boilup and the steam chest).

Each key point is demonstrated in the Game panel by the change named in its Theory section, and practised in isolation in the Units panel, where each exercise loads a preset state into the same game. The Process panel walks the plant unit by unit in flow order. All quantities are in SI units; pressures are absolute unless a pressure difference is stated.

Vapour–liquid equilibrium

On each tray, liquid of mole fractions xi (mole fraction of component i in the liquid, dimensionless) sits in contact with vapour of mole fractions yi (mole fraction of component i in the vapour, dimensionless). The model treats the liquid as an ideal mixture, so Raoult's law applies: the partial pressure of component i in the vapour is its liquid mole fraction times its pure-component vapour pressure Pisat(T) (the pressure at which pure i boils at temperature T, in kPa, with T in K). Summing over the three components gives the tray pressure:

P = Σi xi Pisat(T)

and the vapour composition follows term by term:

yi = xi Pisat(T) / P

The equilibrium ratio Ki (dimensionless) collects the same relation:

Ki = yi / xi = Pisat(T) / P

Pentane has the highest vapour pressure at any temperature in the operating range, so K for pentane is the largest of the three and the vapour leaving a tray is always richer in pentane than the liquid it left. That enrichment, repeated tray by tray, is the whole mechanism of the separation [3].

In the game: watch the tray composition bands on the drawing. The top tray liquid at the nominal point has pentane mole fraction 0.911; the reboiler liquid has 0.0215. The gradient between them is built by the per-tray enrichment above.

Antoine relation and bubble point

The pure-component vapour pressure is computed from the Antoine relation, with constants A, B, and C fitted per component (dimensionless, in K, and in K respectively, for the form used here):

ln(Pisat / kPa) = Ai − Bi / (T + Ci)

The primary constants are the appendix values recorded in the model source [2]; an independent constant set from the NIST Chemistry WebBook [1] is carried in the model as a cross-check and agrees with the primary set to within 0.53 K on the pure boiling points at 101.325 kPa (model values 309.73 K, 342.42 K, and 372.08 K for pentane, hexane, and heptane; NIST averages 309.2 K, 341.9 K, and 371.6 K).

A liquid mixture boils when the Raoult sum reaches the imposed pressure. The bubble point (the temperature at which the first vapour bubble forms from a liquid of stated composition, in K) is the temperature solving

Σi xi Pisat(T) = P

Each tray temperature in the game is the bubble point of that tray's liquid at the column pressure. For the feed composition (0.40, 0.30, 0.30) the bubble point is 328.48 K at 100 kPa, 341.92 K at 150 kPa, and 367.81 K at 300 kPa, and the first vapour formed at 150 kPa has mole fractions (0.724, 0.200, 0.076): one equilibrium contact already raises the pentane fraction from 0.40 to 0.72.

Relative volatility and pressure

The relative volatility of component i to component j, αij (dimensionless), is the ratio of their equilibrium ratios, and for an ideal mixture the pressure cancels:

αij = Ki / Kj = Pisat(T) / Pjsat(T)

It still depends on temperature, and the tray temperature rises with pressure, so relative volatility falls as pressure rises. At the feed bubble point:

Enrichment compounds per stage. At total reflux (all condensate returned, no products drawn), the pentane-to-hexane proportion in the liquid is multiplied by about α at each stage, compounding to αN over N stages, where N is the number of stages (dimensionless) [3]. For N = 12 and the pentane/hexane pair, α12 falls from 3.27 × 105 at 100 kPa to 5.33 × 104 at 300 kPa — a factor of 0.163. The operating column draws products, so its achieved split is smaller, but it moves the same way: in the model, at fixed flows, raising the pressure from 100 kPa to 300 kPa moves the distillate pentane mole fraction from 0.977 to 0.945.

In the game: raise the column pressure with the condenser inlet valve and watch the distillate pentane reading fall out of specification (at least 0.95) even though no flow setpoint changed. The Pressure control exercise practises recovering from this.

Stages and constant molal overflow

Liquid flows down the column and vapour flows up. Above the feed tray (the rectifying section) the flows are the reflux flow LR (liquid molar flow, in mol/s) and the vapour flow VR (in mol/s). Below the feed tray (the stripping section) they are LS and VS (both in mol/s). The distillate flow is D (in mol/s) and the bottoms flow is B (in mol/s). With a total condenser (all overhead vapour condensed, no vapour product) and a feed that enters with vapour fraction ψ (dimensionless; 0 for a liquid feed, 1 for a vapour feed), the section balances are [3]:

VR = LR + D
LS = LR + (1 − ψ) F
VS = VR − ψ F
B = F − D

At the nominal point (ψ = 0): LR = 5 mol/s, D = 4 mol/s, VR = 9 mol/s, LS = 15 mol/s, VS = 9 mol/s, B = 6 mol/s. The vapour flow VS is the boilup: the vapour generated in the reboiler.

The model assumes constant molal overflow: the molar liquid and vapour flows are constant within each section, which follows if every component has the same molar latent heat and sensible-heat effects are neglected. The assumption is stated here because it is an approximation for this mixture: the molar latent heats at the normal boiling points are 25.79 kJ/mol (pentane), 28.85 kJ/mol (hexane), and 31.7 kJ/mol (heptane) [1], a spread of about 20 percent, so the real flows would drift somewhat from tray to tray. The model also takes every stage to be an equilibrium stage, the column pressure to be uniform (no pressure drop per tray), and each tray to hold a constant 200 mol of liquid. Tray temperatures then run from 366.12 K at the reboiler to 323.67 K at the top tray at the nominal point, falling monotonically as the liquid becomes richer in pentane.

Reflux ratio, level, and split

The reflux ratio R (dimensionless) is the split of drum liquid between return to the column and product:

R = LR / D

At the nominal point R = 5 / 4 = 1.25. Raising the reflux at fixed boilup enriches the top stages further (more liquid washing down against the rising vapour) and lowers the distillate rate.

The drum level obeys a separate balance. With Mdrum the drum holdup (in mol), t time (in s), and Cdrain the condensate flow arriving from the riser (in mol/s):

dMdrum/dt = Cdrain − LR − D

Only the sum LR + D appears: the level cannot tell the two outflows apart. One operator action (say, the reflux valve) can therefore be assigned to the level and the other (the distillate valve) sets the split — or the reverse — but a single valve cannot hold the level and set the ratio at once. This is the most common beginner error in the game: chasing purity with the reflux valve while the drum quietly drains or fills.

In the game: the split exercise starts with the reflux valve opened to 75 percent. The drum level falls while the distillate pentane rises; restore the level with the distillate valve only.

Boilup and the steam chest

The reboiler is heated by steam condensing in a chest at pressure Pchest (in kPa). The steam condenses at its saturation temperature Tsteam (in K), which the model computes from an integrated Clausius–Clapeyron relation anchored at 373.15 K and 101.325 kPa with a molar latent heat of steam of 40.7 kJ/mol; it gives 416.77 K at 400 kPa and 424.84 K at 500 kPa, within about 1 K of the steam tables over the game's range. The reboiler duty Qreb (heat flow, in W) is

Qreb = Ureb Areb (Tsteam − Treb)

where Ureb is the overall heat transfer coefficient (in W/(m2 K)), Areb is the reboiler area (in m2), and Treb is the reboiler liquid temperature (in K). The model uses assumed design values Ureb = 900 W/(m2 K) and Areb = 12 m2. At the nominal point the chest pressure is 182.35 kPa, so Tsteam = 390.65 K against Treb = 366.12 K, and Qreb = 264.9 kW. The steam consumption follows from the latent heat: 6.51 mol/s of steam at the nominal point.

Two consequences matter in play. First, boilup responds to the chest pressure, not directly to a valve: the regulator and the steam flow valve set the chest pressure through the steam balance (see Valve law). Second, if the column pressure rises, Treb rises with it and the temperature difference shrinks, so the duty falls unless the chest pressure is raised — the plant partly regulates itself, which is also what caps the pressure escalation in the relief section.

Energy balance and condenser duty

The condenser duty Qcond (in W) is computed from the exposed area:

Qcond,max = U Aexp ΔTlm

where U is the overall coefficient (in W/(m2 K)), Aexp is the tube area not submerged by pooled condensate (in m2), and ΔTlm is the logarithmic mean of the two end temperature differences between the condensing side and the cooling water (in K). The water-side coefficient scales with the circulation rate to the power 0.8, the standard turbulent-flow form [3]; the model's reference value is 1392.9 W/(m2 K) at the reference circulation of 40 kg/s, and the total condenser area is 60 m2 (assumed design values). The condensation rate is the smaller of the vapour supply and the duty divided by the molar latent heat of the condensing vapour: at the nominal point the duty capacity is about 389 kW, the vapour supply limits first, and the realised duty is 239.7 kW.

The two duties at the nominal point, Qreb = 264.9 kW in and Qcond = 239.7 kW out, need not be equal: the feed enters and the products leave at different temperatures and compositions, condensate leaves subcooled, and the nominal point is the game's starting state, close to but not exactly a steady state of the integrated plant — at fixed nominal openings the plant relaxes slowly (the column pressure drifts down over about 15 minutes) and the operating point the player settles at is the one they hold with the valves.

In the game: halve the pump speed and watch Qcond and the column pressure. The duty falls through both U (circulation to the power 0.8) and, as the condenser floods in response, Aexp.

Valve law and series valves

No operator in this game sets a flow. The operator sets a valve opening u (in percent) and the flow follows from the pressure difference across the valve, ΔP (in kPa). For a liquid stream the model uses the square-root law of the standard sizing equations [6], with a sizing constant Kv (in mol/s per (kPa)1/2 at 100 percent opening, a stated design value per valve):

W = Kv (u / 100) √ΔP

where W is the molar flow (in mol/s) and only the positive part of ΔP drives flow. Doubling the opening doubles the flow at fixed ΔP; doubling the flow at fixed opening needs four times the pressure difference. This is why a valve that is nearly shut has strong control and a valve that is nearly open has almost none.

For a vapour stream the model uses the compressible form, with the downstream pressure floored at a fraction rc (dimensionless) of the upstream pressure:

W = Kv (u / 100) √(Pup2 − Pdn2) / (100 kPa)

with Pdn replaced by max(Pdn, rc Pup) inside the root. When the downstream pressure falls below rc times the upstream pressure, the flow no longer responds to the downstream pressure at all: the throat is choked. The model takes rc = 0.40 for hydrocarbon vapour valves and 0.35 for the steam valve (design values adopted in the model specification).

Two pairs of valves are in series on one stream, and they work differently. The condenser inlet and outlet valves are two passive throttles with the condenser between them: their pressure drops add to the total available drop, and the split between them shifts as either valve moves, so parking one fully open and throttling the other gives the cleaner control. The steam pair is not two passive throttles: the regulator holds the pressure upstream of the steam flow valve at its setpoint (400 kPa at the nominal point), and the steam valve throttles against that fixed upstream pressure. The chest pressure is then not a shared drop at all — it is set by the balance between the steam the valve admits and the steam condensed in the reboiler, and it rises when the valve is opened.

Valve sizes were set so that most valves sit at 50 percent opening at the nominal point, with travel in both directions. The exceptions are stated: the condenser inlet valve sits at 79.1 percent (its smaller sizing keeps the warm-cooling case operable) and the hot vapour bypass sits at 1 percent, as a pressure trim.

Flooded-condenser pressure control

The condenser sits below the reflux drum and is deliberately run partly flooded. The flooded fraction f (dimensionless) is the condenser liquid holdup Mc (in mol) over its capacity of 2000 mol:

f = Mc / 2000 mol

Tubes below the pooled liquid condense almost no vapour — they subcool the pool instead — so the exposed area is Aexp = A (1 − f) with A = 60 m2. More flooding means less condensing area, a lower condensation rate, and a rising column pressure: the liquid level in the condenser is the pressure-control handle. At the nominal point f = 0.40 (800 mol pooled) and the pool temperature is 318.39 K, subcooled below the condensing temperature of 321.98 K toward the entering cooling-water temperature in proportion to f. Flooded-condenser control, including the hot vapour bypass variant used here, is a standard industrial arrangement with known failure modes [4, 5].

The bypass valve admits hot vapour directly into the drum vapour space. That raises the drum pressure toward the condenser pressure, which reduces the pressure difference that lifts condensate up the riser (next section), backs liquid up into the condenser, and so raises the flooding. The outlet valve acts from the other side: it throttles the drainage directly. Between them, the pair sets both the flooding and the drum feed rate, which is why the autopilot and the player both work the overhead as a system rather than as single valves.

Riser lift, subcooling, and flashing

Condensate leaves the condenser through a riser 1.5 m tall (the vertical pipe from the condenser up to the drum, in m). Lifting a liquid of density ρ (in kg/m3; the model uses 580 kg/m3) through a height h (in m) against gravitational acceleration g = 9.80665 m/s2 costs a pressure head:

ΔPhead = ρ g h = 8.53 kPa

The riser driving pressure ΔPdrive (in kPa) is what remains after paying it, with Pcond the condenser pressure (in kPa), Pdrum the drum pressure (in kPa), and ΔPpool the head of liquid pooled in the condenser itself (in kPa; 2.28 kPa at the nominal flooded fraction):

ΔPdrive = (Pcond − Pdrum) + ΔPpool − ΔPhead

The riser flow then follows the liquid valve law through the outlet valve. At the nominal point: (148 − 140) + 2.28 − 8.53 = 1.74 kPa of drive, which is why the outlet valve is large and why small pressure changes move the drainage a lot.

The second condition is thermal. The subcooling margin ΔTsub (in K) is the bubble point of the pooled liquid at the drum pressure minus the pool temperature:

ΔTsub = Tbubble(Pdrum) − Tpool

At the nominal point the margin is +1.84 K. If the margin goes negative, the liquid is above its bubble point at the lower drum pressure and part of it flashes to vapour in the riser; the model flashes a fraction min(0.30, cp (−ΔTsub) / λ), with cp the liquid molar heat capacity (170 J/(mol K), assumed) and λ the molar latent heat (in J/mol), and the flashed vapour binds the riser so the delivered liquid falls further. A warm condenser (little subcooling) and a wide-open bypass (small pressure difference) together are the usual route into this state.

Hydraulic limits: what is and is not modelled

Two terms need care, because the game uses one of them in a specific sense. Flooding in this game means condenser flooding (previous section): a modelled, useful operating state, not a fault. Tray flooding in the industrial sense — liquid backed up the column because the downcomers cannot pass it, with a sharp rise in pressure drop [3, 4] — is not in the model: tray pressure drop, downcomer hydraulics, and entrainment are not computed, and the column pressure is uniform across the trays.

What the tray display does show is the liquid depth on each tray, drawn from the liquid rate through a weir-type relation (depth proportional to the liquid rate to the power 2/3, as for flow over a rectangular weir [3]), lagged so changes settle over about 20 s, and a foam band that appears when the vapour rate exceeds 1.25 times its nominal value. At zero liquid rate the drawn depth goes to zero: the tray runs dry, which is the display counterpart of weeping (liquid passing straight through the tray instead of across it). Neither the depth nor the foam feeds back into the separation calculation; they are instruments, not physics, and the separation responds only through the flows and pressure in the relations above.

Pressure escalation and relief

Column pressure rises when vapour is generated faster than it is condensed or drawn off. The protection sequence is fixed: a high-pressure alarm at 360 kPa, a relief valve that lifts at its set pressure of 400 kPa, a design pressure of 500 kPa, and a rupture pressure of 600 kPa. The relief valve is choked at its throat, so its capacity grows in proportion to the upstream pressure; it reaches its stated capacity of 25 mol/s at the design pressure, along a 25 kPa lift ramp above the set pressure.

With the relief path available, rupture is not reachable in this plant: the maximum credible vapour generation (full 900 kPa steam supply, no cooling) is about 17 mol/s, below the relief capacity, because as the pressure rises the reboiler temperature rises and closes the steam-side temperature difference (see Boilup). Rupture requires the relief path to be isolated, which occurs only in the fault scenario. A pump trip (cooling circulation lost) is the normal upset: pressure rises, the relief lifts (peak 403 kPa in the model test), and the pressure is held below the design pressure with inventory lost to the flare. Relief lifting is therefore a heavy penalty in play but not the end of the round; rupture ends it.

What the operator controls

The plant state is carried by the tray compositions, the drum and condenser pool compositions, four liquid holdups (condenser, drum, reboiler, and stripping pool), four pressures, and two temperatures (the drum temperature and the cooling-tank temperature). Against that state, the operator sets eleven openings — condenser inlet, hot vapour bypass, condenser outlet, reflux, distillate, bottoms, steam regulator setpoint, steam flow valve, cooling pump speed, makeup valve, and the feed valve — plus the feed flow setpoint and its automatic/manual mode. The feed supply composition, temperature, and pressure and the cooling supply temperature are scenario quantities: the player can change them in practice, and the disturbances change them without asking.

The count matters because it is smaller than the list of things that must be held: two product compositions, two levels, the column pressure, the condenser flooding, and the riser margin, with the steam and cooling costs to be kept down at the same time. The pairings that work are the ones the balances allow — level to a sum of flows, split to their ratio, pressure to the condensation rate, boilup to the chest pressure — and the autopilot levels in the game are built from exactly these pairings.

Conclusion

The game's behaviour follows from a small set of relations: Raoult's law and the Antoine relation set what each tray can do; relative volatility, falling with pressure, sets how much the 12 trays achieve; the section balances and the reflux split set the products; the valve law turns openings into flows; the flooded condenser and the riser couple the overhead pressure, level, and drainage into one system; and the steam chest turns a pressure into boilup and duty. A player who holds the plant well is, in effect, solving these relations continuously by hand. The discussion point the game leaves open is the one stated in the hydraulics section: real columns also fail by tray flooding, entrainment, and maldistribution, which this model does not compute, so an operating policy that is safe here is necessary but not sufficient on a real unit.

Feed supply and feed valve

Enters: feed liquid from the supply at 250 kPa and its bubble point at the column pressure, 341.92 K, composition 0.40 / 0.30 / 0.30. Inside: the feed valve makes the flow from the supply-to-column pressure difference (100 kPa at the nominal point) by the liquid valve law; on automatic, a controller moves the opening, with a lag of about 4 s, to hold the flow setpoint of 10 mol/s, and the effective opening is 50 percent. Leaves: 10.00 mol/s toward the feed tray.

Feed tray entry

Enters: the feed stream at the supply pressure. Inside: entering tray 6, the feed is let down to the column pressure at constant enthalpy (an isenthalpic flash): a feed above its bubble point at the column pressure partly vaporises, and the vapour fraction at entry, ψ, is shown on the drawing and in the readings. The nominal feed is a saturated liquid, so ψ = 0. Leaves: the liquid part joins the liquid flowing down from tray 7 (raising LS to 15 mol/s); the vapour part joins the vapour rising to tray 7 (reducing the boilup needed below the feed tray by ψF).

Trays: rectifying and stripping

Enters: vapour from below and liquid from above on every tray; reflux (5.00 mol/s) on the top tray; boilup vapour (9.00 mol/s) from the reboiler below tray 1. Inside: vapour and liquid leave each tray in equilibrium at the tray's bubble point. Going up, each tray's liquid is richer in pentane than the one below: 0.052 on tray 1, 0.383 on the feed tray, 0.911 on tray 12, while the temperature falls from 360.64 K on tray 1 to 323.67 K on tray 12. The six trays above the feed tray (rectifying) wash heptane out of the rising vapour with reflux; the feed tray and the five below it (stripping) strip pentane out of the falling liquid with boilup. Leaves: overhead vapour at 9.00 mol/s from the top tray; liquid at 15.00 mol/s from tray 1 into the reboiler pool.

Overhead vapour line and branch

Enters: overhead vapour, 9.00 mol/s at the column pressure. Inside: the line branches. The main path runs through the condenser inlet valve (79.1 percent open) into the condenser: 8.95 mol/s at the nominal point. The branch runs through the hot vapour bypass valve (1 percent open) straight into the drum vapour space: 0.05 mol/s, which condenses in the drum and holds the drum pressure up toward the condenser pressure. A relief branch leaves the same line (see Relief path). Leaves: vapour to the condenser and a small bypass flow to the drum.

Condenser

Enters: vapour through the inlet valve (8.95 mol/s), cooling water from the surge tank at 307.00 K. Inside: vapour condenses on the exposed tubes at 321.98 K. The vessel runs 40 percent flooded: 800 mol of condensate pooled over the lower tubes, which subcool the pool to 318.39 K instead of condensing. The realised duty is 239.7 kW; the cooling water leaves at 309.86 K. Leaves: pooled condensate toward the outlet valve and riser; warmed cooling water back to the surge tank.

Riser

Enters: condensate through the condenser outlet valve (50 percent open). Inside: the liquid climbs 1.5 m to the drum against a lift head of 8.53 kPa, paid from the condenser-to-drum difference of 8.0 kPa plus the pooled-liquid head of 2.28 kPa, leaving 1.74 kPa of drive. The pool is subcooled by a margin of +1.84 K at the drum pressure, so nothing flashes on the way up. Leaves: 8.95 mol/s of liquid into the reflux drum.

Reflux drum

Enters: condensate from the riser (8.95 mol/s) and bypass vapour (0.05 mol/s, which condenses here). Inside: the drum is the inventory buffer of the overhead system: 1500 mol of liquid (50 percent level) at 140.0 kPa and 320.22 K, composition equal to the distillate composition. Its level moves with the difference between what arrives and the sum of the two outflows. Leaves: reflux and distillate, below.

Reflux and distillate

Enters: drum liquid. Inside: two valves split it, both 50 percent open at the nominal point, driven by the drum pressure plus the liquid head above their outlets. Leaves: reflux, 5.00 mol/s down to the top tray; distillate, 4.00 mol/s out as product, meeting the specification pentane mole fraction 0.968 (at least 0.95 required).

Reboiler and steam chest

Enters: liquid from tray 1 (15.00 mol/s) into the reboiler pool (1000 mol, 25 percent of its 4000 mol capacity, at 366.12 K); steam from the 900 kPa supply header through the regulator (set 400 kPa) and the steam flow valve (50 percent open) into the chest at 182.35 kPa. Inside: steam condenses at 390.65 K and delivers 264.9 kW; part of the pool boils off as boilup with the equilibrium vapour composition of the pool liquid. Leaves: boilup vapour, 9.00 mol/s, up to tray 1; steam condensate (the game does not track the steam-side condensate beyond the chest); bottoms, below.

Bottoms

Enters: reboiler pool liquid. Inside: the bottoms valve (50 percent open) lets the pool liquid out against the downstream pressure, driven by the column pressure plus the pool head; together with the boilup it sets the reboiler level. Leaves: bottoms product, 6.00 mol/s, composition 0.022 / 0.479 / 0.500, meeting the specification pentane mole fraction 0.022 (at most 0.05 required).

Cooling loop

Enters: warm water returning from the condenser at 309.86 K into the surge tank (2000 kg of water, well mixed, at 307.00 K). Inside: the circulating pump (half speed) moves 20 kg/s around the loop — tank to condenser and back — and the pump speed also sets the condenser water-side coefficient. The makeup valve (half open) admits 5 kg/s of cold supply water at 298 K; an equal flow of warm water overflows, which is how the loop rejects heat slowly. The tank also loses heat to the surroundings. Leaves: water at the tank temperature to the condenser; overflow to drain.

Relief path

Enters: overhead vapour, only when the column pressure passes the set pressure. Inside: the relief valve on the overhead line lifts at 400 kPa (alarm first at 360 kPa) and vents to the flare; its capacity reaches 25 mol/s at the design pressure of 500 kPa. Leaves: vapour to a safe location, with the inventory lost. The rupture pressure of 600 kPa is reached only if this path is isolated in the fault scenario.

VESSEL RUPTURE — the column pressure passed the rupture pressure of 600 kPa with the relief path isolated.

Units

How the unit panels work

Each unit below is one part of the plant, taken in the Process panel's order. The readings under each unit are the live state of that unit in the running game — they update as the game runs, on whichever panel is open. Each unit ends with an exercise: the button resets the game, loads the stated preset state, and takes you to the Game panel. The task, what to watch, and the success condition are stated in words; judge success from the readings and the drawing, as an operator would. There is no separate score for the exercises.

Steam chest and reboiler

Steam from the 900 kPa header passes the regulator (set 400 kPa) and the steam flow valve in series into the chest. The regulator holds the pressure upstream of the steam valve at its setpoint; the chest pressure is set by the balance between the steam the valve admits and the steam the reboiler condenses, and the duty follows the chest saturation temperature (Theory: Boilup and the steam chest; Process: Reboiler and steam chest).

Chest pressure— Steam flow— Reboiler duty— Boilup VS— Reboiler temperature— Reboiler level—

Exercise — restore the boilup. The preset closes the steam flow valve to 25 percent.

Task: bring the boilup back to about 9 mol/s and the distillate back into specification. Watch: the chest pressure as you open the valve — it rises, because the valve admits steam faster than the chest condenses it, until the higher saturation temperature raises the duty to match. Success: boilup steady near 9 mol/s with the chest pressure steady and both products in specification.

Valves

Every stream in the plant is made by a valve: the operator sets an opening, and the flow follows from the pressure difference — the square-root law for liquids, the compressible law with choking for vapours (Theory: Valve law and series valves). The table below is the live state of all eleven openings.

Exercise — one throttle at a time. The preset parks the steam flow valve fully open and sets the regulator to 250 kPa.

Task: first restore the boilup to about 9 mol/s using only the regulator; then set the regulator back to 400 kPa and do the same job using only the steam flow valve. Watch: the chest pressure as each control works in turn: with the valve parked open the regulator setpoint sets the chest pressure almost directly, while with the regulator fixed the valve opening sets how fast steam is admitted against that fixed upstream pressure. Success: boilup near 9 mol/s reached both ways, with the parked valve untouched in each half.

Feed valve and feed entry

The feed valve makes the feed flow from the supply-to-column pressure difference. On automatic it holds the flow setpoint; moving it takes it to manual, where the flow follows the difference. What the feed becomes on the tray depends on its state at the column pressure: the preset below uses a hot feed (Theory: Stages and constant molal overflow; Process: Feed tray entry).

Exercise — a flashing feed. The preset raises the feed temperature 10 K above its bubble point at the column pressure, so part of the feed enters as vapour.

Task: read the vapour fraction at entry from the readings. The boilup itself will not tell you the feed has flashed — it is set by the reboiler duty and stays where the steam chest puts it. Instead, use the section balances of the Theory panel to work out the new stripping liquid flow and, at unchanged boilup, the new rectifying vapour flow; then check both against the live readings in the Trays unit (the Game readings also show the vapour into the condenser). Watch: ψ, the stripping liquid flow falling below 15 mol/s, the vapour into the condenser rising above 9 mol/s, and the bottoms pentane drifting as the section traffic changes. Success: your two computed flows match the readings to one decimal place, and both products are back in specification within ten minutes of game time.

Condenser

The condenser condenses on its exposed tubes only; the pooled liquid over the submerged tubes subcools instead. Flooding is therefore a controlled variable, not a fault (Theory: Flooded-condenser pressure control; Process: Condenser).

Condenser pressure— Flooded fraction— Condensation rate— Condenser duty— Condensing temperature— Vapour in—

Exercise — a slow pump. The preset cuts the cooling-water pump to quarter speed.

Task: hold the column pressure below the 360 kPa alarm for five minutes of game time without the relief valve lifting. Watch: the duty first (it falls through the water-side coefficient), then the flooded fraction as condensate backs up, then the pressure. Success: pressure steady below the alarm with the pump still at quarter speed — the recovery must come from the overhead valves and the makeup, not from restoring the pump.

Riser and reflux drum

Condensate climbs 1.5 m from the condenser to the drum. The lift is paid from the condenser-to-drum pressure difference, which the bypass and outlet valves set between them, and the liquid must be subcooled or it flashes on the way up (Theory: Riser lift, subcooling, and flashing; Process: Riser, Reflux drum).

Exercise — back the condensate up. The preset opens the bypass to 12 percent and closes the outlet valve to 30 percent.

Task: restore the drainage to about 9 mol/s with the subcooling margin positive, changing only the bypass and outlet valves. Watch: the condenser flooded fraction rising while the drainage is throttled, and the drum level falling — the two inventories move in opposite directions. Success: drainage steady near 9 mol/s, margin above zero, drum level back between 40 and 60 percent.

Cooling loop

Water circulates tank → condenser → tank at a rate set by the pump speed, which also sets the condenser water-side coefficient. The makeup valve admits cold supply water and displaces warm water over the overflow: a slow, whole-loop cooling that costs water (Process: Cooling loop).

Tank temperature— Water leaving condenser— Pump speed— Makeup opening— Condenser duty—

Exercise — warm supply. The preset warms the cooling supply to 35 °C (308.15 K), the same change as the Game panel disturbance.

Task: keep the column pressure below the 360 kPa alarm for five minutes of game time. Watch: how slowly the tank temperature moves compared with the pressure — the makeup works on the whole 2000 kg of loop water. Success: pressure held below the alarm with no relief lift; note which of the pump and the makeup did the work, and how long each took to act.

Pressure control and relief

Pressure is controlled by the condensation rate: the inlet valve throttles vapour toward the condenser, the bypass and outlet valves set the flooding, and the cooling sets the duty (Theory: Flooded-condenser pressure control, Pressure escalation and relief). The protection sequence — alarm 360 kPa, relief 400 kPa, design 500 kPa, rupture 600 kPa — is marked on the pressure bar on the drawing.

Column pressure— Condenser pressure— Relief flow— High-pressure alarm— Distillate pentane—

Exercise — pressure costs purity. The preset closes the condenser inlet valve to 30 percent, throttling the vapour path so the column pressure climbs.

Task: let the pressure rise, note the distillate pentane as it crosses the 0.95 specification limit, then bring the pressure back under 200 kPa and the product back into specification. Watch: the relative-volatility effect of Theory § Relative volatility appearing in a product reading, with no flow setpoint changed. Success: pressure steady under 200 kPa and distillate pentane at or above 0.95, held for two minutes.

Reflux, distillate, and bottoms

The drum's two outlet valves share one job between them: their combined flow sets the drum level, their split sets the reflux ratio and so the separation. The bottoms valve does the same single job for the reboiler level, against the boilup (Theory: Reflux ratio, level, and split; Process: Reflux and distillate, Bottoms).

Watch: the distillate pentane improving as the reflux rises, while the distillate rate falls — purity is bought with product rate. Success: drum level steady in band, reflux valve untouched, both products in specification.
