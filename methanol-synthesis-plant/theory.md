## Process description

Methanol is manufactured industrially by the catalytic hydrogenation of carbon oxides contained in synthesis gas, a mixture of hydrogen (H2), carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO2), hereafter termed syngas. The two principal synthesis reactions are the hydrogenation of carbon monoxide and the hydrogenation of carbon dioxide, both carried out over a copper/zinc oxide/alumina catalyst [13][14]:

- carbon monoxide + 2 hydrogen ⇌ methanol (reaction enthalpy at standard conditions approximately −90.8 kilojoule per mole (kJ/mol), that is, exothermic [14]);
- carbon dioxide + 3 hydrogen ⇌ methanol + water (reaction enthalpy approximately −49.2 kJ/mol, exothermic [14]).

The two reactions are coupled by the reverse water-gas shift reaction, which interconverts carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide over the same catalyst: carbon dioxide + hydrogen ⇌ carbon monoxide + water (endothermic, approximately +41.2 kJ/mol [14]). Because the synthesis reactions reduce the number of gas moles, conversion is favoured by high pressure and low temperature, while acceptable reaction rates require elevated temperature; the industrial operating window is a compromise between equilibrium and kinetics [7][18]. Nearly all current world production is from fossil feedstocks, with natural gas supplying about 65 per cent and coal about 35 per cent of production, and renewable routes supplying under 1 per cent [1][14]. The abbreviation °C denotes degree Celsius, the unit of temperature; kPa denotes kilopascal and MPa denotes megapascal, units of pressure, with 1 MPa equal to 1,000 kPa and approximately equal to 10 bar; kg/h denotes kilogram per hour, the unit of mass flow rate; ppm denotes parts per million and ppb denotes parts per billion by volume.

In the plant of process flow diagram (PFD) PFD-104 the syngas is generated from natural gas by steam reforming followed by oxygen-blown autothermal reforming (ATR), a combination termed combined reforming, and the carbon oxide ratio of the gas is trimmed by addition of carbon dioxide before compression to synthesis pressure. The reference plant produces 65,000 kg/h of refined methanol. Stream values attributed to PFD-104 are the illustrative sample values of the drawing, recorded in the design basis [20]; the values are internally consistent and the overall balance closes at 102,000 kg/h of inputs against 102,000 kg/h of outputs, but the values do not constitute the result of a converged simulation.

The composition of methanol synthesis gas is characterised by the stoichiometric number, denoted $S_N$ and also termed the module. The symbol $n_{\mathrm{H_2}}$ denotes the molar amount of hydrogen in the gas, the symbol $n_{\mathrm{CO}}$ denotes the molar amount of carbon monoxide, and the symbol $n_{\mathrm{CO_2}}$ denotes the molar amount of carbon dioxide, each in mole. The stoichiometric number is defined as

$$S_N = (n_{\mathrm{H_2}} - n_{\mathrm{CO_2}}) / (n_{\mathrm{CO}} + n_{\mathrm{CO_2}})$$

A value of exactly 2.0 corresponds to the stoichiometric requirement of the two synthesis reactions taken together. For optimal operation of the methanol loop the stoichiometric number is maintained slightly above 2.0 [14]; the Lurgi MegaMethanol technology specifies an optimal synthesis gas characterised by a stoichiometric number between 2.0 and 2.1 [13]. Pure steam reforming of natural gas yields a hydrogen surplus and a stoichiometric number above the optimum, whereas autothermal reforming alone yields a substoichiometric gas; combined reforming, an appropriate split of feed between the two reformers, hydrogen recovery from purge gas, or carbon dioxide addition is therefore used to bring the gas to the target range [13]. A small carbon dioxide content in the synthesis gas is additionally beneficial: carbon dioxide co-feed in the range 0.5 to 5 per cent is reported to increase catalyst productivity [6], and about 6 per cent by volume of carbon dioxide is normally present in, or added to, the syngas feed in conventional practice [18].

PFD-104 is arranged in three sections [20]. Section A, feed preparation and syngas generation: natural gas feed (38,000 kg/h at 25 °C and 2,800 kPa) is compressed in the natural gas compressor C-401, preheated in the feed preheater E-401 to 380 °C, and mixed with steam (30,000 kg/h). The mixed feed (68,000 kg/h at 380 °C and 4,050 kPa) enters the primary reformer R-401 and leaves at 850 °C. The partially reformed gas passes to the autothermal reformer R-402, into which compressed oxygen (22,000 kg/h at 3,900 kPa from the oxygen compressor C-402) is injected; the autothermal reformer effluent (90,000 kg/h) leaves at 1,050 °C and 3,600 kPa. Heat is recovered in the waste-heat boiler E-402, which cools the gas to 300 °C, and in the syngas cooler E-403, which cools the gas to 40 °C. Process condensate (12,000 kg/h) is separated in the condensate separator V-401. Carbon dioxide feed (12,000 kg/h) is added to the separated gas (78,000 kg/h) to set the stoichiometric number, giving 90,000 kg/h of conditioned syngas. Section B, compression and synthesis loop: the synthesis gas compressor C-403 raises the syngas from 3,300 kPa to 7,500 kPa; the discharge is cooled in the compressor aftercooler E-404 to 45 °C and joins the recycle stream at the loop junction. The mixed loop feed (350,000 kg/h at 7,350 kPa) is preheated against converter effluent in the synthesis feed preheater E-405 to 220 °C and enters the methanol converter R-403. The converter effluent returns through E-405 and is finally cooled in the effluent cooler E-406 to 40 °C. Crude methanol is condensed and separated in the methanol separator V-402, yielding 74,000 kg/h of crude methanol liquid and 276,000 kg/h of vapour. A purge stream of 16,000 kg/h is withdrawn from the vapour to limit inert accumulation; the remaining 260,000 kg/h is returned by the recycle compressor C-404 at 7,450 kPa. Section C, purification: the crude methanol is let down in pressure by the crude methanol pump P-401 and purified in a two-column train operating near atmospheric pressure. The topping column T-401 removes light ends (4,000 kg/h); the topping bottoms (70,000 kg/h) feed the refining column T-402, from which refined methanol (65,000 kg/h) is withdrawn overhead and aqueous bottoms (5,000 kg/h) are discharged.

## Feed desulfurisation

Removal of sulfur compounds from the natural gas feed to the parts-per-billion level is required before reforming, because sulfur poisons both the nickel reforming catalyst and, with particular severity, the copper synthesis catalyst; for methanol syngas production the sulfur content of the hydrocarbon stream must be reduced to the parts per billion range [12]. Desulfurisation is not drawn as a discrete vessel on PFD-104 and is recorded here as the context unit that the feed system presupposes. Organic sulfur compounds (mercaptans, thiophenes, carbonyl sulfide) are first hydrogenated to hydrogen sulfide (H2S) over a cobalt-molybdenum or nickel-molybdenum catalyst using a small hydrogen stream, conventionally drawn from synthesis purge gas [12][13]. The hydrogen sulfide is then absorbed on a zinc oxide (ZnO) bed: hydrogen sulfide + zinc oxide → zinc sulfide + water, the reaction being effectively irreversible under bed conditions [12][13]. The desulfurisation reactor operates at approximately 350 to 380 °C [13]; a residual sulfur content of less than 0.1 ppm is obtainable and can be tolerated by the downstream processes [13]. The maximum allowable sulfur content of syngas fed to the synthesis loop is 0.1 ppm by volume expressed as hydrogen sulfide [18]. Zinc oxide is a consumable absorbent and bed change-out is scheduled on sulfur breakthrough; chloride guard beds, where fitted, are placed upstream of the zinc oxide bed because zinc oxide provides no protection of the copper catalyst against hydrogen chloride [18].

## Steam reforming

The primary reformer R-401 converts the greater part of the methane (CH4) in the mixed feed to hydrogen and carbon monoxide by the endothermic steam reforming reaction, supplying the hydrogen-rich fraction of the synthesis gas. The principal reactions, over a nickel catalyst on a refractory support contained in externally fired tubes, are [1][13]: methane + water ⇌ carbon monoxide + 3 hydrogen (steam reforming; strongly endothermic); and carbon monoxide + water ⇌ carbon dioxide + hydrogen (water-gas shift; mildly exothermic). Reforming processes for syngas generation from natural gas are high-temperature processes operating above 800 °C [1]. In PFD-104 the mixed feed enters at 380 °C and 4,050 kPa and the effluent leaves at 850 °C and 3,800 kPa [20]. In the combined reforming configuration the tubular steam reformer treats only the fraction of feed required to reach the target stoichiometric number; in the Lurgi MegaMethanol arrangement only about 30 per cent of the hydrocarbons are converted in the steam reformer, and the steam reformer is about one quarter of the size of a reformer in a wholly steam-reformed plant [13]. The tubular steam reformer is the most expensive single equipment item of a combined-reforming methanol plant, and the synthesis gas production section as a whole accounts for more than 50 per cent of the capital cost of the entire plant [13]. Tube wall temperature and creep life govern the outlet temperature; carbon formation on the nickel catalyst is controlled by the steam-to-carbon ratio and by feed desulfurisation; a low steam-to-carbon ratio reduces steam consumption and reformer size but increases the carbon-formation risk, and pre-reforming of feeds containing higher hydrocarbons permits operation at a steam-to-carbon ratio of about 1.8 [13].

## Autothermal reforming

The autothermal reformer R-402 completes the conversion of methane remaining in the primary reformer effluent. Heat for the endothermic reforming reactions is supplied within the same refractory-lined vessel by substoichiometric combustion of part of the feed with oxygen (O2), so that no external firing is required and the vessel can operate at higher pressure than a tubular reformer [13][15]. In the burner and combustion zone (no catalyst; turbulent diffusion flame) the reactions are methane + 2 oxygen → carbon dioxide + 2 water (complete combustion; highly exothermic) and methane + ½ oxygen → carbon monoxide + 2 hydrogen (partial oxidation; exothermic) [15]. In the catalyst bed (catalyst: nickel-based reforming catalyst) the remaining methane is converted by the steam reforming and water-gas shift reactions, and soot precursors formed in the combustion zone are converted and destroyed [15]. The Lurgi MegaMethanol autothermal reformer operates at a pressure above 40 bar (4.0 MPa) with reformer outlet temperatures typically in the range 950 to 1,050 °C [13]. Industrial autothermal reformer syngas outlet temperatures are reported in the range 900 to 1,100 °C at an oxygen-to-hydrocarbon molar ratio of 0.55 to 0.6 [15]. Within the catalyst bed the temperature decreases from typically 1,200 to 1,300 °C at the bed inlet to approximately 1,000 °C at the bed exit, the bed operating adiabatically [15]. In PFD-104 the effluent leaves at 1,050 °C and 3,600 kPa [20]. The burner is a key element: the feed and oxygen are mixed in a turbulent diffusion flame whose core is at very high temperature, and intensive mixing at the burner is essential to avoid soot formation [15]. A stable flame contour is required to protect the reactor walls and the catalyst from excessive temperatures [13]. The preferred autothermal reforming catalyst is nickel-based; at bed temperatures the nickel is subject to a high degree of sintering, so a catalyst and carrier of high thermal stability are required, and graded catalyst layers with higher thermal resistance at the top and higher activity at the bottom are charged [13][15]. Soot formation under the fuel-rich combustion conditions is inhibited in normal operation by correct burner design, by steam addition, and by the action of the catalyst bed in converting soot precursors [15]. The oxygen supply itself is a major cost element: an air separation unit can constitute up to 40 per cent of plant investment in autothermal reforming service [15]; against this, for capacities above about 1,500 tonne per day the capital saving of combined reforming relative to conventional steam reforming exceeds the investment in the air separation unit [13].

## Heat recovery and condensate separation

The waste-heat boiler E-402 recovers the sensible heat of the autothermal reformer effluent as saturated high-pressure steam; the syngas cooler E-403 completes cooling to the temperature at which process condensate, formed from unreacted steam, is knocked out in the condensate separator V-401 [13]. No reaction is intended in the cooling train; the water-gas shift equilibrium continues to move during cooling until quenched by low temperature. In PFD-104 the gas is cooled from 1,050 °C to 300 °C across E-402 and to 40 °C at 3,400 kPa across E-403, and V-401 separates 78,000 kg/h of gas from 12,000 kg/h of process condensate [20]. In the reference combined-reforming arrangement the reformed gas leaving the waste-heat boiler is further cooled in feed preheat and circulation-water heating service and against distillation reboiler duties before final cooling, and the separated process condensate is recycled as saturator and process water [13]. The waste-heat boiler operates with a large temperature difference and a gas containing hydrogen at high pressure; tube-sheet design, bypass control for steam pressure regulation, and protection against metal dusting and dew-point corrosion govern the design. Recovery of reformed-gas heat into the distillation reboilers couples the front end and the purification section and reduces external steam demand [13].

## Carbon dioxide addition

Carbon dioxide (12,000 kg/h in PFD-104) is added to the separated syngas to raise the carbon oxide content and thereby set the stoichiometric number of the combined gas to the target range stated in the Process description section, and to exploit the productivity benefit of a modest carbon dioxide content in the synthesis feed [6][13][18]. The added carbon dioxide is converted in the converter by the carbon dioxide hydrogenation and reverse water-gas shift reactions [6][14]. Addition is made at the suction conditions of the synthesis gas compressor, 40 °C and 3,300 kPa [20]. Carbon dioxide addition consumes three moles of hydrogen per mole of methanol formed from carbon dioxide and produces one mole of water per mole, so excess carbon dioxide raises the water content of the crude methanol, increases the refining column load, and accelerates water-related catalyst deactivation; the addition rate is therefore a controlled variable tied to the measured stoichiometric number [7][14]. Where the carbon dioxide is recovered from flue gas or imported, its purity with respect to sulfur and chloride must meet the same limits as the syngas itself [18].

## Synthesis loop and converter

The synthesis gas compressor C-403 raises the conditioned syngas from the reforming pressure to the synthesis loop pressure; the aftercooler E-404 removes the heat of compression before the gas joins the recycle at the loop junction. In PFD-104 the compressor raises 90,000 kg/h from 3,300 kPa to 7,500 kPa with a discharge temperature of 130 °C, cooled in E-404 to 45 °C at 7,400 kPa [20]. Because the reforming section operates at elevated pressure, compression to synthesis pressure in the reference combined-reforming design is possible with a single-stage (single-casing) compressor, and compression energy is reduced relative to low-pressure reforming [13]. Lubricating oil carried over from compressor seals is a catalyst poison by pore blocking, and oil separation and filtration on the discharge are required [18]. The make-up gas rate sets the loop pressure through the purge and pressure control arrangement; compressor surge protection and the interaction between make-up flow and recycle compressor C-404 duty govern loop operability.

The methanol converter R-403 carries out the synthesis reactions over a fixed bed of copper/zinc oxide/alumina catalyst. The commercial catalyst has a copper, zinc oxide and alumina (aluminium oxide, Al2O3) atomic ratio of approximately 60/30/10, with 1 to 3 per cent by weight of magnesium oxide promoter in some formulations; catalytic activity depends on the exposed copper surface area [6]. The Imperial Chemical Industries (ICI) low-pressure catalyst of this family, operative from the late 1960s, was enabled by upstream gas purification that freed the synthesis gas from sulfur poisoning [7]. Industrial methanol synthesis reactors typically operate at 5 to 10 MPa and 200 to 300 °C [6]; the ICI low-pressure process operates at 50 to 100 bar (5 to 10 MPa) and 200 to 300 °C [7], and typical reaction conditions of 200 to 300 °C at 5 to 11 MPa are reported in the catalyst literature [18]. In PFD-104 the converter feed enters at 220 °C and 7,300 kPa and the effluent leaves at 270 °C and 7,150 kPa [20]. Three converter arrangements are in industrial use for heat removal from the exothermic reaction [13][18]: (1) the quench converter (adiabatic multibed), in which the catalyst is charged in several adiabatic beds in a single pressure shell and temperature is controlled by injection of cold synthesis gas between the beds; (2) the tube-cooled converter (gas-cooled), in which the catalyst is cooled by the incoming synthesis gas flowing in tubes, so that the feed is preheated by the reaction heat; in the Lurgi combined arrangement a gas-cooled reactor downstream of a water-cooled reactor operates down to 220 °C at its outlet, and the decreasing temperature along the reaction path increases the driving force and the per-pass conversion [13]; (3) the boiling-water converter (steam-raising), in which the catalyst is packed in vertical tubes surrounded by pressurised boiling water on the shell side, the reaction heat raises steam, and the reaction temperature is controlled exactly by the steam pressure; about 0.8 tonne of steam at 40 bar is generated per tonne of methanol in the reference combined-converter arrangement [13]. Removal of the reaction heat while maintaining temperature control for optimum catalyst life and rate is the central design problem; beds with insufficient heat removal develop hot spots that damage the catalyst [18]. The converter of PFD-104 is drawn as a single vessel [20], and the choice among the three types is a licensor decision not resolved by the drawing.

Because per-pass conversion is limited by equilibrium, unreacted gas is recycled to the converter by the recycle compressor C-404 to achieve high overall conversion; a purge stream is withdrawn on pressure control to prevent excessive accumulation of the inert components of the feed (methane, nitrogen and argon) in the loop [13][14]. In PFD-104 the separator vapour of 276,000 kg/h is split into a purge of 16,000 kg/h and a recycle of 260,000 kg/h, which C-404 returns at 7,450 kPa [20]; the design-basis recycle ratio, defined as the recycle mass flow divided by the fresh feed mass flow, is therefore approximately 2.9. Reported reference values are a recycle ratio of approximately 3 for a single-stage water-cooled converter, reduced to approximately 1.5 for the combined water-cooled and gas-cooled converter arrangement [13]. The purge rate is the principal control on loop inert concentration: a larger purge stabilises converter temperature and rate but loses hydrogen and carbon oxides to fuel. Purge gas is conventionally routed to hydrogen recovery, with the recovered hydrogen returned to the synthesis gas, the residue used as reformer fuel, and a small amount used for the hydrogenation step of feed desulfurisation [13]. Inert accumulation dilutes the reactants, lowers partial pressures and conversion, and raises compression duty; methane slip from the reformers is the dominant inert source in the PFD-104 configuration.

## Crude methanol separation

The converter effluent is cooled first against the loop feed in the synthesis feed preheater E-405 and then in the effluent cooler E-406 to approximately 40 °C, at which temperature methanol and water condense and are separated from the unreacted gas in the methanol separator V-402 [13]. In PFD-104 the effluent is cooled from 270 °C to 120 °C across E-405 and to 40 °C at 7,050 kPa across E-406; V-402 separates 276,000 kg/h of vapour from 74,000 kg/h of crude methanol [20]. The separator level control delivers crude methanol to purification; the crude product contains, apart from methanol and water, dissolved gases and reaction byproducts [13]. Typical crude methanol feed to purification contains 88 to 90 per cent by weight of methanol, 9 to 11 per cent by weight of water, 0.5 to 0.8 per cent by weight of ethanol and 0.1 to 0.6 per cent by weight of higher alcohols [16]. Cooling below the design temperature increases methanol recovery to the liquid but also condenses more water and increases the dissolved-gas content of the crude; the let-down of the crude from loop pressure to column pressure flashes dissolved gas, which is routed to fuel.

## Topping column T-401

The topping column T-401 removes the light ends of the crude methanol: dissolved gases and the low-boiling byproducts dimethyl ether, methyl formate and acetone [16]. The column is a refluxed stripper: the crude feed enters near the top stage and methanol vapour generated in the topping reboiler E-408 strips the light ends from the descending liquid [16]. The overhead system comprises the topping condenser E-407, the topping reflux drum V-403 and the topping reboiler E-408. No reaction is intended; the separation is by volatility. Methanol purification columns operate at pressures slightly above atmospheric pressure [16]. In PFD-104 the column operates between 160 and 190 kPa, with overhead vapour at 75 °C, reflux returned at 55 °C from V-403, and bottoms leaving at approximately 100 °C [20]. The light ends product is 4,000 kg/h and the topping bottoms to the refining column are 70,000 kg/h [20]. About 20 per cent of the total heat requirement of a two-column purification section is associated with the topping column [16]. The overhead light ends stream, containing dimethyl ether and methyl formate together with methanol vapour, is routed to fuel; vent treatment and flammability control of the overhead system are required. Excessive boil-up carries methanol overhead and reduces yield, while insufficient stripping leaves light ends to contaminate the refined product.

## Refining column T-402

The refining column T-402 separates the topping bottoms into refined methanol overhead, water as the aqueous bottoms, and the middle-boiling impurities as a side draw. The side stream, termed fusel oil and consisting principally of ethanol together with higher alcohols, ketones and esters, is withdrawn below the feed stage; provision of the side stream enables production to the United States federal specification O-M-232K Grade AA [16]. The overhead system comprises the refining condenser E-409, the refining reflux drum V-404 and the refining reboiler E-410. No reaction is intended; the separation is by volatility. In PFD-104 the column operates between 130 and 160 kPa, with overhead vapour at 70 °C, reflux of 20,000 kg/h returned from V-404, refined methanol of 65,000 kg/h withdrawn from V-404 and cooled in the refined methanol cooler E-411 to 40 °C, and aqueous bottoms of 5,000 kg/h [20]. A specific low-pressure steam consumption of 1.338 tonne of steam per tonne of methanol is reported for the two-column scheme in a small-capacity plant study [16]; energy-integrated three-column schemes reduce the purification heat input by 30 to 40 per cent at higher capital cost [16]. The refining column is the largest energy consumer of the purification section [16]. Side-draw rate and draw location control the ethanol content of the overhead product; the aqueous bottoms are treated to limit methanol loss, with a methanol content in process water of at most 10 ppm by weight reported for a four-column configuration [16]. Product water content is controlled by the column temperature profile and reflux ratio.

## Synthesis loop behaviour

Per-pass conversion in the converter is limited by the equilibrium of the synthesis reactions and by the approach to equilibrium attainable in the catalyst bed; an average carbon monoxide conversion per pass of 25 per cent is reported for industrial operation [6]. The unconverted gas is therefore recycled, and overall conversion is achieved through the loop rather than in a single pass [13][14]. Selectivity to methanol over the commercial copper/zinc oxide/alumina catalyst is higher than 99 per cent under industrial conditions [6], and the reactor outlet gas contains only parts per million of reaction byproducts in the reference combined-converter design [13].

The byproducts of the synthesis are formed by side reactions of the carbon oxides and of methanol on the catalyst and on contaminant deposits. Higher alcohols (ethanol, propanol, butanol) are formed by chain-growth reactions; alkali impurities on the catalyst result in increased higher alcohol production [18]; higher alcohols appear in the crude methanol at 0.1 to 0.6 per cent by weight, together with 0.5 to 0.8 per cent by weight of ethanol [16]. Ethers, principally dimethyl ether, are formed by dehydration of methanol on acidic sites; zinc oxide reacts with alumina in the catalyst in a manner that inhibits dimethyl ether formation [18]; dimethyl ether is removed in the topping column as a light end [16]. Formates and other esters, principally methyl formate, are removed as light ends in the topping column [16]; ketones, principally acetone, and aldehydes are formed in smaller amounts [16]. Methane and hydrocarbons are formed by methanation and Fischer-Tropsch type reactions on iron and nickel deposited on the catalyst from iron carbonyl and nickel carbonyl in the feed gas; methanation over deposited iron and nickel proceeds from about 250 °C and is highly exothermic, so carbonyl contamination also creates a temperature-excursion hazard [18].

Three catalyst deactivation mechanisms dominate. Sintering: the active phase is metallic copper, and activity follows the exposed copper area [6]; copper crystallites grow by thermal sintering, which is accelerated by high temperature, by steam, and by hot spots from inadequate heat removal [18]; formulation components, including zinc oxide and alkaline earth promoters, inhibit sintering and agglomeration of the copper particles [18]. Sulfur poisoning: sulfur compounds are potent copper poisons; the zinc oxide component of the catalyst acts as an internal sulfur absorbent, reacting with hydrogen sulfide to form zinc sulfide and water, and the maximum allowable sulfur content of the syngas fed to the loop is 0.1 ppm by volume expressed as hydrogen sulfide, feed desulfurisation to the parts-per-billion range being the primary protection [12][18]. Chlorine poisoning: chlorine in any form is a virulent poison for the catalyst; hydrogen chloride reacts with the active copper to form low-melting copper chloride, which sinters rapidly and destroys activity, and the limiting hydrogen chloride content of the syngas feed is of the order of 1 ppb [7][18]. Water-related deactivation: water formed by the carbon dioxide hydrogenation and reverse water-gas shift reactions inhibits the rate and contributes to deactivation [7]; control of the carbon dioxide content of the loop feed is therefore also a catalyst-life measure. A verified industrial catalyst lifetime figure could not be established from the sources opened for the underlying dossier and is recorded as unverified; vendor claims in the range of two to five years were encountered only in sources that were not opened and are not cited.

## Balance model

The App panel computes a two-part mass balance model. The symbol $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{fresh}}$ denotes the fresh synthesis gas feed rate to the loop (in kg/h), the symbol $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{purge}}$ denotes the purge gas rate withdrawn from the separator vapour (in kg/h), and the symbol $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{rec}}$ denotes the circulation rate, the separator vapour returned to the converter inlet by the recycle compressor (in kg/h). The crude methanol rate, denoted $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{crude}}$ (in kg/h), is the closing product of the loop balance: at steady state the loop inventory is constant, so every kilogram of fresh feed leaves the loop either as purge gas or as condensed crude methanol, giving

$$\dot{m}_{\mathrm{crude}} = \dot{m}_{\mathrm{fresh}} - \dot{m}_{\mathrm{purge}}$$

The converter feed rate, denoted $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{conv}}$ (in kg/h), is the fresh feed plus the circulation,

$$\dot{m}_{\mathrm{conv}} = \dot{m}_{\mathrm{fresh}} + \dot{m}_{\mathrm{rec}}$$

and the separator vapour rate, denoted $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{vap}}$ (in kg/h), is the converter feed minus the condensed crude methanol, so that the separator vapour splits into purge and recycle exactly,

$$\dot{m}_{\mathrm{vap}} = \dot{m}_{\mathrm{conv}} - \dot{m}_{\mathrm{crude}}$$

The recycle ratio, denoted $R$ (dimensionless), is the circulation divided by the fresh feed, $R=\dot{m}_{\mathrm{rec}}/\dot{m}_{\mathrm{fresh}}$. The methanol formation per pass, denoted $w$ (in mass per cent), is the crude methanol condensed per pass divided by the converter feed, multiplied by 100,

$$w = 100 \, \dot{m}_{\mathrm{crude}} / \dot{m}_{\mathrm{conv}}$$

At the default inputs (fresh feed 90,000 kg/h, purge 16,000 kg/h, circulation 350,000 kg/h) the loop balance gives crude methanol of 74,000 kg/h, a converter feed of 440,000 kg/h, a separator vapour of 366,000 kg/h, a recycle ratio of 3.89 and a methanol formation per pass of 16.8 mass per cent. The model is a loop-level balance on prescribed inputs: the split of the separator vapour between purge and recycle is prescribed by the inputs, no reaction or separation calculation is performed, and the stream-by-stream values of the diagram (separator vapour 276,000 kg/h, of which 260,000 kg/h returns through the recycle compressor C-404, joining the fresh feed to form the converter feed of 350,000 kg/h) are the design-basis sample values of PFD-104 [20] and are not recomputed by the model.

In the purification part, the symbol $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{LE}}$ denotes the light ends rate removed overhead in the topping column T-401 (in kg/h) and the symbol $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{aq}}$ denotes the aqueous bottoms rate of the refining column T-402 (in kg/h), both prescribed as model-level splits of the crude methanol. The refined methanol rate, denoted $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (in kg/h), is the closing product of the purification balance,

$$\dot{m}_{\mathrm{ref}} = \dot{m}_{\mathrm{crude}} - \dot{m}_{\mathrm{LE}} - \dot{m}_{\mathrm{aq}}$$

so the purification balance closes to the crude methanol feed exactly. At the default inputs (light ends 2,000 kg/h and aqueous bottoms 7,000 kg/h against the crude methanol of 74,000 kg/h) the refined methanol rate is 65,000 kg/h, equal to the refined methanol product of the PFD-104 design basis [20]. The aggregate model splits differ from the column-by-column design-basis splits (light ends 4,000 kg/h at T-401 and aqueous bottoms 5,000 kg/h at T-402 [20]) because the model treats the two-column train as a single separation step; both bases give the same refined methanol product. The overall plant anchor of the model is the PFD-104 design basis itself: total inputs of natural gas (38,000 kg/h), steam (30,000 kg/h), oxygen (22,000 kg/h) and carbon dioxide (12,000 kg/h) give 102,000 kg/h, equal to total outputs of process condensate (12,000 kg/h), purge gas (16,000 kg/h), light ends (4,000 kg/h), refined methanol (65,000 kg/h) and aqueous bottoms (5,000 kg/h) [20].

## Operating conditions

The operating window of the plant is set by the subunit conditions stated in the preceding sections. Feed desulfurisation operates at approximately 350 to 380 °C with a residual sulfur content of less than 0.1 ppm [13]. Steam reforming operates above 800 °C [1], with the PFD-104 primary reformer effluent at 850 °C and 3,800 kPa [20]. The autothermal reformer operates above 40 bar (4.0 MPa) with outlet temperatures of 950 to 1,050 °C [13], an oxygen-to-hydrocarbon molar ratio of 0.55 to 0.6 [15], and catalyst bed temperatures from 1,200 to 1,300 °C at the bed inlet to approximately 1,000 °C at the bed exit [15]; the PFD-104 effluent leaves at 1,050 °C and 3,600 kPa [20]. The synthesis gas is compressed from 3,300 kPa to 7,500 kPa [20]. The methanol converter operates in the industrial window of 5 to 10 MPa and 200 to 300 °C [6][7], with the PFD-104 feed at 220 °C and 7,300 kPa and the effluent at 270 °C and 7,150 kPa [20]. Crude methanol is condensed and separated at 40 °C [20]. The purification columns operate slightly above atmospheric pressure [16], T-401 between 160 and 190 kPa and T-402 between 130 and 160 kPa [20]. The stoichiometric number of the synthesis gas is maintained slightly above 2.0, and between 2.0 and 2.1 in the reference MegaMethanol design [13][14]. The synthesis loop operates at up to 7,500 kPa with a hydrogen-rich gas [20].

## Production scale

The sources opened report world production on differing reference years, and the figures are presented as a dated series rather than a single value: about 98 million tonne (Mt) produced per annum, referring to 2019, with installed capacity of about 150 Mt [1]; global production of 107 Mt per year in 2021 [5]; approximately 100 Mt per year, as reported in a 2026 techno-economic review [14]; more than 110 Mt produced globally each year, on the current Methanol Institute industry page, which does not print a reference year in the extracted text [2]; and forecast demand of about 100 million metric tonnes for 2026, on Methanex and OPIS Chemical Market Analytics data [10]. China accounts for about 50 per cent of world production and about 60 per cent of demand, and about 75 per cent of Chinese production is from coal-based plants; North America and Latin America together account for about 20 per cent of production and the Middle East and Africa for about 20 per cent [10]. Typical world-scale natural gas based plants have capacities of 3,000 to 5,000 tonne per day, that is, 1 to 1.7 Mt per year [1]; the reference plant of PFD-104, at 65,000 kg/h or about 1,560 tonne per day, is below world scale and corresponds to approximately 0.57 Mt per year at continuous operation.

The United States Energy Information Administration (EIA) reported in February 2019 that three new methanol plants with a combined capacity of about 3.3 million metric tonnes per year would raise total United States methanol capacity to 9.4 million metric tonnes per year (25,600 metric tonnes per day), an increase of 45 per cent; most United States plants are located on the Gulf Coast [4]. The largest of the plants, Natgasoline at Beaumont, Texas, has a capacity of 5,000 metric tonnes per day, entered operation in June 2018, and was estimated to consume 0.15 billion cubic feet per day of natural gas [4]. No later United States production figure was verified against an opened source during preparation of the underlying dossier; the 2019 capacity figure is therefore dated explicitly and no more recent figure is asserted. Methanex Corporation is the world's largest producer and supplier of methanol; its equity production was 7.8 million metric tonnes in 2025, and its operating capacity is about 6.4 million metric tonnes in North America (six plants) and about 4 million metric tonnes in the rest of the world (five plants) [10]. Greenfield projects typically require four to five years from final investment decision to production [10].

The current demand split reported by Methanex, on OPIS data, is: traditional chemical applications about 55 per cent; methanol-to-olefins about 15 per cent; energy-related applications about 30 per cent; and green feedstock uses under 1 per cent [10]. The principal derivatives are formaldehyde, the simplest aldehyde, manufactured by oxidation of methanol and consumed principally in resins, adhesives and engineering plastics, and the largest-volume methanol derivative [1]; methyl tert-butyl ether, an ether manufactured from methanol and isobutylene and used as a gasoline oxygenate and octane enhancer [10][18]; acetic acid, a carboxylic acid manufactured by carbonylation of methanol [18]; methanol-to-olefins, the conversion of methanol over zeolite catalysts to the light olefins ethylene and propylene at a stoichiometric consumption of about three tonnes of methanol per tonne of olefins [1][10]; and fuel uses, comprising direct blending into gasoline, combustion as a neat or blended fuel, conversion to dimethyl ether, use in biodiesel transesterification, and marine fuel [1][10].

## Process economics

Natural gas consumption of natural gas based methanol plants is about 33.4 to 36.5 gigajoule (GJ) of natural gas (higher heating value basis) per tonne of methanol, with best practice at 31.5 GJ per tonne [5]. These figures combine feedstock and fuel gas. Combined reforming reduces the synthesis gas demand per tonne of methanol by approximately 25 per cent relative to conventional steam reforming, with further energy savings of about 8 per cent reported for the combined-reforming configuration [13]. Purification energy is additional to the front-end figure and is dominated by the refining column reboiler [16].

Capital expenditure for a large-scale conventional natural gas based plant is estimated at 804 euro (EUR) per tonne of annual capacity, based on cost data for the Natgasoline greenfield plant of 1.9 Mt per year at Beaumont, United States [5]. At that specific cost, a plant of the Natgasoline capacity corresponds to a total investment of approximately EUR 1.5 billion (calculated from the cited specific cost and capacity). The synthesis gas generation section accounts for more than 50 per cent of the capital cost of the entire plant, so optimisation of that section yields the largest cost benefit [13]. Capital distribution reported for a conventional natural gas based plant of 2,500 tonne per day assigns about 55 per cent to syngas generation, about 12 per cent to distillation and about 12 per cent to compression and synthesis, with utilities and other services comprising the balance [16]. Mega-methanol scale is defined by the Lurgi MegaMethanol technology as gas-based plants larger than 1 Mt per year, with a standard plant size of 5,000 tonne per day and single-train designs up to 10,000 tonne per day [13]. The reference plant of PFD-104, at about 1,560 tonne per day, is below the capacity range (above about 1,500 tonne per day) at which the capital saving of combined reforming exceeds the investment in the air separation unit [13].

The production cost of fossil methanol is reported at USD 100 to 250 per tonne [1], where USD denotes United States dollar. A techno-economic model of a conventional plant reports generic fixed operating expenditure of 4 per cent of capital expenditure per year and variable operating expenditure of EUR 15 per tonne, excluding feedstock [5]. Natural gas is the primary feedstock and the most significant component of production cost; at a gas consumption of 32 GJ per tonne, the modelled production cost rises from EUR 146 per tonne to EUR 551 per tonne as the natural gas price rises from USD 1 to USD 17 per million British thermal unit [5][10]. A verified percentage share of natural gas in total operating cost could not be established from the sources opened: published values in secondary market literature range widely and are not cited; the natural gas share is therefore recorded as unverified in percentage terms, with the qualitative position and the price sensitivity verified as stated. Methanex reported an average realised price of USD 361 per metric tonne in 2025 and USD 355 per metric tonne in 2024 [10]. Historical context: the European contract price has fluctuated roughly between USD 200 and USD 400 per tonne (inflation-adjusted) since 1995 [1], and the conventional methanol market price has been mainly EUR 200 to EUR 400 per tonne over the past decade [5]. The Methanol Institute publishes current price, supply and demand data through an interactive Argus data hub rather than as a static figure [3]. Prices are set regionally (North America, Europe, Asia) under contract postings and spot trading, and the marginal, highest-cost production — coal-based production in China — has historically set the Asian price level [10].

## Safety and environmental issues

Methanol is toxic by inhalation, ingestion and skin absorption, and its odour gives insufficient warning of hazardous concentrations [8]. Toxicity is due to the metabolic products formaldehyde and formate, which cause metabolic acidosis, blindness and death; symptoms may be delayed from 1 to 72 hours after exposure [1][8]. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) Pocket Guide lists a recommended exposure limit of 200 ppm as a time-weighted average, a short-term exposure limit of 250 ppm, and an immediately dangerous to life or health value of 6,000 ppm for methyl alcohol [19]. Methanol is highly flammable: the flash point is about 11 °C, the lower explosive limit is 6.0 per cent by volume and the upper explosive limit is 36 to 36.5 per cent by volume, and the autoignition temperature is about 464 °C [8][9]. Methanol burns with a nearly invisible flame in daylight; the United States Emergency Response Guidebook guidance reproduced in the CAMEO Chemicals datasheet states that methanol (United Nations number UN1230) will burn with an invisible flame and directs the use of an alternate method of detection, such as a thermal camera [9]. Vapours are heavier than air, may travel to a source of ignition and flash back, and collect in low areas; containers may explode when heated [8]. Firefighting media are dry chemical, carbon dioxide, water spray and alcohol-resistant foam; the initial isolation distance for a spill is 50 metre and for a tank fire 800 metre [9]. Storage and handling rules follow from the properties: closed, bonded and grounded transfer systems; ventilation and vapour detection that does not rely on flame visibility; segregation from oxidants and ignition sources; and labelling as a toxic flammable liquid under UN1230 [8][9].

The synthesis loop of PFD-104 operates at up to 7,500 kPa with a hydrogen-rich gas [20]. Hydrogen has a wide flammability range, a very low ignition energy and a nearly invisible flame, and it causes embrittlement of susceptible steels; loop design therefore requires hydrogen-service materials selection, leak detection, and depressurisation to flare. The purge and fuel gas system contains hydrogen, carbon monoxide and methane and is routed to the flare during start-up and shutdown [13]. Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless and toxic gas whose acute effects arise from the formation of carboxyhaemoglobin in the blood, which inhibits oxygen intake [17]. The occupational limits recorded by the United States Environmental Protection Agency are: the Occupational Safety and Health Administration permissible exposure limit of 50 ppm as an 8-hour time-weighted average; the NIOSH recommended exposure limit of 35 ppm as a time-weighted average with a ceiling of 200 ppm; and the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists threshold limit value of 25 ppm [17]. Syngas areas therefore require fixed and personal carbon monoxide detection and permit-controlled entry procedures.

The reforming section combines fired equipment, radiant tubes at up to 850 °C outlet temperature and an oxygen-blown vessel with a flame core at very high temperature. Principal hazards are tube rupture and the resulting hydrogen-rich gas fire; oxygen enrichment of hydrocarbon streams, with explosion risk on maldistribution or loss of steam flow; soot formation and the resulting catalyst bed plugging and hot spots, controlled by burner design and steam addition [15]; refractory failure in the autothermal reformer; and burns and steam release from the waste-heat boiler. Oxygen compression (C-402 in PFD-104) requires oil-free machinery and oxygen-clean construction.

Process carbon dioxide emissions from conventional methanol production are 0.5 to 1.5 tonne of carbon dioxide per tonne of methanol [5]. At a natural gas consumption of 32 GJ per tonne, production-only emissions from combustion are about 0.532 tonne per tonne, and the total including the end-of-life release of the carbon contained in the methanol is 1.92 tonne per tonne, excluding supply-chain emissions [5]. On a life-cycle basis the carbon intensity is about 0.5 kilogram of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilogram of methanol for the natural gas route against 2.6 to 3.8 kilogram per kilogram for the coal route [1], and average direct and indirect fossil emissions of approximately 0.76 kilogram per kilogram are reported for Western European production [14]. Life-cycle emissions from current production are about 0.3 gigatonne of carbon dioxide per annum, about 10 per cent of chemical sector emissions [1]. The boundary of each figure differs (process, combustion, life-cycle, or including end-of-life release) and the figures are not interchangeable. Renewable methanol production was below 0.2 Mt per year at the time of the 2021 IRENA and Methanol Institute assessment, predominantly bio-methanol from biomass and biogas [1]. Bio-methanol production cost is reported at USD 320 to 770 per tonne, and e-methanol, defined as methanol synthesised from carbon dioxide and electrolytic hydrogen, is reported at a current cost of USD 800 to 1,600 per tonne with bioenergy carbon capture carbon dioxide, and USD 1,200 to 2,400 per tonne with direct air capture carbon dioxide [1]; both routes use the synthesis and purification sections substantially unchanged, the differences lying in syngas generation and in stoichiometric number control [13][14].

## Issues of special concern

Catalyst sensitivity. The copper/zinc oxide/alumina synthesis catalyst is the most sensitive element of the plant. Protection requirements established in the preceding sections are: sulfur in the loop feed at or below 0.1 ppm by volume as hydrogen sulfide [18]; hydrogen chloride of the order of 1 ppb or below [18]; exclusion of chlorinated compounds from the synthesis gas production section and the loop during construction, pre-commissioning and maintenance, including as solvents and degreasing agents [18]; control of iron and nickel carbonyl formation in carbon monoxide rich gas in contact with steel at about 200 °C, because the deposited metals poison the catalyst and catalyse exothermic methanation [18]; oil separation on compressor discharge [18]; and temperature control to limit sintering and steaming of the bed [18]. The reforming catalysts are likewise sulfur sensitive, and the autothermal reformer catalyst additionally requires thermal stability against sintering at bed temperatures up to 1,300 °C [15].

Feed gas composition control. The stoichiometric number is the controlling specification of the synthesis gas: the target is slightly above 2.0, and 2.0 to 2.1 in the reference MegaMethanol design [13][14]. Deviations have direct consequences: a hydrogen-deficient gas (stoichiometric number below 2.0) cannot convert all carbon oxides and depresses loop performance, while excess hydrogen behaves as an inert in the loop, increases equipment size and compression duty, and is discharged with the purge to fuel [13]. The composition is set in PFD-104 by the feed split between R-401 and R-402 and by the carbon dioxide addition rate, with the carbon monoxide to carbon dioxide ratio as a secondary variable: a high ratio increases reaction rate and conversion and decreases water formation, which in turn reduces the catalyst deactivation rate [13]. Inert content (methane, nitrogen, argon) is controlled through the purge, because inerts lower the partial pressure of the reactants [13].

Water management in purification. Water enters the purification section as a synthesis product: crude methanol contains 9 to 11 per cent by weight of water [16], and crude methanol from synthesis generally contains 4 to 20 per cent by weight of water depending on the carbon oxide composition of the syngas [18]. The refining column reboiler duty is dominated by the methanol-water separation; the aqueous bottoms must meet a methanol loss limit, with at most 10 ppm by weight of methanol in process water reported for an energy-integrated four-column configuration [16]; process condensate from V-401 is recovered to the saturator and reforming system, closing the plant water balance [13]; and the water content of the product is itself a specification item, limited to 0.100 per cent by weight in the reference specification [11] and to not more than 500 ppm by weight for gasoline blending service [16]. Higher carbon dioxide addition rates increase the water load on all of these items.

Product grades. Two product grades are distinguished in industrial practice. Fuel grade methanol is the less stringent grade, used as a gasoline blending component; the major quality requirements are water content and dissolved gases, and fuel grade product is normally produced with a single distillation column [16]. Grade AA methanol is the high-purity chemical grade and the general standard observed by the chemical industry for methanol product purity; Grade AA is defined in the United States by federal specification O-M-232K, and production of Grade AA requires two or more distillation columns and the fusel oil side draw of the refining column [16]. The internationally used commercial specification is the International Methanol Producers and Consumers Association (IMPCA) Methanol Reference Specifications, Version 9 of 10 June 2021, which specifies, among other items: purity on a dry basis of at least 99.85 per cent by weight; water at most 0.100 per cent by weight; ethanol at most 50 milligram per kilogram (mg/kg); acetone at most 30 mg/kg; chloride at most 0.5 mg/kg; sulfur at most 0.5 mg/kg; a potassium permanganate time test of at least 60 minutes at 15 °C; a distillation range of at most 1.0 °C at 760 millimetre of mercury; specific gravity between 0.7910 and 0.7930 at 20 °C; colour at most 5 on the platinum-cobalt scale; acidity as acetic acid at most 30 mg/kg; iron in solution at most 0.10 mg/kg; and non-volatile matter at most 8 mg per 1,000 millilitre [11]. The refined methanol product of PFD-104 is drawn as the refining column overhead product of a two-column train with a fusel oil capable configuration, consistent with Grade AA production as specified by the IMPCA reference specification [11][16].
