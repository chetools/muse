Stability limits of two first-order systems in series under proportional–integral control

Two first-order systems in series, closed with a proportional–integral controller, give a third-order characteristic equation. Each system has a steady-state gain and a time constant: $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ are the two time constants, in seconds, and $K$ is the overall process gain, the product of the two system gains. In the controller, $K_c$ is the proportional gain and $\tau_I$ is the integral time, in seconds. The Routh–Hurwitz criterion applied to that equation gives a sharp stability boundary: the loop is stable for every positive controller gain $K_c$ when the integral time $\tau_I$ is at or above $\tau_1 \tau_2 / (\tau_1 + \tau_2)$, and below that integral time stability ends at the critical controller gain $K_{c,\mathrm{crit}} = \tau_I (\tau_1 + \tau_2) / [K(\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2))]$. At the boundary the loop oscillates at the angular frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)}$. Assumed background is Laplace transforms, transfer functions, and feedback control; the page is written for chemical engineering students.

1System

Figure 1 shows the loop. Two first-order systems stand in series in the forward path, and a proportional–integral controller drives them from the error between set point and output, with unity negative feedback. The derivation assumes linear time-invariant behaviour, no saturation, no dead time, and positive parameters throughout: $\tau_1 > 0$, $\tau_2 > 0$, $\tau_I > 0$, $K > 0$, and $K_c > 0$. A zero controller gain leaves a root at the origin, and a negative controller gain makes the constant term of the characteristic equation negative; neither case has all roots with negative real parts, and the analysis below takes $K_c > 0$.

Each first-order system has the transfer function of a first-order lag: a steady-state gain over a first-order denominator in $s$ [1]. For the first system, the transfer function is

$$G_1(s) = \frac{K_1}{\tau_1 s + 1}$$(1)

and the transfer function of the second system is

$$G_2(s) = \frac{K_2}{\tau_2 s + 1}$$(2)

Two blocks in series multiply, and the process transfer function, from $U(s)$ to $Y(s)$, is thus the product of Eqs. (1) and (2):

$$\begin{aligned} G_p(s) = G_1(s)\, G_2(s) &= \frac{K_1 K_2}{(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1)} \\ &= \frac{K}{(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1)} \end{aligned}$$(3)

where $K = K_1 K_2$ denotes the overall process steady-state gain.

The proportional–integral controller has proportional gain $K_c$ and integral time $\tau_I$. Its transfer function, from $E(s)$ to $U(s)$, is the sum of a proportional term and an integral term [1]:

$$G_c(s) = K_c \left(1 + \frac{1}{\tau_I s}\right) = K_c + \frac{K_i}{s} = K_c\,\frac{\tau_I s + 1}{\tau_I s}$$(4)

where $K_i = K_c / \tau_I$ denotes the integral gain.

Equation (4) shows the two features of integral action that drive the stability result: a pole at the origin, from the factor $1/s$, and a zero at $s = -1/\tau_I$, from the factor $\tau_I s + 1$. That pole raises the order of the closed loop from two to three; the position of the zero, set by $\tau_I$, decides whether that third-order loop stays stable.

2Characteristic equation

The open-loop transfer function is the product of the controller and the process. From Eqs. (3) and (4), it is

$$\begin{aligned} L(s) &= G_c(s)\, G_p(s) \\ &= K_c\,\frac{\tau_I s + 1}{\tau_I s}\cdot\frac{K}{(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1)} \\ &= \frac{K K_c\,(\tau_I s + 1)}{\tau_I s\,(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1)} \end{aligned}$$(5)

For unity negative feedback, the closed-loop poles are the roots of $1 + L(s) = 0$. The characteristic equation is thus

$$1 + L(s) = 0$$(6)

Substitution of Eq. (5) in Eq. (6) gives

$$1 + \frac{K K_c\,(\tau_I s + 1)}{\tau_I s\,(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1)} = 0$$(7)

Multiplication of Eq. (7) through by the denominator $\tau_I s\,(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1)$ clears the fraction. The first term gives $\tau_I s\,(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1)$, and the second term gives $K K_c\,(\tau_I s + 1)$. Equation (7) thus becomes

$$\tau_I s\,(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1) + K K_c\,(\tau_I s + 1) = 0$$(8)

Expansion of the product of the two first-order factors proceeds term by term: $\tau_1 s$ times $\tau_2 s$ gives $\tau_1 \tau_2 s^2$; $\tau_1 s$ times $1$, plus $1$ times $\tau_2 s$, gives $(\tau_1 + \tau_2)\,s$; and $1$ times $1$ gives $1$. Hence

$$(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1) = \tau_1 \tau_2 s^2 + (\tau_1 + \tau_2)\,s + 1$$(9)

Multiplication of Eq. (9) by $\tau_I s$ multiplies each term in turn: $\tau_I s$ times $\tau_1 \tau_2 s^2$ gives $\tau_I \tau_1 \tau_2 s^3$; $\tau_I s$ times $(\tau_1 + \tau_2)\,s$ gives $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)\,s^2$; and $\tau_I s$ times $1$ gives $\tau_I s$. Thus

$$\begin{aligned} \tau_I s\,(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1) &= \tau_I \tau_1 \tau_2 s^3 + \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)\,s^2 \\ &\qquad + \tau_I s \end{aligned}$$(10)

Expansion of the remaining term of Eq. (8) gives

$$K K_c\,(\tau_I s + 1) = K K_c \tau_I s + K K_c$$(11)

Substitution of Eqs. (10) and (11) in Eq. (8) gives a polynomial with two terms in $s$: $\tau_I s$ from Eq. (10) and $K K_c \tau_I s$ from Eq. (11). Collection of those terms gives $\tau_I s + K K_c \tau_I s = \tau_I(1 + K K_c)\,s$. The characteristic equation is therefore

$$\begin{aligned} \tau_I \tau_1 \tau_2 s^3 + \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)\,s^2 &+ \tau_I(1 + K K_c)\,s \\ &+ K K_c = 0 \end{aligned}$$(12)

Equation (12) is a cubic in $s$. Written with coefficients $a_3$, $a_2$, $a_1$, $a_0$, it is

$$a_3 s^3 + a_2 s^2 + a_1 s + a_0 = 0$$(13)

where $a_3 = \tau_I \tau_1 \tau_2$, $a_2 = \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$, $a_1 = \tau_I(1 + K K_c)$, and $a_0 = K K_c$.

Each term of Eq. (12) is dimensionless: $a_3$ has units of s3 and multiplies $s^3$ in s−3; $a_2$ has units of s2 and multiplies $s^2$ in s−2; $a_1$ has units of s and multiplies $s$ in s−1; and $a_0$ is dimensionless. Under the assumptions of Section 1, every coefficient is positive: $a_3 > 0$, $a_2 > 0$, $a_1 > 0$, and $a_0 > 0$ for $\tau_1, \tau_2, \tau_I, K, K_c > 0$.

3Routh–Hurwitz criterion

The Routh–Hurwitz criterion decides stability from the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial, without solving for its roots [1]. For a polynomial with real coefficients, all roots have negative real parts, and the closed loop is asymptotically stable, if and only if every entry in the first column of the Routh array is positive. This form of the criterion assumes the array completes with no zero entry in the first column; the boundary case $b_1 = 0$ is handled in Section 5 by the auxiliary equation, built there from the $s^2$ row of the array. A necessary condition, met here, is that all coefficients are non-zero and of the same sign; Eq. (13) meets it, since all four coefficients are positive.

For the cubic of Eq. (13), the Routh array has four rows, one per power of $s$ from $s^3$ to $s^0$. Coefficients enter the $s^3$ and $s^2$ rows in alternating order. Each entry below them is the negative determinant of the two rows above, divided by the first entry of the row directly above. With those entries, the array is

From the determinant rule applied to the $s^3$ and $s^2$ rows, the $s^1$ entry $b_1$ follows:

$$b_1 = \frac{a_2 a_1 - a_3 a_0}{a_2}$$(14)

Application of the same rule to the $s^2$ and $s^1$ rows gives the $s^0$ entry $c_1$. The second column of the $s^1$ row is $0$; the determinant thus gives $b_1 a_0 - a_2 \cdot 0$, divided by $b_1$:

$$c_1 = \frac{b_1 a_0 - a_2 \cdot 0}{b_1} = a_0$$(15)

The first column of the array is therefore $a_3$, $a_2$, $b_1$, $a_0$. Three of its entries are positive by the assumptions of Section 1: $a_3 > 0$, $a_2 > 0$, and $a_0 > 0$. Stability thus turns on the single remaining entry, $b_1 > 0$. For a cubic, the condition $b_1 > 0$ is the same as $a_2 a_1 > a_3 a_0$, since $a_2 > 0$.

4Stability condition

Evaluation of $b_1$ starts with the two products in Eq. (14). From the coefficients of Eq. (13), the first product is

$$\begin{aligned} a_2 a_1 &= \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)\cdot\tau_I(1 + K K_c) \\ &= \tau_I^2(\tau_1 + \tau_2)(1 + K K_c) \end{aligned}$$(16)

and the second product is

$$a_3 a_0 = \tau_I \tau_1 \tau_2 \cdot K K_c$$(17)

Subtraction of Eq. (17) from Eq. (16) gives the numerator of Eq. (14). Factorisation of $\tau_I$ from both terms gives

$$\begin{aligned} a_2 a_1 - a_3 a_0 &= \tau_I^2(\tau_1 + \tau_2)(1 + K K_c) \\ &\qquad - \tau_I \tau_1 \tau_2 K K_c \\ &= \tau_I\big[\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)(1 + K K_c) - \tau_1 \tau_2 K K_c\big] \end{aligned}$$(18)

Division of Eq. (18) by $a_2 = \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$, as Eq. (14) requires, proceeds term by term. The first term gives $\tau_I^2(\tau_1 + \tau_2)(1 + K K_c) / [\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)] = \tau_I(1 + K K_c)$, and the second term gives $\tau_I \tau_1 \tau_2 K K_c / [\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)] = \tau_1 \tau_2 K K_c / (\tau_1 + \tau_2)$. Hence

$$b_1 = \tau_I(1 + K K_c) - \frac{\tau_1 \tau_2 K K_c}{\tau_1 + \tau_2}$$(19)

The stability condition $b_1 > 0$ is, from Eq. (19),

$$\tau_I(1 + K K_c) > \frac{\tau_1 \tau_2 K K_c}{\tau_1 + \tau_2}$$(20)

Multiplication of Eq. (20) by the positive quantity $\tau_1 + \tau_2$ gives $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)(1 + K K_c) > \tau_1 \tau_2 K K_c$. Expansion of the left side gives $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) + \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) K K_c > \tau_1 \tau_2 K K_c$. Collection of the terms in $K_c$ on the left gives the stability condition in its most useful form:

$$\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) + K K_c\,\big[\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) - \tau_1 \tau_2\big] > 0$$(21)

Equation (21) has two terms: a positive term $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$, independent of $K_c$, and a term in $K_c$ whose sign is the sign of the bracket $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) - \tau_1 \tau_2$. The sign of that bracket divides the result into two cases.

Case A: long integral time, stability for every gain

When the bracket is positive, the $K_c$ term of Eq. (21) adds to the positive first term; Eq. (21) thus holds for every $K_c > 0$. The bracket is positive when

$$\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) - \tau_1 \tau_2 > 0 \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \tau_I > \frac{\tau_1 \tau_2}{\tau_1 + \tau_2}$$(22)

In this case the closed loop is stable for every positive controller gain. The threshold $\tau_1 \tau_2 / (\tau_1 + \tau_2)$ is the parallel combination of the two time constants, equal to $1/(1/\tau_1 + 1/\tau_2)$. For equal time constants $\tau_1 = \tau_2 = \tau$, it is $\tau/2$.

Case B: short integral time, a critical gain

When the bracket is negative, the $K_c$ term of Eq. (21) subtracts from the positive first term; stability thus holds only up to a limiting gain. The bracket is negative when $\tau_I < \tau_1 \tau_2 / (\tau_1 + \tau_2)$. In this case Eq. (21) rearranges as follows: movement of the $K_c$ term to the right gives $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) > K K_c\,[\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)]$, where the bracket on the right is now positive. Division by the positive quantity $K\,[\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)]$ preserves the inequality and gives

$$K_c < \frac{\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)}{K\,\big[\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)\big]} = K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$$(23)

where $K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$ denotes the critical controller gain.

Equation (23) is the stability limit for short integral times: the loop is stable for $0 < K_c < K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$ and unstable for $K_c > K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$. At $K_c = K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$, $b_1 = 0$ and the loop stands at the stability boundary. Two limits of Eq. (23) check the result: as $\tau_I$ approaches $\tau_1 \tau_2 / (\tau_1 + \tau_2)$ from below, the denominator approaches zero and $K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$ approaches infinity, joining Case A, where the loop is stable for every gain; and as $\tau_I$ approaches zero, the numerator approaches zero and $K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$ approaches zero; integral action with a very short integral time thus destabilises the loop at any gain. At the boundary value $\tau_I = \tau_1 \tau_2 / (\tau_1 + \tau_2)$ itself, the bracket in Eq. (21) is zero, Eq. (21) reduces to $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) > 0$, and the loop is stable for every $K_c > 0$.

For equal time constants $\tau_1 = \tau_2 = \tau$, Eq. (23) reduces to $K_{c,\mathrm{crit}} = 2\tau_I / [K(\tau - 2\tau_I)]$, valid for $\tau_I < \tau/2$.

5Oscillation at the limit

At $K_c = K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$, the entry $b_1$ is zero; the $s^1$ row of the Routh array is thus zero and the $s^0$ row cannot be formed from it. The Routh–Hurwitz procedure handles a zero row with the auxiliary equation, built from the row above it, the $s^2$ row: $a_2 s^2 + a_0 = 0$. Its roots are the pair of closed-loop poles on the imaginary axis at the stability boundary:

$$a_2 s^2 + a_0 = 0 \qquad\text{that is}\qquad s^2 = -\frac{a_0}{a_2}$$(24)

Equation (24) has roots $s = \pm j\sqrt{a_0/a_2}$, a conjugate pair on the imaginary axis. Their angular frequency, from $a_0 = K K_c$ and $a_2 = \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$ evaluated at $K_c = K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$, is

$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{a_0}{a_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{K K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}}{\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)}}$$(25)

Substitution of $K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$ from Eq. (23) in Eq. (25) proceeds in two steps. First, $K K_{c,\mathrm{crit}} = \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) / [\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)]$. Second, division of that result by $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$ cancels the factor $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$ and leaves

$$\omega_0^2 = \frac{1}{\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)}$$(26)

The oscillation frequency and its period at the stability boundary are therefore

$$\begin{aligned} \omega_0 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)}}, \\ T_0 &= \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} = 2\pi\sqrt{\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)} \end{aligned}$$(27)

Equation (27) applies only in Case B, where $\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) > 0$ and the square root is real. As $\tau_I$ approaches the threshold $\tau_1 \tau_2 / (\tau_1 + \tau_2)$ from below, the denominator of Eq. (26) approaches zero; $\omega_0$ thus approaches infinity and $T_0$ approaches zero; the boundary oscillation grows arbitrarily fast as the critical gain grows without bound.

Factorisation of the characteristic polynomial at $K_c = K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$ gives the third closed-loop pole at the boundary. At the boundary, $a_2 a_1 = a_3 a_0$ and thus $a_1/a_3 = a_0/a_2$. Division of Eq. (13) by $a_3$ gives $s^3 + (a_2/a_3)s^2 + (a_1/a_3)s + a_0/a_3 = 0$, and replacement of $a_1/a_3$ by $a_0/a_2$ gives $s^3 + (a_2/a_3)s^2 + (a_0/a_2)s + a_0/a_3 = 0$. That polynomial factorises as $(s + a_2/a_3)(s^2 + a_0/a_2) = 0$, since expansion of the product gives $s^3 + (a_2/a_3)s^2 + (a_0/a_2)s + (a_2/a_3)(a_0/a_2)$, and $(a_2/a_3)(a_0/a_2) = a_0/a_3$. The three poles at the boundary are thus $s = \pm j\omega_0$ and

$$s_3 = -\frac{a_2}{a_3} = -\frac{\tau_1 + \tau_2}{\tau_1 \tau_2} = -\left(\frac{1}{\tau_1} + \frac{1}{\tau_2}\right)$$(28)

The third pole is real and negative; the sustained oscillation at the boundary thus comes from the imaginary pair alone, with the third mode decaying at the rate $1/\tau_1 + 1/\tau_2$.

6Worked example

Take $\tau_1 = 5\,\mathrm{s}$, $\tau_2 = 2\,\mathrm{s}$, $K = 1$, and $\tau_I = 1\,\mathrm{s}$. Equation (22) gives the threshold integral time as

$$\frac{\tau_1 \tau_2}{\tau_1 + \tau_2} = \frac{5 \times 2}{5 + 2} = \frac{10}{7} \approx 1.43\,\mathrm{s}$$(29)

Since $\tau_I = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ is below the threshold of $1.43\,\mathrm{s}$, the loop falls in Case B and has a finite critical gain. From Eq. (23), the numerator is $\tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) = 1 \times 7 = 7\,\mathrm{s}$, and the denominator is $K[\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2)] = 1 \times (10 - 7) = 3\,\mathrm{s}^2$. The critical gain is therefore

$$K_{c,\mathrm{crit}} = \frac{7}{3} \approx 2.33$$(30)

Equation (27) with $\tau_1 \tau_2 - \tau_I(\tau_1 + \tau_2) = 10 - 7 = 3\,\mathrm{s}^2$ gives

$$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.577\,\mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{3} \approx 10.9\,\mathrm{s}$$(31)

From Eq. (28), the third pole at the boundary is $s_3 = -(1/5 + 1/2) = -0.700\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. 7Proportional-only control as a check

Proportional-only control, with $\tau_I \to \infty$ in Eq. (4), gives $G_c(s) = K_c$. The characteristic equation $1 + K_c K / [(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1)] = 0$ then clears to $(\tau_1 s + 1)(\tau_2 s + 1) + K K_c = 0$. Expansion by Eq. (9) gives the second-order equation

$$\tau_1 \tau_2 s^2 + (\tau_1 + \tau_2)\,s + 1 + K K_c = 0$$(32)

All three coefficients of Eq. (32) are positive for $\tau_1, \tau_2, K, K_c > 0$, and a second-order polynomial with positive coefficients has both roots with negative real parts. Proportional-only control of two first-order systems in series is therefore stable for every $K_c > 0$. The instability of Section 4 comes from integral action alone: the pole at the origin raises the loop to third order, and the Routh–Hurwitz condition $a_2 a_1 > a_3 a_0$ is the condition that the added dynamics do not push a pole pair across the imaginary axis.

From the limit of Eq. (12) as $\tau_I \to \infty$, the same conclusion follows. Division of Eq. (12) by $\tau_I$ gives $\tau_1 \tau_2 s^3 + (\tau_1 + \tau_2)s^2 + (1 + K K_c)s + K K_c/\tau_I = 0$. As $\tau_I \to \infty$, the last term vanishes and the equation reduces to $s[\tau_1 \tau_2 s^2 + (\tau_1 + \tau_2)s + 1 + K K_c] = 0$: the two roots of Eq. (32) plus a pole at the origin, the integrator pole in the limit. Weak integral action, meaning long $\tau_I$, is safe, and $K_{c,\mathrm{crit}}$ in Eq. (23) grows without bound as $\tau_I$ approaches its threshold from below, as Section 4 states.

Closing

Two first-order systems in series under proportional–integral control have the third-order characteristic equation of Eq. (12). The Routh–Hurwitz criterion reduces its stability to the single condition $b_1 > 0$, which gives Eq. (21). When $\tau_I \ge \tau_1 \tau_2/(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$, the loop is stable for every positive controller gain. When $\tau_I < \tau_1 \tau_2/(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$, stability ends at the critical gain of Eq. (23), where the loop oscillates at the frequency of Eq. (27) with the third pole of Eq. (28). The result is limited to the linear model of Section 1: no saturation, no dead time, positive parameters, and unity negative feedback. It gives the stability boundary, not gain or phase margins, and not the shape of the closed-loop response inside the stable region.
