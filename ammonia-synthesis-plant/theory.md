## Process description

Ammonia (NH3, where N denotes nitrogen and H denotes hydrogen) is manufactured in the reference plant by the Haber–Bosch route: hydrogen (H2) is generated from natural gas, principally methane (CH4, where C denotes carbon), by steam reforming; nitrogen (N2) is introduced with process air; carbon oxides are removed in successive purification stages; and the purified hydrogen–nitrogen mixture is reacted over an iron-based catalyst in a high-pressure synthesis loop. The Haber–Bosch reaction accounts for 96 percent of global annual ammonia production [3]. Just over 70 percent of world ammonia production uses natural gas-based steam reforming, with most of the remainder using coal gasification [2]. The abbreviation kg/h denotes kilogram per hour, the unit of mass flow rate; °C denotes degree Celsius, the unit of temperature; kPa denotes kilopascal and MPa denotes megapascal, units of pressure, with 1 MPa = 1,000 kPa and 1 bar = 100 kPa; t/d denotes tonne per day, with 1 tonne = 1,000 kg.

The overall chemistry comprises five reaction groups. Steam reforming of methane proceeds as

$$\mathrm{CH_4 + H_2O \rightarrow CO + 3\,H_2}$$

where H2O denotes water and CO denotes carbon monoxide, with the parallel overall reaction

$$\mathrm{CH_4 + 2\,H_2O \rightarrow CO_2 + 4\,H_2}$$

where CO2 denotes carbon dioxide [4]. The water–gas shift reaction,

$$\mathrm{CO + H_2O \rightarrow CO_2 + H_2}$$

converts carbon monoxide to additional hydrogen in two stages [4]. Carbon dioxide is removed by regenerative absorption (Carbon dioxide absorber section). Residual carbon oxides are converted in the methanator (Methanator section), and ammonia synthesis proceeds as

$$\mathrm{N_2 + 3\,H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\,NH_3}$$

an exothermic and equilibrium-limited reaction [3][13].

The flowsheet logic of process flow diagram PFD-102 follows the conventional sequence recorded in the patent and review literature: desulfurised hydrocarbon feed is steam-reformed in a primary reformer to a gas containing carbon oxides, hydrogen and methane at about 800 °C; the gas is reacted with air in a secondary reformer at about 1,000 °C; the cooled gas passes shift converters, a carbon dioxide removal unit and a methanator; a multi-stage compression section then feeds the make-up synthesis gas (the fresh hydrogen–nitrogen feed to the loop) to the high-pressure synthesis loop [6]. The five sections of PFD-102 correspond directly. Section A comprises feed compression (compressor C-201, preheater E-201), the primary reformer R-201, the air compressor C-202 and the secondary reformer R-202. Section B comprises the waste-heat boiler E-202, the high-temperature shift converter R-203, the intercooler E-203, the low-temperature shift converter R-204, the cooler E-204 and the condensate separator V-201. Section C comprises the carbon dioxide absorber T-201, the lean/rich exchanger E-205, the regenerator T-202 with condenser E-206, reflux drum V-202 and reboiler E-207, and the lean solvent pump P-201. Section D comprises the methanator R-205 and two-stage synthesis gas compression (compressors C-203 and C-204 with intercooler E-208) from 2,900 kPa to 15,000 kPa. Section E comprises the synthesis loop: feed preheater E-209, ammonia converter R-206 at 14,500–14,800 kPa, refrigeration cooler E-210, ammonia separator V-203 at −10 °C, recycle compressor C-205, and a purge stream of 6,000 kg/h. The loop pressure of approximately 150 bar in PFD-102 matches the typical value of around 150 bar, within a usual range of 80–300 bar, recorded for commercial synthesis loops [6].

The reference plant rates are natural gas 28,000 kg/h, process steam 42,000 kg/h and process air 52,000 kg/h as inputs, and process condensate 18,000 kg/h, carbon dioxide product 22,000 kg/h, purge gas 6,000 kg/h and liquid ammonia 76,000 kg/h as outputs; the overall balance closes at 122,000 kg/h on each side. The ammonia rate is equivalent to 1,824 t/d and to 608,000 tonne per year at 8,000 operating hours per year, the operating hours being an assumption. The App panel computes the two balances of the plant. In Part 1, the overall plant balance, the symbol $\dot{m}_{NH_3}$ denotes the liquid ammonia product rate (in kg/h), the symbol $\dot{m}_{NG}$ denotes the natural gas feed rate, the symbol $\dot{m}_{steam}$ denotes the process steam feed rate, the symbol $\dot{m}_{air}$ denotes the process air feed rate, the symbol $\dot{m}_{cond}$ denotes the process condensate rate, the symbol $\dot{m}_{CO_2}$ denotes the carbon dioxide product rate and the symbol $\dot{m}_{purge}$ denotes the purge gas rate, all in kg/h, and

$$\dot{m}_{NH_3} = \dot{m}_{NG} + \dot{m}_{steam} + \dot{m}_{air} - \dot{m}_{cond} - \dot{m}_{CO_2} - \dot{m}_{purge}$$

so that the ammonia rate is the closing stream of the overall balance. Part 2, the synthesis loop balance, is treated in the Synthesis loop behaviour section.

## Feed preparation and desulfurisation

Function: compression and preheating of natural gas to reforming pressure, and removal of sulfur compounds before any nickel or iron catalyst is contacted. PFD-102 compresses the feed from 2,800 kPa to 4,200 kPa in C-201 and preheats the feed to 380 °C in E-201, consistent with a front-end operating pressure of 25–40 bar reported for steam reforming plants [4]. All sulfur compounds must be removed from the natural gas because sulfur has an adverse effect on the catalysts used in the reforming and synthesis reactions; removal is effected by heating the gas to 400 °C and reacting hydrogen sulfide (H2S) over zinc oxide (ZnO, where Zn denotes zinc and O denotes oxygen) as

$$\mathrm{ZnO + H_2S \rightarrow ZnS + H_2O}$$

where ZnS denotes zinc sulfide, conventionally preceded by a hydrodesulfurisation bed that converts organic sulfur compounds to H2S [4][12]. Purification systems are typically designed for a sulfur slip of 0.1 parts per million by volume (ppmv, where ppmv denotes parts per million by volume) of H2S into the reformer section at end of run, with total sulfur at the zinc oxide outlet typically a few tens of parts per billion by volume (ppbv) [12]. Key issues are guard-bed breakthrough monitoring, because slip at even the ppbv level shortens the life of sensitive downstream beds, and steam-to-carbon ratio control, for which ratios of 2.5–8.0 are recorded in reforming practice [5][12]; the PFD-102 design steam-to-gas feed ratio is 42,000/28,000 = 1.5 by mass.

## Primary reformer

Function: partial conversion of the methane–steam mixture to hydrogen and carbon oxides in externally heated catalyst tubes (R-201). The principal reactions are the two reforming reactions of the Process description section [4]. Catalyst: nickel (Ni) on a refractory support, charged into externally heated tubes and heated by combustion of fuel, normally natural gas and purge gas [4]. In PFD-102 the mixed feed of 70,000 kg/h (natural gas 28,000 kg/h and steam 42,000 kg/h at 380 °C and 4,050 kPa) leaves the reformer at 800 °C and 3,800 kPa. Reported operating ranges are an outlet temperature of approximately 770–800 °C and a pressure of 25–40 bar, with conversion of about 70 percent of the hydrocarbon feed in the primary reformer [4][6]. A lower-temperature patent variant operates the primary stage at an outlet temperature under 800 °C, in the range 450–650 °C for an adiabatic pre-reforming configuration, and accepts a higher outlet methane content; the two ranges describe different configurations and are both reported here because the sources differ [5]. Key issues are tube metal temperature and creep life (Safety and environmental issues section), carbon deposition at low steam-to-carbon ratio, flame impingement and burner management, and pressure drop growth from catalyst breakage [4].

## Secondary reformer

Function: completion of reforming by partial oxidation with process air, which simultaneously supplies the nitrogen required for synthesis in the stoichiometric proportion (R-202). The principal reactions are partial combustion of a portion of the gas, represented by

$$\mathrm{2\,CH_4 + O_2 \rightarrow 2\,CO + 4\,H_2}$$

where O2 denotes oxygen, followed by continued steam reforming over the catalyst bed; the air rate is set so that the final synthesis gas has a hydrogen-to-nitrogen molar ratio of approximately 3 to 1 [4][6]. Catalyst: refractory-supported nickel or cobalt (Co), in an adiabatic vessel [5]. In PFD-102 the primary reformer effluent of 70,000 kg/h and compressed air of 52,000 kg/h (air compressor C-202, discharge 3,900 kPa) give an effluent of 122,000 kg/h at 1,000 °C and 3,600 kPa. One source records a secondary reformer outlet temperature of around 1,000 °C [6]; a second source gives a preferred outlet range of 800–950 °C with an outlet methane content of 0.2–1.0 percent on a dry basis for a low-temperature variant [5]. The discrepancy reflects different licensor configurations; PFD-102 follows the approximately 1,000 °C convention. Key issues are burner mixing and hot-spot control, refractory integrity, methane slip control (residual methane becomes a loop inert; Synthesis loop behaviour section), and air-rate control, because the air rate fixes the hydrogen-to-nitrogen ratio of the entire plant.

## Waste-heat recovery

Function: cooling of the secondary reformer effluent from 1,000 °C to the shift inlet temperature with recovery of heat as high-pressure steam and feed preheat (waste-heat boiler E-202). The gas leaving the secondary reformer is cooled with heat recovery and is not cooled below the carbon monoxide shift inlet temperature; conventional high-temperature shift inlet temperatures are 300–400 °C, especially 320–350 °C [5]. In PFD-102 the outlet of E-202 is 380 °C at 3,500 kPa, inside that range. Key issues are metal dusting and corrosion in the cooling range, thermal stress at the tube sheet, and steam-system integration, because recovered steam drives the large compressors of Sections A, D and E of the diagram.

## High-temperature shift converter

Function: conversion of the bulk of the carbon monoxide by the shift reaction of the Process description section (R-203). Catalyst: iron–chromium, specifically magnetite (Fe3O4, where Fe denotes iron) as the active phase with chromium oxide (Cr2O3) as stabiliser; the iron–chrome catalyst and the temperature programme are recorded in the primary patent source [5]. Operating range: inlet 320–350 °C, outlet typically 400–450 °C [5]. PFD-102 operates with an effluent temperature of 430 °C at 3,400 kPa, inside the recorded outlet range. The gas is then cooled in intercooler E-203 to 220 °C before the low-temperature stage. Key issues are equilibrium limitation at the outlet temperature, catalyst sensitivity to sulfur (Issues of special concern section), and temperature control, because the reaction is exothermic. A carbon monoxide content of approximately 12–15 percent on a dry basis is sometimes quoted for secondary reformer gas from secondary teaching material; the figure could not be verified against an opened primary source and is not used in the page calculations.

## Low-temperature shift converter

Function: reduction of residual carbon monoxide to a level suitable for methanation, by the same shift reaction (R-204). Catalyst: a copper-containing catalyst, in industrial practice copper (Cu) with zinc oxide and alumina (Al2O3, where Al denotes aluminium) as structural components; the copper-containing catalyst is recorded in the primary patent source [5]. Operating range: gas cooled to 200–240 °C before the bed, with an outlet temperature in the range 240–270 °C and a final carbon monoxide content of up to 0.5 percent by volume on a dry basis [5]. PFD-102 operates with an inlet of 220 °C (after E-203) and an effluent of 250 °C at 3,200 kPa, inside the recorded ranges. Key issues are condensation of steam on the catalyst, which is a stated risk at higher pressures, sulfur and chlorine poisoning of the copper catalyst, and low-temperature activity loss [5].


## Condensate separation

Function: cooling of the shifted gas to 40 °C in cooler E-204 and removal of condensed process water in separator V-201 before carbon dioxide removal; in PFD-102, 18,000 kg/h of process condensate is separated from 104,000 kg/h of dry gas, each at approximately 3,000–3,100 kPa. After low-temperature shift the gas is cooled and water, if still present, is removed before contact with the regenerable liquid absorbent used for carbon dioxide removal [5]. Key issues are condensate treatment for reuse as boiler feed water, dissolved gas content of the condensate, and level control, because carry-over of liquid into the absorber dilutes the solvent.

## Carbon dioxide absorber

Function: removal of carbon dioxide, present at approximately 18 percent in the gas downstream of the low-temperature shift converter, to a residual level that protects the synthesis catalyst (absorber T-201) [7]. Carbon dioxide is a strong catalyst poison and also reacts with ammonia in the synthesis recycle gas to form ammonium carbamate and ammonium carbonate deposits that can damage machinery [7]. Industrial solvent systems in use are: aqueous monoethanolamine (MEA) at 15–20 percent solvent content, giving a treated-gas content below 50 ppmv at high regeneration heat; activated methyldiethanolamine (aMDEA, the BASF process) at 40–50 percent methyldiethanolamine plus additive, giving 100–1,000 ppmv at low regeneration heat and normally used in ammonia plants; hot potassium carbonate processes, principally the UOP Benfield process at 25–30 percent potassium carbonate (K2CO3) plus additives with a vanadium-based corrosion inhibitor and diethanolamine as activator, giving 500–1,000 ppmv at low regeneration heat and used extensively for ammonia; the Catacarb and Giammarco-Vetrocoke hot carbonate variants; and physical solvents, principally refrigerated methanol (Rectisol, treated gas below 10 ppmv), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (Purisol) and dimethyl ether of polyethylene glycol (Selexol), which are favoured at high pressure and high acid-gas content [7]. The governing Benfield reaction in absorption is

$$\mathrm{K_2CO_3 + CO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow 2\,KHCO_3}$$

where KHCO3 denotes potassium bicarbonate, reversed in regeneration [7]. PFD-102 circulates 180,000 kg/h of lean solvent against 104,000 kg/h of gas at approximately 3,000 kPa and 45–55 °C, a circulation ratio consistent with the large solution volumes recorded for carbonate systems [7]; the absorber overhead (sweet gas) is 82,000 kg/h and the rich solvent bottoms stream is 202,000 kg/h, closing the absorber balance at 284,000 kg/h on each side. Key issues are solvent degradation and foaming, corrosion control through inhibitor concentration, water balance of the circulating solution, and regeneration steam demand, which is a major component of plant energy consumption.

## Solvent regeneration

Function: stripping of absorbed carbon dioxide from rich solvent with reboiler heat (regenerator T-202, condenser E-206, reflux drum V-202, reboiler E-207), recovery of a carbon dioxide product of 22,000 kg/h in PFD-102, available for urea manufacture or for compression and storage, and return of lean solvent through the lean/rich exchanger E-205 and pump P-201 to the absorber at 3,200 kPa. The regenerator overhead vapour of 30,000 kg/h divides in V-202 into reflux of 8,000 kg/h and the carbon dioxide product of 22,000 kg/h; the bottom liquid of 198,000 kg/h divides in E-207 into boilup vapour of 18,000 kg/h and lean solvent of 180,000 kg/h. About 130 million tonne (Mt, where Mt denotes million tonne) of carbon dioxide is utilised annually for urea production, most of it supplied from ammonia production, equivalent to about a quarter of the carbon dioxide generated from ammonia production [2]. Regeneration heat is the principal operating cost of the unit; modern energy-saving processes (activated methyldiethanolamine and promoted hot carbonate) are selected specifically to reduce that heat relative to monoethanolamine [7]. Key issues are reboiler steam supply from the waste-heat system, condenser and reflux control at the regenerator top, solvent losses, and corrosion in the rich-solvent and overhead systems.

## Methanator

Function: final purification by conversion of residual carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide, present at a fractional percentage after shift and absorption, to methane and water, rendering the carbon oxides harmless to the synthesis catalyst (R-205) [5]. The principal reactions are

$$\mathrm{CO + 3\,H_2 \rightarrow CH_4 + H_2O}$$

and

$$\mathrm{CO_2 + 4\,H_2 \rightarrow CH_4 + 2\,H_2O}$$

Catalyst: supported nickel, at an inlet temperature of 250–350 °C [5]. Promoted zinc oxide is also used as a purification absorbent upstream of methanator units to protect the nickel catalyst from sulfur [12]. PFD-102 operates at an effluent temperature of 320 °C and 2,900 kPa on a feed of 82,000 kg/h, inside the recorded inlet range when allowance is made for the reaction temperature rise. Key issues are the strongly exothermic character of both reactions, so that a carbon oxide breakthrough from the absorber produces a rapid temperature excursion; hydrogen consumption in the methanation reactions, which is a direct yield loss; and formation of methane, which is inert in the synthesis loop and must be purged (Synthesis loop behaviour section).

## Synthesis gas compression

Function: compression of purified make-up gas from 2,900 kPa to the loop pressure of 15,000 kPa in two centrifugal stages (compressors C-203 and C-204) with intercooling to 40 °C in intercooler E-208; the intermediate condition is 8,000 kPa and 120 °C after the first stage. A main compression section, usually with a multi-stage compressor, feeds the make-up gas to the high-pressure synthesis loop [6]. Modern plants use steam-turbine-driven centrifugal compressors supplied from the waste-heat steam system; the synthesis gas compressor is normally the largest single power consumer in the plant, stated qualitatively because a verified percentage split of compressor power was not found in the opened sources. Key issues are surge control, seal integrity on hydrogen service, interstage condensate and residual carbon oxide removal (molecular sieve drying after compression is used in some configurations to remove the last traces of water, carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide), and driver steam balance.

## Ammonia converter

Function: catalytic synthesis of ammonia in multiple beds with interbed cooling or quench (converter R-206). The principal reaction is the synthesis reaction of the Process description section, with a standard enthalpy change of −91.8 kilojoule per mole (kJ/mol, where kJ denotes kilojoule and mol denotes mole) of reaction as written for 2 mol of NH3 [13]. Catalyst: iron-based, most commonly magnetite (Fe3O4) as the catalyst precursor, reduced in service to porous iron, with aluminium, potassium and calcium oxides (Al2O3, K2O, where K denotes potassium, and CaO, where Ca denotes calcium) as promoters that increase thermal stability and prevent sintering; ruthenium-based catalysts are the principal alternative and are disfavoured at large scale by cost [13]. Form: fused, crushed and screened granules charged in radial-flow or axial-flow beds. Operating range: industrial Haber–Bosch synthesis is typically performed at 450–600 °C (723–873 K, where K denotes kelvin) and 150–400 bar in the review source [3], and at 300–500 °C and 100–250 bar in the patent-landscape source [13]; the ranges differ because the second source includes low-pressure designs, for which a synthesis pressure of 40–120 bar with a catalyst outlet temperature of 300–450 °C and an ammonia content of 10–15 percent by volume in the reacted gas is recorded [5]. PFD-102 operates at 14,200–14,800 kPa (142–148 bar) with a feed temperature of 220 °C after E-209 and an effluent temperature of 450 °C, inside all three recorded ranges. Key issues are equilibrium limitation per pass (Synthesis loop behaviour section), catalyst poisoning by oxygen compounds (water, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide) and sulfur, bed temperature profiling, and pressure-vessel integrity in hydrogen service at temperature.

## Refrigeration and separation

Function: cooling of converter effluent, after feed preheating in E-209 (effluent 292,000 kg/h cooled from 450 °C to 150 °C), to −10 °C in refrigeration cooler E-210 so that ammonia condenses and is separated in separator V-203 as liquid product of 76,000 kg/h, while unreacted gas of 216,000 kg/h passes to recycle. Separation of ammonia from unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen is typically achieved by condensation [3]. Recovery by condensation using moderate refrigeration, for example between +2 °C and −10 °C, is recorded for low-pressure synthesis designs [5]; PFD-102 adopts the −10 °C end of that range, with refrigeration supplied by an ammonia refrigeration cycle in which the product itself is the refrigerant. Key issues are refrigeration compressor duty, which rises steeply as separator temperature falls; ammonia slip in the recycle gas, which inhibits the converter; and two-phase flow and relief design in the separator system (Safety and environmental issues section).

## Recycle and purge

Function: recompression of the separator vapour fraction assigned to recycle (210,000 kg/h in PFD-102) in recycle compressor C-205 from 14,100 kPa to 15,000 kPa and return to the converter feed junction, with continuous withdrawal of a purge stream of 6,000 kg/h to reject inerts. The fresh feed of 82,000 kg/h and the recycle discharge of 210,000 kg/h combine at the junction to the mixed feed of 292,000 kg/h. The quantitative behaviour of the loop, including the inert balance that fixes the purge rate, is treated in the Synthesis loop behaviour section.


## Synthesis loop behaviour

Single-pass conversion is equilibrium-limited. Under industrial conditions a 3 to 1 hydrogen-to-nitrogen mixture reacted over an iron-based catalyst gives a single-pass conversion in the range 15–25 percent, and recycling of unreacted gas raises the overall conversion to approach 97 percent [3]. An independent patent source records an ammonia content of 10–15 percent by volume in reacted synthesis gas for low-pressure designs, consistent with the lower part of that conversion range [5].

The loop balance of the App panel (Part 2) is defined as follows. The symbol $\dot{m}_{fresh}$ denotes the fresh synthesis gas rate (in kg/h), the symbol $\dot{m}_{recycle}$ denotes the recycle gas rate (in kg/h), the symbol $\dot{m}_{feed}$ denotes the converter feed rate (in kg/h), the symbol $\dot{m}_{product}$ denotes the liquid ammonia product rate (in kg/h), the symbol $\dot{m}_{vap}$ denotes the separator vapour rate (in kg/h) and the symbol $\dot{m}_{purge}$ denotes the loop purge rate (in kg/h), the purge symbol having been defined for the overall balance in the Process description section with the same meaning. The converter feed rate is

$$\dot{m}_{feed} = \dot{m}_{fresh} + \dot{m}_{recycle}$$

the purge rate follows from the steady-state loop balance, in which the loop receives the fresh rate and discharges the product and purge rates only,

$$\dot{m}_{purge} = \dot{m}_{fresh} - \dot{m}_{product}$$

and the separator vapour rate is

$$\dot{m}_{vap} = \dot{m}_{feed} - \dot{m}_{product}$$

dividing at the split point into recycle gas and purge gas. The recycle ratio $R$ (dimensionless) is defined as $R = \dot{m}_{recycle} / \dot{m}_{fresh}$, the circulation ratio (dimensionless) as $\dot{m}_{feed} / \dot{m}_{fresh}$, and the ammonia formation per pass $X$ (in mass per cent) as $X = 100 \, \dot{m}_{product} / \dot{m}_{feed}$, a mass-basis measure formed from the loop streams of the design basis and not a molar single-pass conversion. At the PFD-102 design basis the recycle ratio is 210,000/82,000 = 2.56, the circulation ratio is 292,000/82,000 = 3.56 and the formation per pass is 26.0 mass per cent, each derived from the design basis rates.

Inerts enter the loop from two sources: residual methane from the reforming section (methane slip from the secondary reformer and methane formed in the methanator) and argon (Ar) introduced with the process air. Make-up gas contains a small content, usually around 1 mol percent (mol %), of non-reacting components termed inerts, mainly methane and argon [6]. Because neither component reacts or condenses, both accumulate in the circulating gas until the purge rate balances the inlet rate. The inert concentration is controlled by withdrawal of a purge stream, typically to 5–20 mol % inerts in the circulating gas [6]. Higher inert levels reduce the partial pressures of hydrogen and nitrogen, shift the synthesis equilibrium unfavourably, and increase circulation and compression cost; lower inert levels require a larger purge and lose more hydrogen. The purge gas contains a substantial quantity of hydrogen and nitrogen, typically 5–10 percent of the total hydrogen and nitrogen content of the make-up gas, and is generally used as fuel, with membrane hydrogen recovery as the established alternative [6]. In PFD-102 the purge is 6,000 kg/h, equal to 2.8 percent of the separator vapour and 7.3 percent of the fresh make-up gas, both derived from the design basis and consistent in magnitude with the recorded 5–10 percent reactant loss range [6].

## Production scale

United States production. Ammonia was produced by 18 companies at 37 plants in 17 states during 2024, with 2 additional plants idle for the entire year; about 55 percent of total United States ammonia production capacity was in Louisiana, Oklahoma and Texas, and actively producing plants operated at about 80 percent of rated capacity [1]. United States production was 14,000 thousand metric tons of nitrogen content in 2024 (estimated), against 13,800 thousand metric tons of nitrogen content in 2023 [1]. The source reports production on a nitrogen-content basis; conversion at the stoichiometric nitrogen mass fraction of ammonia (14.007/17.031 = 0.822) gives approximately 17,000 thousand metric tons of ammonia product, a derived value. Approximately 88 percent of domestic ammonia production was for fertilizer use, including anhydrous ammonia for direct application, urea, ammonium nitrates, ammonium phosphates and other nitrogen compounds, with the remainder used for explosives, plastics, synthetic fibres and resins and other chemical compounds [1].

World production. The world total in the same source was 150,000 thousand metric tons of nitrogen content in 2024 (estimated), against 152,000 thousand metric tons of nitrogen content in 2023, with China the largest producer at 47,000 thousand metric tons of nitrogen content, followed by India at 15,000, the United States at 14,000 and Russia at 14,000 thousand metric tons of nitrogen content [1]. On a product-mass basis the sources differ in basis and year: 176 Mt of ammonia for the Haber–Bosch total in the review source [3]; approximately 183 Mt produced in 2020 in the energy-systems source [8]; and approximately 200 Mt produced and consumed annually, attributed to the International Fertilizer Association (IFA), in the industry source [9]. The range 176–200 Mt is reported because the sources disagree in year and in accounting basis; the USGS (United States Geological Survey) nitrogen-content total of 150 Mt of nitrogen corresponds to approximately 182 Mt of ammonia product, inside that range (derived). The International Energy Agency (IEA) records that about 70 percent of ammonia is used for fertilisers, with the remainder in industrial applications such as plastics, explosives and synthetic fibres [2], whereas the United States figure is 88 percent for fertilizer use [1] and a patent-landscape review states around 80 percent for the fertilizer industry [13]; the three figures are reported together because they refer to different populations (world, United States, and a secondary review estimate). Single-stream plant capacity has risen to 3,300 t/d in recent designs, with a 4,000 t/d single-stream plant reported [4]; typical plant capacities generally exceed 1,000 t/d [3]. The PFD-102 reference plant at 1,824 t/d sits in the middle of the recorded commercial range.

## Process economics

Energy consumption. The global average energy intensity of ammonia production is around 41 gigajoule per tonne (GJ/t, where GJ denotes gigajoule) on a net basis, compared with best available technology (BAT) levels of 28 GJ/t for natural gas-based production and 36 GJ/t for coal-based production [2]. An independent review reports present-day consumption of around 30 GJ/t, reduced from over 60 GJ/t in the 1950s [3], and an energy-systems review tabulates 28–36 GJ/t for steam methane reforming (SMR) and autothermal reforming (ATR) without carbon capture and storage (CCS), with 33 GJ/t for SMR with CCS and 29 GJ/t for ATR with CCS [8]. A higher figure of 46 GJ/t appears in the same energy-systems source as an assumed average specific energy consumption including fossil fuel feedstock for a 2019 inventory [8]. The figures are not contradictory: the BAT and tabulated values describe efficient gas-based plants on a stated boundary, whereas 41 GJ/t is a global fleet average including coal-based production and 46 GJ/t is an inventory assumption. At the BAT figure of 28 GJ/t, the PFD-102 reference plant would consume approximately 51,072 GJ per day (derived: 28 GJ/t multiplied by 1,824 t/d).

Capital cost. A directly comparable published capital cost for a conventional natural gas-based plant, with capacity and year of estimate stated in an opened authoritative source, could not be verified in the dossier sources and is therefore not stated as a figure on this page. Verified project figures on stated bases are: a low-carbon (blue) ammonia facility at Ingleside, Texas, using autothermal reforming with carbon capture, with total project investment expected in the range USD 2.6–2.9 billion (USD denotes United States dollar) for a capacity of 1.2–1.4 million tons per annum and production start-up in 2027/2028, announced in 2023 [10]; and a new ammonia plant project in Louisiana announced by CF Industries at USD 3.7 billion, described by the company as the largest new ammonia plant in the world by nameplate capacity, with construction expected to begin in the year of the statement and start-up expected in 2029, the capacity figure itself not being stated in the opened source [9]. Industry context from the same source: approximately USD 500 million per year of sustaining capital across one major producer's existing network, and a construction period of at least 3–4 years for new nitrogen capacity [9]. Economy of scale is documented quantitatively in two forms: capital cost increases by only 50–60 percent when design output is doubled [4], and the synthesis plant plus air separation unit represents approximately USD 0.5 billion of a USD 7 billion fully integrated renewable ammonia system per million tonne per year of capacity, the balance being renewable generation, electrolysers and storage [8]. The second figure is a green-ammonia system breakdown and is not a conventional plant cost.

Operating cost structure. Natural gas dominates. For many producers globally, more than 70 percent of the variable cost to produce ammonia is from the cost of natural gas [9]. On a total production cost basis, an independent assessment estimates grey ammonia production cost at USD 175–810 per ton across a gas price range of USD 1–15 per GJ, with a global average of USD 470 per ton; energy costs account for just below 60 percent of total grey ammonia cost, split between 45 percent gas and 13 percent electricity, and hydrogen production accounts for 70 percent of total production cost [8]. The two gas shares (over 70 percent of variable cost; 45 percent of total cost) differ because the denominators differ, and both are reported. The same assessment estimates blue ammonia (steam methane reforming route) at around USD 600 per ton global average and green ammonia at USD 400–1,670 per ton, global average USD 730 per ton [8].

Price context. The average Gulf Coast ammonia price for 2024 was estimated at USD 440 per short ton (free on board), with the weekly average price at USD 478 per short ton at the beginning of 2024, USD 364 per short ton in late May and USD 510 per short ton in late September [1]. Converted at 1 short ton = 0.907 metric tonne, the 2024 average corresponds to approximately USD 485 per metric tonne (derived). The Henry Hub spot natural gas price in the United States ranged between USD 1.25 and USD 3.25 per million British thermal units (MMBtu) for most of 2024, with an average of about USD 2.10 per MMBtu [1]. During the 2022 gas price shock, global spot ammonia prices reached USD 1,300 per ton in the first quarter of 2022, after a 2017–2020 range of USD 200–400 per ton [8].

## Safety and environmental issues

Hydrogen and high-pressure hazards. The front end operates at up to approximately 4,200 kPa with hydrogen-rich gas at up to 1,000 °C, and the synthesis loop operates at approximately 15,000 kPa. Failure experience concentrates in the reforming section and the synthesis loop, which are recorded as the major areas of failure in ammonia plants [4]. Hydrogen attack (decarburisation of steel in high-temperature hydrogen service) is a documented degradation mechanism in ammonia synthesis equipment [4].

Ammonia toxicity and release behaviour. Anhydrous ammonia is a highly toxic substance. In the 31 July 2024 release at a food processing facility in Sterling, Virginia, an overpressure in a surge drum discharged approximately 275 pound of anhydrous ammonia through an emergency pressure relief valve; the release occurred in two-phase flow, producing a dense toxic ammonia cloud that rapidly settled at ground level, with modelled ground-level concentrations at levels classified as Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health (IDLH, 300 parts per million as cited in the investigation reporting); 33 people were transported to hospitals, four were admitted including one to intensive care, and property damage including production losses was estimated at USD 3 million [11]. The investigation found that the ammonia detection system did not trigger alarms, no evacuation signal was sounded and no emergency shutdown was initiated [11]. The incident is a refrigeration release rather than a synthesis plant release; it is cited for release behaviour (two-phase discharge, dense ground-hugging cloud, dependence on detection and evacuation) and not for synthesis-specific frequencies; a synthesis-specific incident frequency figure could not be verified in the opened sources and none is stated.

Reformer tube failure. Numerous incidents of reformer tube failure and fire in ammonia plants are on record. Documented mechanisms are creep rupture from overheating, including overheating caused by choked flow from damaged catalyst, flame impingement from partially blocked burner tips, stress corrosion cracking at weld heat-affected zones, and carburisation and oxidation during service ageing [4]. Catalyst tubes are manufactured from cast high-alloy steels selected for creep resistance, including modified HK 40 and micro-alloyed HP grade (35 nickel–25 chromium–1 niobium–titanium alloy, in percent) [4]. One recorded case involved seven tubes of modified HK 40 steel ruptured after 4 years of service during plant start-up, with overheating identified as the dominant factor [4]. Low steam flow to the reformer is a recognised precursor: insufficient steam raises carbon deposition and the risk of tube cracking, and steam flow measurement to the reformer is therefore protected by safety-instrumented functions in modern plants [4].

Catalyst hazards. Reduced nickel catalysts (reforming, methanation) and the reduced iron synthesis catalyst are air-reactive after reduction and require controlled passivation before vessel entry or catalyst discharge; the copper low-temperature shift catalyst is similarly air-sensitive. The hazard is stated qualitatively; a quantitative incident record specific to catalyst handling could not be verified in the opened sources and none is stated. Carbonyl formation (nickel carbonyl from nickel catalysts in carbon monoxide service at low temperature) is a recognised additional hazard in methanation service; no verified quantitative exposure figure was found and none is stated.

Carbon dioxide emissions. Direct emissions from ammonia production amount to 450 Mt of carbon dioxide per year, with indirect emissions of around 170 Mt per year; at around 2.4 tonne of carbon dioxide per tonne of production on a direct basis, ammonia is nearly twice as emissions-intensive as crude steel and four times that of cement [2]. Source-specific figures differ by boundary: fuel-related direct emissions for efficient steam methane reforming are tabulated at 1.51–2.02 tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2-eq) per tonne, with cradle-to-factory-gate totals of 2.29–4.27 tonne CO2-eq per tonne for steam methane reforming and autothermal reforming without carbon capture and storage once electricity and methane emissions are included [8]. Ammonia production accounts for around 2 percent (8.6 exajoule) of total final energy consumption, and just over 70 percent of production is gas-based, corresponding to 170 billion cubic metre of natural gas demand [2]. At the fleet-average direct intensity of 2.4 tonne per tonne, the PFD-102 reference plant would emit approximately 4,378 tonne of carbon dioxide per day before any capture (derived: 2.4 multiplied by 1,824 t/d); the process carbon dioxide stream separated in T-202 (22,000 kg/h, approximately 528 tonne per day, derived) is the concentrated fraction available for urea manufacture or storage at lowest capture cost [2].

## Issues of special concern

Catalyst poisoning by sulfur. Sulfur is the defining feedstock contaminant. All sulfur compounds must be removed upstream of reforming because sulfur adversely affects the reforming and synthesis catalysts [4]; the poisoned units include pre-reforming, steam reforming and low-temperature shift catalysts [12]. The protection system (hydrodesulfurisation followed by zinc oxide absorption) is designed for 0.1 ppmv hydrogen sulfide slip at end of run, with ultra-purification stages reducing total sulfur to below 10 ppbv where highly stressed reformers or pre-reformers are installed [12]. Documented operating experience records severe deactivation of a methanation catalyst protected by a non-promoted zinc oxide absorbent, eliminated after installation of a promoted zinc oxide absorbent, and a doubling of steam reforming catalyst life after installation of an ultra-purification stage [12]. Oxygen compounds are the parallel concern at the synthesis converter: carbon dioxide is a strong catalyst poison for the synthesis catalyst and forms carbamate deposits with recycle ammonia [7], which is the reason the absorber–methanator sequence must hold carbon oxides at the ppmv level.

Feedstock price sensitivity. Production cost is approximately linear in gas price on the evidence of the cost assessment in the Process economics section: grey ammonia cost spans USD 175–810 per ton over a gas price range of USD 1–15 per GJ [8]. The 2022 European gas price increase shuttered or curtailed 50 percent of European ammonia manufacturing capacity in the first half of 2022 [8], and the United States Gulf Coast price record shows a threefold swing between the 2021 average (USD 578 per short ton) and the 2022 average (USD 1,070 per short ton), returning to USD 440 per short ton in 2024 [1]. Plant location therefore follows low-cost gas: 55 percent of United States capacity is concentrated in Louisiana, Oklahoma and Texas [1], and low-cost gas in the United States, Middle East and Russia explains the prominent production roles of those regions [2].

Carbon capture integration. Carbon dioxide separation is an inherent part of commercial ammonia production, but permanent storage is not yet widely adopted [2]. Capture from the concentrated process stream requires principally compression, whereas capture from the dilute reformer flue gas requires additional capture equipment; if both streams are captured and stored, steam methane reforming production becomes near-zero-emission depending on the capture rate realised, with an optimum capture rate of 85–90 percent at current amine capture costs and up to around 99 percent technically achievable [2]. The autothermal reforming configuration is better suited to capture because combustion and reforming occur in a single reactor giving a single concentrated stream, with an effective capture rate above 90 percent, an energy consumption of 29 GJ/t with carbon capture and storage and emissions of 0.65–0.70 tonne CO2-eq per tonne in the cited assessment [8]. The Texas blue ammonia project of the Process economics section anticipates capture of approximately 95 percent of the carbon dioxide generated, with transport to permanent geologic storage [10].

Green ammonia context. Green ammonia denotes ammonia produced from hydrogen obtained by water electrolysis powered by renewable electricity, with nitrogen from air separation, retaining the Haber–Bosch synthesis loop. Replacing natural gas with hydropower electrolysis as the hydrogen source would reduce carbon dioxide emissions by an estimated 75 percent [3]. Near-zero-emission routes are typically 10–100 percent more expensive per tonne than conventional routes, depending on energy prices and regional factors, and announced near-zero-emission capacity totalling nearly 8 Mt was scheduled to come online by 2030, equivalent to 3 percent of total 2020 capacity [2]. Cost assessment places green ammonia at USD 400–1,670 per ton (global average USD 730 per ton), competitive with grey ammonia only in regions combining good wind and solar resources with a cost of capital below 8 percent [8]. The principal technical constraints are operation of the synthesis loop on variable hydrogen supply, for which milder-condition catalysts and replacement of condensation separation by absorption are active research directions [3].
