1. Reading the Register

Each entry names one problem type, states why model training and reasoning are weak for it, gives an example problem and a best-attempt solution with every step computed, and states the plausible range of the headline result. Ratings follow the scale below; percentages are plausible errors for a single unaided answer.

RatingMeaning
ModeratePlausible error of roughly 10 to 25 percent.
LowPlausible error of roughly 25 to 100 percent, or a wrong convention or correlation may be selected.
Very lowError by a factor of two or more, a wrong flow regime, relief scenario, or model form is plausible. The headline result is an order-of-magnitude statement.

Three kinds of uncertainty are kept separate; the corrective action differs by kind. Propagation uncertainty: spread from a stated input range. Model-form uncertainty: spread from a choice between defensible published models, conventions, or correlations. Scenario uncertainty: the solved problem may not govern in practice, for example a relief case other than the case sized. Entry ranges are labelled by kind.

Status of numerical values. Property values, correlation outputs, and heuristic factors in the examples are rounded illustrative values from published ranges, chosen to make the uncertainty arithmetic transparent. The values are not design data. Before engineering use, each must be replaced from a primary databank, a vendor document, or the current governing code edition (see each entry's check). The uncertainty ranges are insensitive to this rounding.

Standing commitment. The register is recorded in standing memory. A future answer depending on a listed type must carry the qualification, give a range rather than a single value where the entry so indicates, and name the primary source for checking. Agreement between two AI models on a listed type is not verification (Section 2).

2. Mechanisms of Training-Data Weakness

Absence of industrial data. Plant operating data, vendor ratings, and inspection and troubleshooting records are confidential and enter training text in small quantity. Engineers' experience — which services foul, foam, plug, or corrode; what cannot be started up as drawn; what will be bypassed — is recorded rarely and unsystematically.

Restricted codes and databanks. Design codes, recommended practices, and commercial property databanks are restricted. Secondary sources summarise their values, often without the conditions, exclusions, and edition date that determine applicability. Training weights the value above its conditions.

Definitional conflict. Several quantities have more than one accepted definition. Henry's law constants appear under reciprocal conventions; equilibrium constants differ by standard state; overall and stage efficiencies are interchanged in secondary sources. A model adopting a number without its defining sentence errs; its source does not.

Propagation through secondary sources. Reviews, textbooks, lecture material, and solution sites copy values from earlier sources. A copied error gains repetition, and repetition is a training signal. Corrections and retractions are separate documents, not reliably attached to the original.

Locally fitted parameters quoted as constants. Kinetic and phase-equilibrium parameters are often fitted to a narrow data set and published without covariance, confidence interval, or range. Quoted later without its range, the pair becomes an apparent universal constant.

Simulation output at the weight of measurement. Simulator results are published as findings, with thermodynamic method and validation status stated incompletely or only in supplementary material. In training text, such results have the form of measured results.

Loss of graphical information. Capacity charts, compressor maps, phase diagrams, and packed-column charts carry values plotted. Text extraction keeps captions and prose, and loses the values. The model retains a relationship's shape and an approximate magnitude.

Undated and superseded material. Cost data, emission factors, materials practice, and superseded correlations remain in the record unmarked. Material from different decades is textually equivalent.

Consequence for cross-checking. Models trained on the same public record share the absences, copied errors, and definitional conflicts above. Cross-checking removes independent arithmetic and algebra errors; it does not remove a shared absence or copied value.

3. Notation

Symbols are defined at first use in each entry, with SI units where applicable; SI is the International System of Units. The symbol % denotes percent. A range written with to between two values denotes that interval. Factor plus a number denotes the ratio of the larger to the smaller value in a range. AI denotes artificial intelligence. Headline result denotes the single numerical answer an entry requests.

Part A. Physical properties and phase equilibrium

A1. Henry's Law Conventions

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form, with a small propagation component.

Problem. Estimate the equilibrium dissolved-oxygen concentration in water at 25 degrees Celsius in contact with air. The partial pressure of oxygen, denoted p, in atmospheres (atm), is 0.21 atm. The solubility-form Henry constant, denoted kH, in moles per litre per atmosphere (mol/(L·atm)), is 1.3×10−3 mol/(L·atm). The molar mass of oxygen, denoted M, in grams per mole (g/mol), is 32 g/mol.

Best attempt. Concentration, in moles per litre: kH × p = 1.3×10−3 × 0.21 = 2.7×10−4 mol/L. Multiplied by M and by 1,000 milligrams per gram: 8.7 mg/L. Published kH values of 1.1×10−3 to 1.4×10−3 mol/(L·atm) give 7.4 to 9.4 mg/L (−15% to +8%).

Why the confidence is low. The constant appears in at least four forms, including a volatility form reciprocal to the solubility form; its reciprocal here is 769 L·atm/mol. Secondary sources omit the defining sentence: the number can be retrieved without its convention and the calculation inverted — an error of orders of magnitude, not percent.

Experienced check. State the convention in words beside every Henry constant; test against air-saturated water (8 to 9 mg/L at 25 degrees Celsius).

Defensive prompt. "State the Henry's law convention used, in words and in an equation with units, and verify the result against one known saturation concentration."

A2. Non-Ideal Vapour–Liquid Equilibrium

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form.

Problem. Estimate the bubble-point pressure at fixed temperature for a strongly non-ideal binary liquid. The liquid mole fraction of component 1, denoted x1, is 0.40. The pure-component vapour pressures, denoted P1sat and P2sat, in kilopascals (kPa), are 120 kPa and 50 kPa.

Best attempt. Raoult's law gives the bubble pressure P, in kPa: x1P1sat + (1 − x1)P2sat = 78 kPa. With activity coefficients γ1 and γ2, dimensionless, of 1.6 and 1.2 from one published fit: P = x1γ1P1sat + (1 − x1)γ2P2sat = 112.8 kPa, 45% higher. A second fit (γ1 from 1.4 to 2.0, γ2 from 1.1 to 1.35) gives 100 to 136 kPa.

Why the confidence is low. Activity-coefficient parameters are pair parameters fitted to one data set over one temperature range. Training text gives a model name (Wilson, NRTL, UNIQUAC, predictive methods) without the pair, data set, or range; fitted and predictive methods differ by the spread calculated here, and returning one pressure conceals the selection. Electrolyte, associating, and near-azeotrope systems widen it.

Experienced check. Require the parameter source, data set, and temperature range behind any such value; test against one measured point for the actual mixture before use.

Defensive prompt. "Give the model, its parameter source and data range, and bubble pressure under at least two defensible models. If they differ by more than 10%, report the range, not a single value."

A3. Reactive Absorption Capacity

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form and scenario.

Problem. Size the solvent circulation for removal of carbon dioxide at 100 kilomoles per hour (kmol/h) by an aqueous amine solvent. Loading, denoted α, in moles of carbon dioxide per mole of amine (mol/mol), is 0.10 mol/mol in the lean solvent leaving the regenerator.

Best attempt. Stoichiometric estimate: rich loading α of 0.45 mol/mol gives net capacity 0.35 mol/mol and circulation 100 / 0.35 = 286 kmol/h. An equilibrium-limited rich loading of 0.30 mol/mol at absorber conditions gives net capacity 0.20 mol/mol and circulation 500 kmol/h, a factor of 1.75 higher. Circulation sets pump duty, regenerator duty, and column diameter: the factor propagates through the design.

Why the confidence is very low. Operating capacity follows equilibrium at absorber conditions, kinetics for tertiary and hindered amines, degradation and heat-stable salts, and a corrosion limit differing by amine and metallurgy. Textbooks give nominal capacity; vendor and operator limits are proprietary. Nominal loading therefore understates circulation, reboiler duty, and energy by that factor or more.

Experienced check. Require rich and lean loadings measured or simulated at absorber and regenerator conditions for the named amine and concentration, plus the supplier's corrosion limit.

Defensive prompt. "Do not use nominal amine capacity. State rich and lean loadings at the stated conditions, the source of each, the corrosion limit, and circulation at both optimistic and equilibrium-limited loadings."

A4. Estimated Properties and Compressibility

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form and propagation.

Problem. Estimate the density of a gas with molar mass M, in kilograms per mole (kg/mol), of 0.020 kg/mol, at pressure P of 150 bar and temperature T of 350 kelvin (K). The universal gas constant, denoted R, in joules per mole per kelvin (J/(mol·K)), is 8.314 J/(mol·K).

Best attempt. The ideal-gas density, denoted ρ, in kilograms per cubic metre (kg/m3), is PM/(RT) = 103.1 kg/m3. With compressibility factor Z, dimensionless, of 0.85 from a generalised chart: ρ = PM/(ZRT) = 121.3 kg/m3. Chart and equation-of-state estimates of Z from 0.78 to 0.92 (critical properties themselves estimated) give ρ from 112.1 to 132.2 kg/m3. The ideal-gas value is 15% below the central estimate.

Why the confidence is low. For a pure, well-characterised gas the calculation is reliable. For a mixture of uncertain composition, a heavy fraction on a pseudocritical rule, or group-contribution critical properties, every chart input is estimated; the chart itself averages over fluids. The error in estimated critical properties, omitted in training text, can exceed the spread calculated here for heavy or polar compounds. Density errors propagate into line sizing, compressor work, and relief calculations.

Experienced check. Require each critical property identified as measured or estimated, with method; compare a cubic equation-of-state density with the chart value before use.

Defensive prompt. "State whether each critical property is measured or estimated, give density by two independent methods, and report the spread."

A5. Crystallisation Yield

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation and scenario.

Problem. Estimate the crystal yield from cooling a saturated solution from 60 to 20 degrees Celsius, per 100 grams (g) of solvent. Solubility, in grams of solute per 100 g of solvent, is 30 at 60 degrees Celsius and 12 at 20 degrees Celsius in one data set.

Best attempt. Yield is the solubility difference: 18 g per 100 g of solvent, a recovery of 60% of dissolved solute. Two data sets for comparable systems (hot solubility 25 to 35, cold solubility 9 to 16, same units) give yields of 9 to 26 g and recoveries of 36% to 74%.

Why the confidence is very low. Solid solubility depends on the polymorph crystallising, hydrate or solvate formation, trace impurities absent from the reported system, and analytical method. The metastable zone width — whether crystals are filterable or fines — is not an equilibrium property, and depends on seeding, agitation, and cooling rate. Plant yields lose material to mother-liquor retention in the cake.

Experienced check. Require solubility measured for the actual feed, impurities included, with solid form identified; and a metastable-zone measurement or pilot crystallisation before committing yield or crystal size.

Defensive prompt. "Give yield as a range over at least two solubility sources; identify the solid form assumed; state what equilibrium cannot determine: metastable zone width, crystal size, and mother-liquor loss."

A6. Polymer Viscosity

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form.

Problem. Estimate the intrinsic viscosity of a polymer in solution from the Mark–Houwink relation, in which intrinsic viscosity, denoted [η], in decilitres per gram (dL/g), equals a constant K, in dL/g, multiplied by the viscosity-average molar mass M, in grams per mole (g/mol), raised to an exponent a, dimensionless. Given: K = 3×10−4 dL/g and M = 100,000 g/mol.

Best attempt. With a = 0.65 (flexible polymer, good solvent), [η] = 0.53 dL/g. Published exponents span 0.55 to 0.80; holding K fixed gives [η] from 0.17 to 3.00 dL/g, a factor of 18.

Why the confidence is very low. K and a are a fitted pair for a named polymer, solvent, temperature, and molar-mass range and average. Secondary sources quote one without the other, or a number-average-fitted pair for use with viscosity-average mass. Since M enters as a power, a modest exponent error is amplified by the size of M. Polydispersity, branching, and solvent quality shift the result comparably; a single value here is unusable.

Experienced check. Require K and a as a pair, with polymer, solvent, temperature, molar-mass range, and the fit average; refuse a calculation whose pair and supplied mass differ in type.

Defensive prompt. "Use only a Mark–Houwink pair quoted with its polymer, solvent, temperature, and molar-mass average. Report the result at the ends of the exponent range and at the central value."

Part B. Kinetics and reactors

B1. Arrhenius Extrapolation

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form, from parameter correlation in the source fit.

Problem. Estimate a first-order rate constant at 400 kelvin (K) from kinetics measured between 300 K and 330 K. Rate constant k, in reciprocal seconds (1/s), is 0.010 1/s at 315 K. Activation energy Ea, in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol), is reported as 80 kJ/mol. The gas constant R, in joules per mole per kelvin (J/(mol·K)), is 8.314 J/(mol·K).

Best attempt. Anchored at 315 K, k at 400 K is 0.010 times the exponential of (−Ea/R)(1/400 − 1/315): at Ea = 80 kJ/mol, 6.59 1/s. Two pairs fitting the same 30 K data (Ea 70 and 95 kJ/mol) give 2.93 1/s and 22.3 1/s — a factor of 7.6 between extrapolated values.

Why the confidence is very low. Over a narrow interval, Ea and the pre-exponential factor correlate: a higher Ea with a compensating higher pre-exponential factor fits the data. Papers report the pair without covariance; secondary sources quote Ea alone. Extrapolation by 70 K converts that correlation into the factor calculated here. Mechanism change, pore diffusion, or phase change across the interval adds error no range on Ea captures.

Experienced check. Require the source temperature range; refuse extrapolation beyond it without validation; require a rate near the design temperature before sizing.

Defensive prompt. "State the kinetic data temperature range, extrapolation distance, and rate constant at both ends of the plausible activation-energy range. Do not give a single extrapolated value."

B2. Catalyst Deactivation

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: scenario and propagation.

Problem. Estimate conversion after 180 days on stream in a first-order catalytic reactor. The product of fresh rate constant and residence time, dimensionless, is 2.0 (fresh conversion 0.865). Catalyst activity a, dimensionless, initial value 1, decays exponentially with deactivation constant kd, in reciprocal days (1/day), of 0.004 1/day.

Best attempt. At 180 days, a = exp(−0.004 × 180) = 0.49; conversion, as 1 − exp(−2.0a), is 0.622. Published constants for comparable services, 0.002 to 0.008 1/day, give activities 0.70 to 0.24 and conversions 0.377 to 0.752 after the same period.

Why the confidence is very low. Deactivation follows feed poisons at parts-per-million level, thermal excursions, coking, and sintering; the cause differs by unit and feed slate, often identified only at change-out. Vendor guarantees are commercial documents. A generic decay constant gives the run-length error calculated here, growing with time, and no indication of the cause that will end the run.

Experienced check. Require the deactivation basis for the named catalyst and feed: poison limits and the temperature policy (start-of-run and end-of-run) behind the quoted life.

Defensive prompt. "State the assumed mechanism, the feed poison limits on which life depends, and conversion at both ends of the plausible deactivation-constant range. Identify what event would end the run earlier."

B3. Reactor Heat Removal

Confidence: Low for area; very low for stability margin. Kind of uncertainty: propagation for area; scenario for stability.

Problem. Size the cooling area for a reactor releasing heat at rate Q, in kilowatts (kW), of 500 kW, with mean temperature difference between reaction mass and coolant, denoted ΔT, in kelvin (K), of 25 K.

Best attempt. With overall heat-transfer coefficient U, in watts per square metre per kelvin (W/(m2·K)), of 500 W/(m2·K): area A, in square metres (m2), is Q/(UΔT) = 40.0 m2. Published coefficients of 300 to 800 W/(m2·K) (agitated, possibly fouling or viscous services) give areas of 25.0 to 66.7 m2. Adiabatic rise for the stated inventory (reaction energy 2.5×106 kilojoules (kJ), mass 5,000 kilograms (kg), specific heat capacity 2.5 kJ/(kg·K)): 200 K.

Why the confidence is low. The area inherits the uncertainty of U: viscosity, fouling, gas evolution, and agitation regime decisive, known only from pilot or plant measurement. Stability is separate: whether heat generation, rising exponentially, crosses heat removal, rising approximately linearly, depends on the slopes and on response to coolant failure, feed excursion, or loss of agitation. A steady-state solution can carry no usable margin.

Experienced check. Require heat-generation and heat-removal curves over the full range, a basis for U in service, and a response to loss of cooling and of agitation.

Defensive prompt. "Give area at both ends of the plausible coefficient range, the adiabatic rise, and the temperature at which heat generation exceeds maximum heat removal. State the consequence of loss of cooling."

B4. Mixing Scale-Up

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form, from mutually inconsistent published criteria.

Problem. Scale up a stirred vessel from 0.1 to 10 cubic metres (m3): volume ratio 100 and, under geometric similarity, length ratio 4.64. Laboratory agitator speed N, in revolutions per minute (rpm), is 300 rpm; power input is 50 watts (W).

Best attempt. Three criteria give three plant speeds: constant power per unit volume, 108 rpm (plant power 5.0 kilowatts (kW)); constant impeller tip speed, 65 rpm (plant power 1.08 kW); constant Reynolds number, 14 rpm. The first two, both routine, differ in plant power by a factor near five, with different blend times, gas dispersion, and solids suspension.

Why the confidence is very low. The criteria are not approximations to one result: each preserves a different quantity and sacrifices the others; choice depends on the limiting phenomenon (mass transfer, suspension, blending, heat transfer, shear sensitivity). Guidance is application-specific, vendor and pilot experience with named impellers and geometries. Selecting a criterion without that phenomenon returns precise speed and power for a vessel of undetermined performance.

Experienced check. Require the limiting phenomenon named, the criterion matched to it, and non-preserved quantities (tip speed, blend time, minimum suspension speed) reported with the selected speed.

Defensive prompt. "Report plant speed and power under constant power per volume, constant tip speed, and constant Reynolds number. State which phenomenon limits the process and which criterion follows."

B5. Non-Ideal Flow

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: scenario.

Problem. Estimate conversion of a first-order reaction in a continuous reactor where the product of rate constant and nominal residence time, dimensionless, is 2.0. Under ideal flow with full volume active, conversion is one minus the exponential function of −2.0; with exp denoting the exponential function, 1 − exp(−2.0) = 0.865.

Best attempt. With bypass fraction b, dimensionless, of 0.15 of the feed passing through the reactor with negligible reaction, and the active stream at ideal conversion, outlet conversion is (1 − b) × 0.865 = 0.735. Bypass fractions of 0.05 to 0.30 (tracer-tested range for vessels with poor inlet distribution) give conversions of 0.605 to 0.821.

Why the confidence is low. Residence-time distribution is a property of the vessel — internals, inlet device, operating level — not inferable from volume and flow. Textbooks assume plug flow or perfect mixing; tracer results are case-by-case, not generalisable. The error is omission: no problem statement signals a bypass fraction, and the ideal answer returns complete and internally consistent.

Experienced check. Require tracer evidence, a computational fluid dynamics result, or a design allowance for the vessel before ideal-flow conversion supports a guarantee or safety duty.

Defensive prompt. "Give conversion under ideal flow and under bypass fractions of 0.05, 0.15, and 0.30. State what evidence would establish the flow pattern in the vessel."

Part C. Separations

C1. Relative Volatility Near Unity

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation with divergence, and model-form at an azeotrope.

Problem. Estimate the minimum number of theoretical stages for a binary separation in which the product of the distillate and bottoms composition ratios in the Fenske equation is 9,801, at a relative volatility, denoted α and dimensionless, of 1.30.

Best attempt. The Fenske count is the natural logarithm of 9,801 divided by the natural logarithm of α. At α = 1.30: 35.0 stages. At α = 1.15: 65.8 stages. At α = 1.08: 119.4 stages. The count varies inversely with the logarithm of α: a small uncertainty more than triples it.

Why the confidence is very low. Relative volatility carries the model-form uncertainty of entry A2, amplified without bound by the Fenske relation as α approaches 1. Where α crosses 1, an azeotrope exists: no stage count in one conventional column achieves the separation. The remedy is a different configuration (pressure change, entrainer, membrane, or extraction). A stage count alone does not establish feasibility.

Experienced check. Require α against composition from the stated thermodynamic model, whether α crosses 1, and the count at the ends of the α range.

Defensive prompt. "Report relative volatility across the composition range, state whether an azeotrope intervenes, and give the count at the lowest and highest plausible relative volatility."

C2. Flooding and Diameter

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form and scenario.

Problem. Size a column diameter for a vapour volumetric flow of 3.0 cubic metres per second (m3/s) at the flooding-limited section. The allowable superficial vapour velocity, denoted u and expressed in metres per second (m/s), is estimated as 1.2 m/s from a generalised capacity correlation at a design fraction of flooding.

Best attempt. Area is 3.0 / 1.2 = 2.50 m2; diameter is 1.78 m. Velocities of 0.7 (foaming derating) to 1.6 (clean system) m/s give areas of 1.88 to 4.29 m2 and diameters of 1.55 to 2.34 m.

Why the confidence is very low. Flooding correlations scatter by ±20 to 30% against their own data, before any system factor. Foaming can halve capacity and is a property of the actual liquid and its contaminants, not of pure-component data. Downcomer area and entrainment interact with the vapour limit; vendor ratings of proprietary internals are not public. Diameter comes in discrete increments: different vessels, not a tolerance.

Experienced check. Require the correlation and its scatter, the system factor and its basis in the actual liquid's foaming behaviour, and downcomer and entrainment checks.

Defensive prompt. "Name the flooding correlation and its scatter, state the system factor and why it applies, and give the diameter with and without a foaming derating."

C3. Liquid–Liquid Extraction

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation from a single measured coefficient.

Problem. Estimate the number of equilibrium stages, denoted N and dimensionless, to reduce the fraction of a solute remaining in a raffinate to 0.02 (2%), with a solvent-to-feed ratio of 1.0 and a distribution coefficient, denoted D and dimensionless (solute concentration in the extract phase divided by solute concentration in the raffinate phase), of 2.0.

Best attempt. The extraction factor, denoted E and dimensionless, is D multiplied by the solvent-to-feed ratio: E = 2.0. The Kremser relation for the fraction unextracted, (E − 1)/(EN+1 − 1), gives N = 4.7, or 5 stages in practice. At D = 1.2: N = 12.2 (13 stages). At D = 3.0: N = 3.2 (4 stages).

Why the confidence is low. The coefficient varies with composition, temperature, and other solutes, and published ternary data often rest on one study whose purity and method secondary sources omit. Mutual solubility, setting solvent loss and raffinate contamination, sits in tie-line data separated from the coefficient. Stage efficiency varies widely by device. Hence 4 to 13 stages on the coefficient alone.

Experienced check. Require tie-line data at the actual feed and temperature, mutual solubility at both cascade ends, and a stage-efficiency basis for the named contactor.

Defensive prompt. "Give stage count at the ends of the distribution-coefficient range, and report mutual solubility and solvent loss with it."

C4. Adsorption Breakthrough

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form and scenario.

Problem. Estimate the time to breakthrough for a fixed bed of 1,000 kilograms (kg) of adsorbent treating a stream carrying solute at 0.5 kg per hour (kg/h). The equilibrium capacity at the feed concentration, denoted q and expressed in kilograms of solute per kilogram of adsorbent (kg/kg), is 0.08 kg/kg from a fitted isotherm.

Best attempt. The stoichiometric (sharp-front) time is 1,000 × 0.08 / 0.5 = 160 hours. At a usable fraction of 0.70 (mass-transfer zone), the time is 112 hours. Comparable isotherm fits, with q of 0.03 to 0.15 kg/kg at the feed concentration, give usable times of 42 to 210 hours.

Why the confidence is very low. At polishing concentrations, isotherm parameters rest on sparse, imprecise data, and isotherm forms diverge most there. Competitive adsorption of water or a second solute, the normal industrial condition, cuts capacity beyond any single-solute isotherm. Ageing, fouling, and maldistribution shorten the cycle further. Regeneration sets working capacity: the economic quantity, least often reported.

Experienced check. Require a breakthrough curve on the actual feed, with competing components and humidity, and working capacity after repeated regeneration.

Defensive prompt. "Give breakthrough time at the ends of the isotherm-fit capacity range, state the competing components the isotherm ignores, and distinguish fresh from working capacity after regeneration."

C5. Membrane Ageing and Fouling

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: scenario and propagation over operating life.

Problem. Size the membrane area for a permeate production of 100 cubic metres per hour (m3/h) in a pressure-driven liquid separation at a design flux, denoted J and expressed in litres per square metre per hour (L/(m2·h)), of 20 L/(m2·h).

Best attempt. Area is the rate in litres per hour divided by J: 100,000 / 20 = 5,000 m2. Flux of 12 (fouled, aged) and 28 (clean-water) L/(m2·h) gives areas of 8,333 m2 and 3,571 m2 respectively.

Why the confidence is low. Manufacturer data come from standard test solutions on new membrane. Process performance is set by fouling and scaling, concentration polarisation at design recovery, cleaning frequency, chemical tolerance, and irreversible ageing and compaction. Selectivity and permeance trade against each other, and rejection of the minor component of interest is often not the reported test-solute rejection. Catalogue flux prices a system with no fouling history.

Experienced check. Require flux and rejection on the actual feed at the design recovery over a fouling and cleaning cycle, and a stated replacement interval, before area or cost is committed.

Defensive prompt. "Distinguish clean-water flux from process flux, give the area at a fouled end-of-cycle flux, and state the cleaning and replacement assumptions behind any cost figure."

C6. Column Efficiency

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form.

Problem. Convert a requirement of 20 theoretical stages into an actual tray count, using an overall column efficiency, denoted Eo and dimensionless, of 0.65.

Best attempt. The tray count is 20 / 0.65 = 31 trays. Efficiencies of 0.40 to 0.90, from viscous or foaming systems to light hydrocarbons, give 50 trays and 22 trays respectively.

Why the confidence is low. Efficiency correlations were fitted to limited hydrocarbon and aqueous test systems and scatter widely outside them. Efficiency depends on viscosity, surface tension, relative volatility, tray geometry, weeping, entrainment, and liquid distribution; a stage calculation supplies none of these. Murphree and overall efficiencies are interchanged in secondary sources and are not interchangeable.

Experienced check. Require the correlation named, with its applicable systems, and comparison with operating data from a similar column where one exists.

Defensive prompt. "State whether the efficiency is Murphree or overall, name the correlation and its fitted systems, and give the tray count at efficiencies of 0.4, 0.65, and 0.9."

Part D. Transport and equipment

D1. Exchanger Fouling

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation from a fouling resistance that is not a physical constant.

Problem. Size a heat exchanger for a duty, denoted Q, in megawatts (MW), of 2 MW at a logarithmic mean temperature difference of 20 kelvin (K). The clean overall coefficient, denoted U, in watts per square metre per kelvin (W/(m2·K)), is 1,000 W/(m2·K).

Best attempt. The clean area is 100 m2. With a combined fouling resistance, denoted Rf and expressed in square metres kelvin per watt (m2·K/W), of 0.0004 m2·K/W, the fouled coefficient is 1 / (1/1,000 + 0.0004) = 714 W/(m2·K) and the area is 140 m2. Resistances of 0.0001 (clean treated water) to 0.0010 (depositing crude-oil or cooling-water service) m2·K/W give coefficients of 909 to 500 W/(m2·K) and areas of 110 to 200 m2.

Why the confidence is low. Tabulated resistances are allowances from service experience, not measured properties. Deposit growth depends on wall temperature, velocity, water treatment, and history; the cleaning interval fixes run-end undersizing. Film coefficients scatter further. Without service and run length, the computed area is an allowance, not a rating.

Experienced check. Require the fouling basis by service, cleaning interval, margin policy, and a check that velocity and wall temperature avoid rapid deposition.

Defensive prompt. "Give the area clean, at the tabulated fouling resistance for the named service, and at twice that resistance. State the cleaning interval the area implies."

D2. Multiphase Pressure Drop

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form, with a possible wrong flow regime.

Problem. Estimate the pressure drop over one kilometre (km) of horizontal line carrying a gas–liquid mixture. The liquid-only pressure drop, computed as if the total flow were liquid, is 0.4 bar per km.

Best attempt. A two-phase multiplier, denoted φ2 and dimensionless, of 5 (separated-flow correlation) gives 2.0 bar per km. Multipliers of 3 to 9, from homogeneous to separated-flow treatment, give 1.2 to 3.6 bar per km.

Why the confidence is very low. Correlations report ±30 to 50% accuracy against their own data banks, dominated by small-diameter air–water and steam–water flow, remote from high-pressure hydrocarbon service. Regime sets slugging, hold-up, and terrain effects; a transition changes mean pressure drop and dynamic loads at bends and risers. Hold-up errors also falsify inventory, residence time, and relief estimates.

Experienced check. Require the regime named at several points along the line, the correlation and its data bank, and a slug and terrain assessment for undulating routes.

Defensive prompt. "Predict the flow regime, give pressure drop by a homogeneous and by a separated-flow method, and state the hold-up each method implies."

D3. Net Positive Suction Head

Confidence: Low, with a discontinuity. Kind of uncertainty: propagation through vapour pressure, which is exponential in temperature.

Problem. Estimate the available net positive suction head for a pump drawing a light hydrocarbon of density 650 kilograms per cubic metre (kg/m3) from a vessel at atmospheric pressure, 101 kilopascals (kPa). Liquid level is 3 metres (m) above the pump centreline; suction-line losses are 1 m; liquid temperature is 60 degrees Celsius, with vapour pressure 80 kPa.

Best attempt. The available head is (101 − 80) × 1,000 / (650 × 9.81) + 3 − 1 = 5.3 m, in which 9.81 metres per second squared (m/s2) is the acceleration due to gravity. At 50 degrees Celsius, with vapour pressure 55 kPa: 9.2 m. At 70 degrees Celsius, with vapour pressure 115 kPa: -0.2 m. The liquid then boils in the suction vessel: no operation without cavitation.

Why the confidence is low. The inputs, not the calculation, are weak. Mixture vapour pressure is set by the lightest components: a small composition change moves it as much as a larger temperature change. Suction temperature at start-up, recycle, or hot standby often exceeds the normal value used. Required head is a manufacturer test quantity with its own margins. The result can change sign, and cavitation damages the pump.

Experienced check. Require the actual mixture's vapour pressure at the maximum credible suction temperature, start-up and recycle included, and the margin over the manufacturer's required head at all flows.

Defensive prompt. "Compute the available head at the normal temperature and at 10 degrees above and below it, using the actual mixture's vapour pressure, and state the margin over the required head at maximum flow."

D4. Compressor Work

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form, arising from two non-interchangeable efficiency definitions.

Problem. Estimate the shaft work to compress a gas, treated as air with a specific heat capacity at constant pressure, denoted cp and expressed in kilojoules per kilogram per kelvin (kJ/(kg·K)), of 1.005 kJ/(kg·K) and a heat-capacity ratio of 1.4, from 1 to 10 bar at an inlet temperature of 300 kelvin (K). Work is expressed in kilojoules per kilogram (kJ/kg).

Best attempt. The isentropic work is cp × 300 × (10(1.4−1)/1.4 − 1) = 281 kJ/kg. Dividing by isentropic efficiency 0.75 gives 374 kJ/kg. A polytropic efficiency of 0.75, changing the exponent rather than the divisor, gives 317 kJ/kg. The two conventions differ by approximately 18% before any real-gas correction.

Why the confidence is low. The two efficiencies are interchanged in secondary sources; an undefined efficiency is ambiguous by the amount calculated here. Real-gas compressibility and heat capacity varying along the path add corrections, and discharge temperature, governing materials, seals, and downstream risk, is more sensitive to the convention than the work. Interstage drop and intercooling approach are vendor data.

Experienced check. Require the efficiency definition, discharge temperature with the work, and real-gas properties at suction and discharge.

Defensive prompt. "State whether the efficiency is isentropic or polytropic, compute the work under both conventions, and report the discharge temperature in each case."

D5. Solids Drying

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form.

Problem. Estimate the batch drying time for 100 kilograms (kg) of dry solid from which 25 kg of water is to be removed, in a dryer with a drying area of 10 square metres (m2) and a constant-rate drying flux of 1.0 kilograms per square metre per hour (kg/(m2·h)).

Best attempt. At the constant rate throughout: 25 / (10 × 1.0) = 2.5 hours. Below the critical moisture content, the rate falls with moisture. At a mean rate of 0.35 of the constant rate (internal diffusion resistance), the time is 7.1 hours. Mean-rate fractions of 0.20 to 0.60 give 12.5 to 4.2 hours.

Why the confidence is very low. Falling-rate behaviour is a property of the material in its actual particle form: internal diffusion, bound moisture, pore structure, shrinkage, crust formation. Published curves are for named materials prepared in named ways; equilibrium moisture content moves with gas humidity and temperature. Scale-up adds air distribution and bed depth. Constant-rate sizing understates time by the calculated factor and mis-sizes the dryer and cycle.

Experienced check. Require a drying curve on the actual material at its particle size and bed depth, with critical and equilibrium moisture contents identified.

Defensive prompt. "Identify the critical and equilibrium moisture contents, give the time under the constant-rate assumption and under a measured falling-rate curve, and state the ratio of the two."

D6. Compressible-Cake Filtration

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation from a resistance spanning an order of magnitude.

Problem. Estimate the time to obtain a filtrate volume V of 1 cubic metre (m3), at a constant pressure difference ΔP of 200 kilopascals (kPa), through a filter area A of 2 square metres (m2). The filtrate viscosity μ is 0.001 pascal seconds (Pa·s); the cake mass deposited per unit volume of filtrate c is 50 kilograms per cubic metre (kg/m3); the specific cake resistance α is 3×1011 metres per kilogram (m/kg).

Best attempt. Neglecting medium resistance, the filtration relation gives μ α c V2 / (2 ΔP A2) = 2.6 hours. Resistances of 1×1011 to 1×1012 m/kg, the published span for compressible cakes, give 0.9 to 8.7 hours.

Why the confidence is very low. Cake resistance is measured on the actual slurry: for compressible cakes it varies with pressure, particle size distribution, flocculation, and feed age and preparation. A tabulated value for a similar solid is uncertain by the order of magnitude shown here. Medium resistance and blinding, neglected here, can dominate short cycles. Literature values cause undersized filters.

Experienced check. Require a bench leaf or bomb filter test on the actual slurry at design pressure, resistance reported against pressure, before area or cycle time is committed.

Defensive prompt. "Refuse a tabulated cake resistance as a design basis. Give the time over the full published resistance range and state the bench test required to narrow it."

Part E. Safety and loss prevention

E1. Relief Sizing

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: scenario, the governing kind in relief design.

Problem. Size a relief orifice for a relieving rate, denoted W, in kilograms per second (kg/s), of 2 kg/s. Under a vapour-only assumption, the orifice mass flux, denoted G, in kilograms per square metre per second (kg/(m2·s)), is 1,200 kg/(m2·s).

Best attempt. The area is W/G = 16.7 cm2 (square centimetres). If the stream is two-phase (liquid swelling into the inlet, as in a fire or runaway case), a representative flux is 250 kg/(m2·s) and the area is 80 cm2, a factor of 4.8 larger. Fluxes of 150 to 400 kg/(m2·s) give 50 to 133 cm2.

Why the confidence is very low. Relief arithmetic is reliable once the scenario, phase, set and accumulation pressures, and back-pressure corrections are fixed. Fixing them is the substance of the work: code clauses; scenario judgement (blocked outlet, fire, control failure, utility failure, reactive case); and two-phase and reactive relief methods in restricted practices. Sizing from a stated rate and a vapour assumption performs only the final arithmetic; inlet pressure drop, chattering, and reaction forces are also unrepresented.

Experienced check. Require, before any area is reported: the scenario list, governing case identified; the relieving phase and basis; verification against current code and recommended-practice editions; and inlet and outlet pressure-drop checks.

Defensive prompt. "List every relief scenario, identify the governing one, state whether the stream is vapour, liquid, or two-phase and why, then compute the area. Report the code edition used."

E2. Thermal Runaway Time

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation through an exponential dependence, plus calorimetry uncertainty.

Problem. Estimate the response time after a reacting mass reaches an absolute temperature, denoted T (kelvin, K), of 393 K (120 degrees Celsius), under adiabatic conditions. The universal gas constant, denoted R (joules per mole per kelvin, J/(mol·K)), is 8.314. The self-heating rate there is 5 watts per kilogram (W/kg); the specific heat capacity, denoted cp (joules per kilogram per kelvin, J/(kg·K)), is 2,000; the activation energy, denoted Ea (kilojoules per mole, kJ/mol), is 100.

Best attempt. The self-heating rate is 5 / 2,000 = 0.0025 kelvin per second. The characteristic rise is RT2/Ea = 12.8 K; over that rise the rate increases by a factor equal to the base of natural logarithms, denoted e (approximately 2.718). The time to maximum rate is that rise divided by the rate: 1.43 hours. Activation energies of 80 and 120 kJ/mol alone give 1.78 hours and 1.19 hours. With a factor of two uncertainty in the measured rate: 0.59 to 3.57 hours, a factor of approximately six.

Why the confidence is very low. The governing quantities come from calorimetry on the actual mixture (contaminants, aged material, off-ratio charges included) and depend on the test cell's thermal inertia correction. Induction, gas generation, and a decomposition-pathway change at higher temperature fall outside a single-activation-energy treatment. An overestimate by that factor removes the response time the protective design (emergency response, venting, dump or quench) depends on.

Experienced check. Require calorimetry on the actual mixture (thermal inertia correction stated), a gas-generation measurement, and a sensitivity case at lowest plausible activation energy and highest plausible rate, before use in design.

Defensive prompt. "Give the time to maximum rate at the combined worst case, not central values; state the calorimetric basis, thermal inertia correction, and whether gas generation was measured."

E3. Gas Dispersion

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: scenario and model-form.

Problem. Estimate the ground-level centreline concentration at 500 metres (m) downwind of a continuous 1 kilogram per second (kg/s) release at a wind speed of 3 metres per second (m/s), using a Gaussian plume relation: concentration is the release rate divided by the product of pi, denoted π (approximately 3.1416), the wind speed, and the lateral and vertical dispersion coefficients, denoted σy and σz, in metres (m).

Best attempt. Neutral conditions (stability class D), σy = 35 m and σz = 18 m: 168 mg/m3 (milligrams per cubic metre). Unstable conditions (class B), coefficients 80 m and 50 m: 27 mg/m3. Stable night-time conditions (class F), coefficients 20 m and 8 m: 663 mg/m3, a factor of 25 above the unstable case. Consequence distances inherit the full factor.

Why the confidence is very low. Stability class, wind speed, and roughness are site- and time-specific; the worst credible case is the stable, low-wind condition average-weather calculation misses. Dense-gas behaviour, building wakes, terrain, and time-varying release rate each displace a Gaussian result; deposition and reaction remove material the relation omits. A single distance with no weather case is neither conservative nor average; the case is unspecified.

Experienced check. Require the weather case stated and justified, including a stable, low-wind case; release duration and rate profile; and a dense-gas assessment for denser-than-air material.

Defensive prompt. "Report concentrations and consequence distances for stability classes B, D, and F at the stated wind speed and at 1 metre per second, and state which case governs and why."

Part F. Control, operations, and data

F1. Control-Loop Tuning

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation from identified model parameters, and model-form for interacting loops.

Problem. Tune a proportional–integral controller for a self-regulating process: steady-state gain, denoted Kp (process output per unit controller output), 1.5; time constant, denoted τ (seconds, s), 60 s; dead time, denoted θ (seconds, s), 5 s; closed-loop time constant, denoted θc (seconds, s), set equal to θ. The internal-model-control gain is (1/Kp) × τ / (θ + θc).

Best attempt. The gain is (1/1.5) × 60 / (5 + 5) = 4.00. At a dead time of 15 s — within the uncertainty of a step-test estimate on a noisy trend — the rule gives 1.33, a factor of three lower. The first setting on a process with the second dead time oscillates; the second, on a process with the first, is sluggish and lets a level or quality variable drift.

Why the confidence is low. Identification is limited by noise, test disturbances, valve stiction and backlash — apparent gain and dead time then depend on move direction and size — and nonlinearity across the range. Loop interaction can change effective gain when a neighbouring loop is on automatic, at worst reversing the correct pairing. The calculation is exact arithmetic on loosely determined parameters.

Experienced check. Require the identification data, test-move direction and size, a valve travel and stiction check, and an interaction assessment, before tuning changes on an operating unit.

Defensive prompt. "Give the tuning at the ends of the dead-time and gain ranges, state the valve condition assumed, and identify any neighbouring loop that changes the effective gain."

F2. Data Reconciliation

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: scenario, concerning which instrument is in error.

Problem. Two feed meters read 100 and 50 flow units; one product meter reads 140, in the same units. The unit is at steady state, with no inventory change; the imbalance is 10 units. Each meter's standard deviation, denoted σ (same units), is 2% of its reading.

Best attempt. The imbalance standard deviation is the square root of the sum of the squared meter standard deviations: 3.58 units. The imbalance is 2.79 standard deviations — borderline: a gross error is neither established nor excluded. The unreconciled yield, product over total feed, is 93.3%. Reconciled yields span approximately 93% to 99% depending on which meter carries the error; the three readings cannot locate a loss, leak, or meter fault.

Why the confidence is low. Reconciliation is determined by the assumed meter variances — in practice estimates from instrument class, not measured performance under actual installation conditions (straight-run, compensation, fouling, impulse-line effects). Plant redundancy is often insufficient to localise a gross error, and a reconciled value shown without its adjustment conceals the correction. Models tend to report a closed balance whether or not the data support closure.

Experienced check. Require each adjustment reported with the reconciled values, each meter's assumed variance and basis, and a statement whether redundancy suffices to localise an error.

Defensive prompt. "Show the raw imbalance, the adjustment per meter, and the result assuming each meter in turn is faulty. Do not present a closed balance without the adjustments."

F3. Corrosion and Remaining Life

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation across an order of magnitude, with a localised mechanism not represented.

Problem. Estimate the remaining life of a vessel with a measured wall thickness of 12 millimetres (mm) and a minimum required thickness of 8 mm, giving 4 mm of available metal loss. The general corrosion rate, denoted r (millimetres per year, mm/year), is 0.15.

Best attempt. The remaining life is 4 / 0.15 = 27 years. Rates of 0.05 to 0.50 mm/year — the published span for carbon steel in one service class as chemistry, temperature, and contaminants vary — give lives of 8 to 80 years.

Why the confidence is very low. Tabulated rates are class averages; actual attack is governed by local chemistry (under deposits, in crevices, at welds), flow-assisted mechanisms, wet–dry cycling, corrosion under insulation, and brief excursions causing much of the metal loss. Localised attack, at a multiple of the general rate, is invisible to a life calculation from average thickness. Only item-specific inspection data, taken where the mechanism acts, narrows the range above; such data are confidential.

Experienced check. Require item thickness data at mechanism-specific locations, a damage mechanism identified by materials review, and an inspection interval set from the shortest credible life, not the central rate.

Defensive prompt. "Give remaining life at both ends of the rate range, name the damage mechanisms, including localised attack, and state the item-specific inspection data required to narrow the range."

Part G. Economics, energy, and environment

G1. Capital Cost Estimation

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: propagation across three estimated multipliers, within a wider estimate-class range.

Problem. Estimate the capital cost of a 100 kilotonnes per year unit from a known cost of 10 million United States dollars (USD) for a 50 kilotonnes per year unit of the same process, in a different year and location. Cost scales as capacity to an exponent, denoted n and dimensionless, of 0.6.

Best attempt. Scaling gives 10 × 20.6 = USD 15.2 million. At an exponent of 0.85 (near-linear items dominant): USD 18.0 million. A cost-index factor of 1.25 and a location factor of 1.15 on the first result give USD 21.8 million. Combining the exponent range with index factors of 1.20 to 1.30 and location factors of 0.90 to 1.30 gives USD 16.4 million to USD 30.5 million, before contingency.

Why the confidence is very low. Every multiplier comes from data dated, aggregated over unlike scopes, or proprietary to cost-engineering firms. The exponent depends on battery limits and on which items are duplicated rather than enlarged. Indices average equipment and labour baskets unlike a specific project. Classification itself assigns approximately −50% to +100% at this stage, wider than the range propagated above; a single figure suppresses it.

Experienced check. Require estimate class and standard range, battery limits and scope, base date and index basis, and location factor source.

Defensive prompt. "State the estimate class and its standard accuracy range, scope and battery limits, base date, and results at both ends of the exponent and location-factor ranges. Report a range, not a figure."

G2. Energy Integration

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: model-form in the trade-off between energy and capital.

Problem. Estimate the annual hot-utility cost at a minimum temperature approach in heat recovery, denoted ΔTmin (kelvin, K), of 10 K, where the hot-utility duty is 20 megawatts (MW). Utility price: 25 United States dollars per megawatt hour (USD/MWh). Operating time: 8,000 hours per year (h/year).

Best attempt. The cost is 20 × 25 × 8,000 = USD 4.0 million per year. At ΔTmin of 20 K: duty 26 MW, cost USD 5.2 million per year, area lower. At ΔTmin of 5 K: duty 17 MW, cost USD 3.4 million per year, area much higher: area varies approximately inversely with local temperature difference.

Why the confidence is low. The optimum approach is set by the energy-price to capital-cost ratio for the services, fouling in the added low-velocity area, start-up and control requirements that steady-state targets omit, and retrofit constraints. Rules of thumb derive from one period's energy and capital costs. A standard approach therefore gives a target and cost that are arithmetically consistent and economically undetermined for the case at hand.

Experienced check. Require duty and area together at several approach temperatures, energy price and exchanger cost basis dated, and operability and start-up addressed.

Defensive prompt. "Give duty, cost, and relative area at approaches of 5, 10, and 20 kelvin, with energy price and cost date behind any recommended approach."

G3. Carbon Intensity

Confidence: Low for a total; very low for a product intensity. Kind of uncertainty: model-form, arising from accounting conventions rather than from measurement.

Problem. Estimate annual emissions for a plant emitting 100 kilotonnes (kt) of carbon dioxide directly and leaking 2 kt of methane per year, and the carbon intensity of a principal product (80 kt per year, produced with a co-product).

Best attempt. With a global warming potential, denoted GWP and dimensionless, of 28 over a 100-year horizon, the total is 100 + 2 × 28 = 156 kt of carbon-dioxide equivalent per year. Over a 20-year horizon, GWP 80: 260 kt per year. Allocating the 100-year total by mass (60% share) gives 1.17 tonnes per tonne of product; by economic value (85% share), 1.66 tonnes per tonne. The 20-year horizon with economic allocation gives 2.76 tonnes per tonne, a factor of 2.4 above the first intensity.

Why the confidence is low. The spread is entirely conventional: time horizon, allocation rule, system boundary (purchased energy, upstream feedstock, downstream use), and treatment of exported energy and co-product credits. Each is set by a standard or programme, and standards differ. Emission factors are published averages with wide variation; methane leakage is among the least well measured quantities in plant reporting, with measurement-based and inventory-based estimates differing substantially. An intensity without its convention set cannot be compared with any other.

Experienced check. Require standard, boundary, allocation rule, and horizon with any intensity, and methane as mass as well as equivalent.

Defensive prompt. "State the standard, boundary, allocation rule, and horizon. Report intensity under mass and economic allocation and both methane horizons."

4. Prompt Library

These prompts reduce how often the weaknesses in Parts A to G pass undetected into a final answer. A prompt creates no data the model does not hold; it forces disclosure of assumptions, ranges, and missing information, so an experienced reader can verify at the point of weakness.

Prompt 1 — Universal defensive prompt. "Solve the problem. With the solution, give: every assumption, with its effect on the final result in percent; every property, correlation, and heuristic used, with source type and valid range; the status of each input — given, measured, fitted, estimated, or assumed; and a range, not a single value, for the result wherever any input is fitted, estimated, or assumed."

Prompt 2 — Specification completion. "Before solving, list every unspecified quantity the solution requires, including basis, reference state, pressure basis, composition basis, and per-pass or overall conversion. For each, ask for the value or solve under the two most plausible values and report both. Adopt no value silently."

Prompt 3 — Verification. "Verify independently: close every material and energy balance, reporting the closure error; test the two limiting cases known by inspection; check every correlation against its valid range; execute the arithmetic in code and show the result. Report failed checks; do not adjust the solution to pass them."

Prompt 4 — Audit by an experienced engineer. "Audit your own solution as a chemical engineer with twenty-five years of industrial design and operating experience would. Name the three points most likely to depart from built-equipment behaviour, each departure's direction and approximate size, and the document, test, or record that would settle each."

Prompt 5 — Unknown unknowns. "List what this calculation does not consider: effects omitted from the model; information proprietary or in a restricted code or databank, unavailable to you; mechanisms appearing only in operating equipment; and conditions under which the answer changes by more than 10 percent. Conclude with the questions a reviewer should ask before use."

Per-entry prompt pattern. Each entry in Parts A to G ends with a prompt of one construction: name the weak quantity; require results at both ends of its range; require the source, test, or record that would narrow it. The pattern transfers to unlisted types.

5. Standing Protocol

For the reader. Treat a result in a listed type as a draft calculation. Verify first at the entry's stated check — where the model's information is weakest — not at the arithmetic, settled cheaply by code execution. Where the check requires a primary databank value, vendor document, current code edition, or measurement on the actual material, no model consultation substitutes.

For the preparing agent. The register is recorded in standing memory under these rules. Answers depending on a listed type state the entry and rating. A range replaces a single value where the entry so indicates. Fitted, estimated, or assumed inputs are labelled at point of use. Claims resting on restricted codes, proprietary databanks, or vendor data are identified as unverified against source. Agreement with another model is not verification for a listed type. User-supplied plant data, vendor documents, or measurements supersede the register's ranges for that case.

For instruction. The entries serve as detection exercises: present a best-attempt solution without the uncertainty paragraphs; require the weakness located and the settling check named. Detection depends on experience with built, operated equipment — itself a finding of the register.

6. Classes Outside the Register

The register covers types admitting a numerical best attempt and a bounded uncertainty. These classes are also weak and fall outside it, because no comparable range can be attached: plant layout and spacing; human factors and procedural reliability; maintenance and inspection planning beyond entry F3; procurement and vendor evaluation; construction sequencing; regulatory interpretation for a jurisdiction and date; and any question depending on a current restricted code edition, a live price, or a specific unit's state. Absence from Parts A to G is not a claim of competence here.

Closing note. The recorded weaknesses are properties of the model's available information and of the form in which chemical engineering knowledge is published, restricted, and held as experience. The register should change slowly: added computing and cross-model checking act on arithmetic and independent reasoning errors, weakly on absent data, copied values, and unstated conventions. Revision is warranted when an entry's check becomes routinely satisfiable from public sources, or when measured error rates appear.
