1. Introduction: Recursive Self-Improvement

This study reports an AI agent (Muse Spark 1.3) improving its own answers on chemical-engineering problems, by a loop of four steps. The loop is called recursive self-improvement: recursive means that the output of one round feeds back as input to the next.

Step 1. Self-reflection on weaknesses. The agent worked answers to 32 chemical-engineering problems, then pulled each answer apart to find where its confidence was low and why. The result is a list of 32 problem types, each with a confidence rating, the best answer that could be given, the reason confidence is low, the check an experienced engineer would run, and the range an answer should fall in. The list is Appendix A.

Step 2. Experiment. Fresh agents, with no sight of the list, answered the same 32 problems. This first blind test set the starting point: how the agent performs on its own weak problem types when it does not know they are weak. The test ran with tool use (a program for running calculations, and web search) and with defensive standing rules in force, both at once.

Step 3. Defensive prompts devised. For each weakness on the list, a prompt was written that names the weakness and states the guard against it: give a range instead of one number, name the missing facts instead of filling them in silently, label which inputs are given and which are guessed, and check the answer by a second route. The prompts stand at the end of each list entry; the shared prompts and the standing rules made from them are Appendix B. How the prompts were made is Appendix C.

Step 4. Try again. The 32 problems were answered again by 64 fresh agents, two per problem, with the defensive prompt as the only difference between the two answers to each problem. Tool use was open to both halves, and no rule or note from the earlier rounds was in force for either half. This second test separates the prompt's effect from the effect of tools.

The headline result of the second test: answers warning about doubt rose from 21 of 32 to 32 of 32; answers with the main number inside the list's range rose from 21 of 32 to 24 of 32; weak shortcuts in method fell from 2 to none. Section 2 gives the full tables, before and after defensive prompting. Section 3 gives the analysis. The appendices give the details: the list of 32 problem types (Appendix A), the prompts and rules (Appendix B), and the method of the study (Appendix C).

3. Analysis

The warnings. The clear effect of the defensive prompt is on warnings. In the half without prompts, 21 of 32 answers warned about doubt; in the prompted half, all 32 did, and none merely repeated the words of its prompt: each applied the prompt to its own problem, naming the missing fact, the model limit, or the range for that case. The 11 answers without a warning in the unprompted half were mostly problems where every needed number was given (A1, C1, C2, C6, D3, F1, G2 among them): the agent did the work and stopped.

The numbers. The effect on the main number is small. Answers inside the range rose from 21 to 24 of 32. Answers giving no value fell from 6 to 3: prompted agents more often committed to a value under stated assumptions (A3, A6) instead of stopping at a formula. Five answers fell outside the range in each half, but not the same five. Three problems were outside in both halves (A4, D1, G1): in A4 the prompted answer named the missing property data and cross-checked by a second method, yet still headlined the ideal-gas value of 103 kg/m³; in D1 both halves headlined the clean area of 100 m²; in G1 the prompted range (USD 7.6–30.3 million) was wider than the list range and centred lower. A warning, in these cases, did not move the headline number.

The methods. Weak shortcuts fell from 2 to none. The two in the unprompted half were the ideal-gas rule used alone at 150 bar (A4) and the constant-rate drying time taken as the whole time (D5). In the prompted half the same two problems were handled by naming the missing data and giving bounds or a second method.

The first test, read against the second. The unprompted half of the second test scored lower than the first test on both counts (21 against 25 inside the range; 21 against 25 warning). Both runs had tool use. They differ in that the standing defensive rules were in force in the first test and removed in the second, and in that each answer is a single attempt, so some difference is ordinary variation. These counts cannot separate the share of the standing rules from that variation.

Limits. The same kind of agent wrote the list, sat the tests, and scored the answers, so agreement inside the study is not proof from an outside source. Each agent tried once; with 32 problems, a change of 3 in the number count is small next to the change of 11 in the warning count. The ranges used for scoring are the list's own ranges, set before either test. Missing plant facts stayed missing in every answer: no agent could supply plant data, maker data, or rule-book values, because no agent in this setup can reach them.

Recursive self-improvement, with its limits. The loop described in Section 1 is a form of recursive self-improvement: a study of the agent's own weak points was turned into prompts and rules for the agent, and the second test separates the prompt's share from tool use. Under the conditions of that test, the prompt on its own produced the full gain in warnings and the removal of weak shortcuts, and a small gain in numbers inside the range. What the standing rules add on top of the prompts, and what either adds without tools, was not measured.

Appendix A. The List of 32 Problem Types

How to Read the List

Each entry names one kind of problem. It says why AI answers are weak for that kind. It gives an example problem and a best-try answer, with every step worked out. It also gives a range: the low and high values that the main answer could plausibly take. The ratings follow the scale below. The percentages are the error that is plausible in a single answer given with no tools and no extra help.

RatingMeaning
ModerateThe answer may plausibly be wrong by roughly 10 to 25 percent.
LowThe answer may plausibly be wrong by roughly 25 to 100 percent. Or the answer may use the wrong convention (an agreed way of defining a quantity) or the wrong correlation (a formula fitted to test data).
Very lowThe answer may plausibly be wrong by a factor of two or more. Or the wrong flow regime (the pattern of the flow), the wrong relief case (the event a safety valve must handle), or the wrong model form (the set of equations used) may be chosen. The main answer is then only a rough guide to size.

Uncertainty comes in three kinds, and they are kept separate because the fix differs by kind. Propagation uncertainty is the spread in the answer that comes from a stated range in one of the numbers fed in. Model-form uncertainty is the spread that comes from choosing between published models, conventions, or correlations, each of which can be defended. Scenario uncertainty means the case that was worked out may not be the case that rules in practice; for example, a safety valve may face a different event from the one it was sized for. Each entry says which kind its range is.

Status of the numbers. Property values, correlation outputs, and rule-of-thumb factors in the examples are rounded values taken from published ranges, picked to make the size of the uncertainty easy to see. They are not design data; do not design from them. Before any real use, each number must be replaced by a value from a primary databank (the original source of measured data), a maker's document, or the current edition of the governing code (the rule book in force). Each entry's check says which source. Rounding the numbers in this way does not change the uncertainty ranges.

Standing commitment. The list is kept in standing memory. A later answer that rests on a kind in the list must carry the warning, give a range rather than a single value where the entry says to, and name the main source to check against. Two AI models agreeing with each other on a listed kind is not proof (see Why the Weakness Arises, below).

Why the Weakness Arises

Absence of industrial data. Data from running plants, makers' ratings, and inspection and repair records are private, and very little of them reaches the text that AI learns from. What experienced engineers know from practice — which services foul (build up deposits), foam, plug, or corrode (eat away metal); what cannot be started up as drawn; what operators will bypass — is written down rarely, and never in an orderly way.

Restricted codes and databanks. Design codes, recommended practices, and commercial property databanks cannot be read freely. Books and papers that sum them up often give their values without the conditions, the exceptions, and the edition date that say when a value may be used. The learning text keeps the value and loses the conditions.

Definitional conflict. Some quantities are defined in more than one accepted way. Henry's law constants appear under two conventions that are reciprocals of each other; each one is one divided by the other. Equilibrium constants differ by standard state (the agreed reference condition). Overall and stage efficiencies are swapped for each other in some books and papers. A model that takes a number without the sentence that defines it will go wrong; the source it took the number from did not.

Propagation through secondary sources. Reviews, textbooks, lecture notes, and solution sites copy values from earlier works. A copied error gains repetition, and repetition is a signal in training: the more often a value appears, the more it is trusted. Corrections and formal withdrawals are separate documents, and they are not reliably tied to the original.

Locally fitted parameters quoted as constants. Kinetic and phase-equilibrium parameters are often fitted to a narrow set of tests and printed without the scatter of the fit, a confidence interval (the range the true value likely lies in), or the range of the tests. Copied later without that range, such a pair of numbers looks like a fixed constant of nature.

Simulation output at the weight of measurement. Results from computer simulations are printed as findings. The thermodynamic method used and whether the results were ever checked against measurements are often stated badly, or only in extra material. In the learning text, such results look like measured results.

Loss of graphical information. Capacity charts, compressor maps, phase diagrams, and packed-column charts hold their values in plotted curves. When text is pulled out of these documents, the captions and the prose are kept and the plotted values are lost. The model keeps the shape of a relationship and a rough size, not the numbers.

Undated and superseded material. Cost data, emission factors, materials practice, and correlations that have since been replaced stay in the record with no mark on them. Material from different decades reads the same.

Consequence for cross-checking. Models that learn from the same public record share the same gaps, copied errors, and clashing definitions. Checking one model against another removes plain errors in arithmetic and in algebra. It does not remove a gap or a copied value that both models share.

Signs Used

Symbols are defined at first use in each entry, with SI units where units apply; SI is the International System of Units. The symbol % denotes percent. A range written with to between two values denotes that interval. Factor plus a number denotes the ratio of the larger to the smaller value in a range. AI denotes artificial intelligence. Headline result denotes the single numerical answer an entry requests.

Part A. Physical properties and phase equilibrium

A1. Henry's Law Conventions

Confidence: Low. Kind of doubt: mostly in which form of the rule applies; a little in how errors grow through the arithmetic.

Problem. Estimate how much oxygen is dissolved in water, at the state where no more will dissolve, at 25 degrees Celsius, with the water in contact with air. The partial pressure of oxygen (the share of the air pressure due to oxygen alone), denoted p, in atmospheres (atm), is 0.21 atm. The solubility-form Henry constant (the form that gives the dissolved amount from the pressure), denoted kH, in moles per litre per atmosphere (mol/(L·atm)), is 1.3×10−3 mol/(L·atm). The molar mass of oxygen, denoted M, in grams per mole (g/mol), is 32 g/mol.

Best try. Dissolved amount, in moles per litre: kH × p = 1.3×10−3 × 0.21 = 2.7×10−4 mol/L. Times M and times 1,000 milligrams per gram: 8.7 mg/L. Published kH values of 1.1×10−3 to 1.4×10−3 mol/(L·atm) give 7.4 to 9.4 mg/L (−15% to +8%).

Why the confidence is low. Henry's constant is written in at least four forms. One form is the upside-down version of the form used here; that upside-down value is 769 L·atm/mol. Many sources give the number without the sentence that says which form it is. The number can then be used the wrong way up, and the result is wrong by a large factor, not by a few percent.

Experienced check. Write the form in words beside every Henry constant. Check against water that has taken up all the oxygen it can from air: 8 to 9 mg/L at 25 degrees Celsius.

Defensive prompt. "State the Henry's law form used, in words and in an equation with units, and check the result against one known value for water in contact with air."

A2. Non-Ideal Vapour–Liquid Equilibrium

Confidence: Low. Kind of doubt: in the form of the rule used.

Problem. Estimate the bubble-point pressure (the pressure at which the first bubble of vapour forms) at fixed temperature for a liquid of two parts that mixes far from simply. The liquid mole fraction of component 1 (its share of the molecules), denoted x1, is 0.40. The pure-component vapour pressures (the pressure each pure liquid makes above itself), denoted P1sat and P2sat, in kilopascals (kPa), are 120 kPa and 50 kPa.

Best try. Raoult's law (the simple mixing rule for ideal liquids) gives the bubble pressure P, in kPa: x1P1sat + (1 − x1)P2sat = 78 kPa. With activity coefficients γ1 and γ2, dimensionless, which are numbers that correct the simple rule for real mixing, of 1.6 and 1.2 from one published fit: P = x1γ1P1sat + (1 − x1)γ2P2sat = 112.8 kPa, 45% higher. A second fit (γ1 from 1.4 to 2.0, γ2 from 1.1 to 1.35) gives 100 to 136 kPa.

Why the confidence is low. The γ numbers are a fitted pair: they come from fitting one set of measurements, for this pair of liquids only, over one temperature range. Books often give a model name (Wilson, NRTL, UNIQUAC, or a prediction method) without the pair, the data set, or the range. Values from fits and values from prediction methods differ by the spread worked out here, and giving one pressure hides that choice. Mixtures with dissolved salts, parts that stick together, or a mixture close to one that boils as if it were a single liquid widen the spread further.

Experienced check. Ask for the source of the numbers, the data set, and the temperature range behind any such value. Check against one measured point for the actual mixture before use.

Defensive prompt. "Give the model, where its numbers came from, and the range of the data behind them, and give the bubble pressure under at least two defensible models. If they differ by more than 10%, report the range, not a single value."

A3. Reactive Absorption Capacity

Confidence: Very low. Kind of doubt: in the form of the rule used, and in the case assumed.

Problem. Find the solvent flow needed to remove carbon dioxide at 100 kilomoles per hour (kmol/h) with a water-based amine solvent (a chemical liquid that takes up carbon dioxide). Loading, denoted α, in moles of carbon dioxide per mole of amine (mol/mol), is 0.10 mol/mol in the lean solvent leaving the regenerator (the vessel where the gas is driven back out of the solvent).

Best try. A simple count from the amounts alone: a rich loading α (the loading of the solvent leaving the absorber, full of gas) of 0.45 mol/mol gives a working capacity (the difference between rich and lean loading) of 0.35 mol/mol and a flow of 100 / 0.35 = 286 kmol/h. A rich loading limited by equilibrium (the most the solvent can hold under the absorber conditions) of 0.30 mol/mol gives a working capacity of 0.20 mol/mol and a flow of 500 kmol/h, a factor of 1.75 higher. The flow sets the pump duty, the regenerator duty, and the column diameter: the factor carries through the whole design.

Why the confidence is very low. The working capacity is set by equilibrium at the absorber conditions; by how fast the gas is taken up, for tertiary and hindered amines (amine types that take up the gas slowly); by breakdown of the amine and by heat-stable salts (salts that build up and are not removed in the regenerator); and by a corrosion limit (the loading above which the solvent attacks the metal) that differs by amine and by metal. Textbooks give a nominal capacity (a nameplate value); maker and operator limits are not public. The nominal loading therefore understates the flow, the reboiler duty, and the energy by that factor or more.

Experienced check. Ask for the rich and lean loadings, measured or worked out by simulation, at the absorber and regenerator conditions, for the named amine at its stated strength; ask also for the supplier's corrosion limit.

Defensive prompt. "Do not use nominal amine capacity. State rich and lean loadings at the stated conditions, the source of each, the corrosion limit, and circulation at both optimistic and equilibrium-limited loadings."

A4. Estimated Properties and Compressibility

Confidence: Low. Kind of doubt: in the form of the rule used, and in how errors grow through the arithmetic.

Problem. Estimate the density of a gas with molar mass M, in kilograms per mole (kg/mol), of 0.020 kg/mol, at pressure P of 150 bar and temperature T of 350 kelvin (K). The universal gas constant, denoted R, in joules per mole per kelvin (J/(mol·K)), is 8.314 J/(mol·K).

Best try. The ideal-gas density (the density from the simple gas rule), denoted ρ, in kilograms per cubic metre (kg/m3), is PM/(RT) = 103.1 kg/m3. With a compressibility factor Z, dimensionless, which is a correction number stating how far the real gas sits from the simple rule, of 0.85 from a generalised chart: ρ = PM/(ZRT) = 121.3 kg/m3. Chart and equation-of-state estimates of Z from 0.78 to 0.92 (the critical properties, the temperature and pressure at the critical point, are themselves estimated) give ρ from 112.1 to 132.2 kg/m3. The ideal-gas value is 15% below the central estimate.

Why the confidence is low. For a pure, well-measured gas the calculation is reliable. For a mixture of uncertain make-up, a heavy fraction handled by a pseudocritical rule (a rule that treats the mixture as if it were one pure gas), or critical properties estimated from the parts of the molecule, every chart input is estimated; the chart itself is an average over many fluids. The error in the estimated critical properties, left out of most textbook accounts, can exceed the spread worked out here for heavy or polar compounds (compounds whose molecules carry uneven electric charge). Errors in density carry into line sizing, compressor work, and relief calculations.

Experienced check. Ask for each critical property marked as measured or estimated, with the method. Compare a density from a cubic equation of state (a standard equation for real gases) with the chart value before use.

Defensive prompt. "State whether each critical property is measured or estimated, give density by two independent methods, and report the spread."

A5. Crystallisation Yield

Confidence: Very low. Kind of doubt: in how errors grow through the arithmetic, and in the case assumed.

Problem. Estimate the crystal yield (the mass of crystals formed) from cooling a saturated solution (a solution holding all the solute it can dissolve) from 60 to 20 degrees Celsius, per 100 grams (g) of solvent. Solubility, in grams of solute per 100 g of solvent, is 30 at 60 degrees Celsius and 12 at 20 degrees Celsius in one data set.

Best try. Yield is the solubility difference: 18 g per 100 g of solvent, a recovery of 60% of dissolved solute. Two data sets for comparable systems (hot solubility 25 to 35, cold solubility 9 to 16, same units) give yields of 9 to 26 g and recoveries of 36% to 74%.

Why the confidence is very low. How much dissolves depends on which solid form (polymorph) crystallises, on hydrate or solvate formation (water or solvent built into the crystal), on trace impurities not listed for the reported system, and on the method of measurement. The metastable zone width, the range in which the solution holds more than it should without forming crystals, which decides whether the crystals are easy to filter or are fine powder, is not set by equilibrium, and depends on seeding (adding starter crystals), stirring, and cooling rate. Plant yields also lose material to mother liquor (the liquid left after crystallising) held in the cake.

Experienced check. Ask for solubility measured for the actual feed, impurities included, with the solid form named; and for a metastable-zone measurement or a small trial crystallisation before yield or crystal size is promised.

Defensive prompt. "Give yield as a range over at least two solubility sources; identify the solid form assumed; state what equilibrium cannot determine: metastable zone width, crystal size, and mother-liquor loss."

A6. Polymer Viscosity

Confidence: Very low. Kind of doubt: in the form of the rule used.

Problem. Estimate the intrinsic viscosity of a polymer in solution (a number stating how much the dissolved polymer thickens the liquid, which carries information about its chain length) from the Mark–Houwink relation, in which intrinsic viscosity, denoted [η], in decilitres per gram (dL/g), equals a constant K, in dL/g, multiplied by the viscosity-average molar mass M, in grams per mole (g/mol), raised to an exponent a, dimensionless. Given: K = 3×10−4 dL/g and M = 100,000 g/mol.

Best try. With a = 0.65 (flexible polymer, good solvent), [η] = 0.53 dL/g. Published exponents span 0.55 to 0.80; holding K fixed gives [η] from 0.17 to 3.00 dL/g, a factor of 18.

Why the confidence is very low. K and a are a fitted pair for a named polymer, solvent, temperature, and molar-mass range and average. Secondary sources quote one without the other, or a pair fitted to number-average mass (the plain mean over molecules) for use with viscosity-average mass. Since M enters as a power, a modest error in the exponent is made larger by the size of M. Spread in chain lengths, branching, and solvent quality shift the result by a comparable amount; a single value here is unusable.

Experienced check. Ask for K and a as a pair, with polymer, solvent, temperature, molar-mass range, and the fit average; refuse a calculation whose pair and supplied mass differ in type.

Defensive prompt. "Use only a Mark–Houwink pair quoted with its polymer, solvent, temperature, and molar-mass average. Report the result at the ends of the exponent range and at the central value."

Part B. Reaction rates and reactors

B1. Reaction Rate at a Higher Temperature

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: the form of the model: two numbers fitted to the source data trade off against each other.

Problem. Estimate a first-order rate constant at 400 kelvin (K), from measurements made only between 300 K and 330 K. First-order means the reaction rate rises in direct proportion to the amount of reactant present. The rate constant k, in reciprocal seconds (1/s), is 0.010 1/s at 315 K. The activation energy Ea, in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol), is reported as 80 kJ/mol; the activation energy is the energy barrier that reacting molecules must cross. The gas constant R, in joules per mole per kelvin (J/(mol·K)), is 8.314 J/(mol·K).

Best try. Start from the value at 315 K. The Arrhenius rule, the standard rule linking reaction rate to temperature, gives k at 400 K as 0.010 times the exponential of (−Ea/R)(1/400 − 1/315). At Ea = 80 kJ/mol, the result is 6.59 1/s. Two other pairs of numbers fit the same 30 K of data just as well: Ea of 70 kJ/mol gives 2.93 1/s, and Ea of 95 kJ/mol gives 22.3 1/s. The spread between the two end values is a factor of 7.6.

Why the confidence is very low. Over a short temperature range, Ea and the pre-exponential factor, the multiplier that goes with Ea in the Arrhenius rule, trade off: a higher Ea together with a higher multiplier fits the same data. Papers usually report the pair without saying how strongly the two trade off, and later sources often quote Ea alone. Stretching the rule 70 K beyond the measured range turns that hidden trade-off into the factor worked out above. A change in how the reaction proceeds, slow movement of molecules inside the tiny holes of a catalyst, or a phase change across the temperature gap adds further error that no range on Ea covers.

Experienced check. Ask for the temperature range of the source data. Do not accept a rate stretched beyond that range without a check test. Before sizing equipment, require a measured rate near the design temperature.

Defensive prompt. "State the temperature range of the kinetic data, how far the answer stretches beyond it, and the rate constant at both ends of the plausible activation-energy range. Do not give a single stretched value."

B2. Catalyst Losing Strength

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: what happens in the real unit, and spread in one input number.

Problem. Estimate the conversion after 180 days on stream in a first-order catalytic reactor. Conversion is the share of the feed that reacts. The product of the fresh rate constant and the residence time (how long the feed stays in the reactor), a dimensionless number, is 2.0; the fresh conversion is 0.865. Catalyst activity a, dimensionless, starts at 1 and falls off as the catalyst loses strength, with a deactivation constant kd, in reciprocal days (1/day), of 0.004 1/day.

Best try. At 180 days, a = exp(−0.004 × 180) = 0.49, where exp denotes the exponential function. The conversion, worked out as 1 − exp(−2.0a), is 0.622. Published constants for similar services run from 0.002 to 0.008 1/day; they give activities of 0.70 to 0.24 and conversions of 0.377 to 0.752 after the same 180 days.

Why the confidence is very low. A catalyst loses strength because of poisons in the feed at parts-per-million level (a few parts in a million), spells of high temperature, coke (carbon build-up on the catalyst), and sintering (the active surface slowly fusing and shrinking at high temperature). Which cause rules differs from unit to unit and from feed to feed, and is often found only when the catalyst is changed out. Maker guarantees are sales documents, not measurements. A general decay constant gives the run-length error worked out above; the error grows with time, and the constant says nothing about what will actually end the run.

Experienced check. Require the basis for the decay for the named catalyst and feed: the poison limits, and the temperature plan (start-of-run and end-of-run temperatures) behind the quoted life.

Defensive prompt. "State the assumed cause of the strength loss, the feed poison limits on which the life depends, and the conversion at both ends of the plausible range for the decay constant. Name what event would end the run sooner."

B3. Reactor Cooling

Confidence: Low for the area; very low for the safety margin. Kind of uncertainty: spread in the area, from spread in one input; the safety margin depends on what happens in the real unit.

Problem. Size the cooling area for a reactor releasing heat at rate Q, in kilowatts (kW), of 500 kW, with a mean temperature difference between the reaction mass and the coolant, denoted ΔT, in kelvin (K), of 25 K.

Best try. Take the overall heat-transfer coefficient U, in watts per square metre per kelvin (W/(m2·K)), as 500 W/(m2·K); the coefficient states how fast heat passes through the wall per unit area for each kelvin of temperature difference. The area A, in square metres (m2), is then Q/(UΔT) = 40.0 m2. Published coefficients of 300 to 800 W/(m2·K), for stirred vessels that may be fouled (dirt build-up on the wall) or hold thick liquids, give areas of 25.0 to 66.7 m2. The temperature rise if no heat escaped, for the stated contents (reaction energy 2.5×106 kilojoules (kJ), mass 5,000 kilograms (kg), specific heat capacity (the heat needed to raise one kilogram by one kelvin) 2.5 kJ/(kg·K)), is 200 K.

Why the confidence is low. The area is only as good as U. What sets U — liquid thickness, fouling, gas given off, and how strongly the vessel is stirred — is decisive, and is known only from pilot tests or plant measurement. Staying safe is a separate question. Heat is made faster and faster as the temperature rises, while heat removal grows only in simple proportion to the temperature difference. Which one wins depends on those two rates of growth and on what happens if the coolant fails, too much feed enters, or stirring stops. A solution that balances at one steady point can still leave no usable safety margin.

Experienced check. Require curves of heat made and heat removed over the full temperature range, a basis for U in the real service, and the response to loss of cooling and loss of stirring.

Defensive prompt. "Give the area at both ends of the plausible coefficient range, the temperature rise if no heat escaped, and the temperature at which heat made exceeds the most heat that can be removed. State what follows from loss of cooling."

B4. Mixing in a Larger Vessel

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: the form of the model: published scale-up rules that do not agree with each other.

Problem. Scale up a stirred vessel from 0.1 to 10 cubic metres (m3): a volume ratio of 100 and, with the large vessel the same shape as the small one, a length ratio of 4.64. The laboratory stirrer speed N, in revolutions per minute (rpm), is 300 rpm; the power input is 50 watts (W).

Best try. Three common scale-up rules give three different plant speeds. Keeping power per unit volume the same gives 108 rpm, with plant power 5.0 kilowatts (kW). Keeping impeller tip speed the same gives 65 rpm, with plant power 1.08 kW; the impeller is the rotating stirrer, and tip speed is the speed of its blade tips. Keeping the Reynolds number the same gives 14 rpm; the Reynolds number is a dimensionless number that marks how turbulent the flow is. The first two rules are both in routine use, yet they differ in plant power by a factor near five, and they give different mixing times, different spreading of gas through the liquid, and different lifting of solids off the bottom.

Why the confidence is very low. The three rules are not near-misses of one answer: each keeps a different quantity the same and gives up the others. Which rule is right depends on what limits the process — moving material between phases, keeping solids lifted, mixing, moving heat, or harm to material that strong stirring forces can damage. The guidance is tied to the use, and to maker and pilot experience with named stirrers and vessel shapes. Picking a rule without naming what limits the process gives an exact speed and power for a vessel whose performance is unknown.

Experienced check. Require the limiting effect to be named, the rule matched to it, and the quantities not kept the same (tip speed, mixing time, lowest speed that keeps solids lifted) reported with the chosen speed.

Defensive prompt. "Report plant speed and power under all three rules: same power per volume, same tip speed, and same Reynolds number. State what limits the process and which rule follows from that."

B5. Uneven Flow in a Reactor

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: what happens in the real vessel.

Problem. Estimate the conversion of a first-order reaction in a continuous reactor, where the product of the rate constant and the nominal residence time, dimensionless, is 2.0. Nominal means the value from vessel volume divided by flow, before any correction for uneven flow. Under ideal flow, with the full volume active, the conversion is one minus the exponential function of −2.0; with exp denoting the exponential function, 1 − exp(−2.0) = 0.865.

Best try. Suppose a bypass fraction b, dimensionless, of 0.15: 15% of the feed slips through the reactor with almost no reaction, while the rest reaches the ideal conversion. The outlet conversion is then (1 − b) × 0.865 = 0.735. Bypass fractions of 0.05 to 0.30, the range found by tracer tests (a marked substance added to the feed and timed at the outlet) in vessels with poor inlet spreading, give conversions of 0.605 to 0.821.

Why the confidence is low. How long material actually stays in the vessel is a property of the vessel itself — its internals, its inlet device, and the liquid level — and cannot be worked out from volume and flow alone. Textbooks assume plug flow (all material moving through evenly, like a plug) or perfect mixing. Tracer results exist case by case and do not carry over to other vessels. The error is one of omission: nothing in a problem statement signals a bypass fraction, and the ideal answer still looks complete and hangs together.

Experienced check. Require tracer evidence, a computer simulation of the flow (computational fluid dynamics), or a design allowance for the vessel, before an ideal-flow conversion is used to support a guarantee or a safety duty.

Defensive prompt. "Give the conversion under ideal flow and under bypass fractions of 0.05, 0.15, and 0.30. State what evidence would establish the flow pattern in the vessel."

Part C. Separations

C1. Relative Volatility Near Unity

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: an error in the input grows as the sum is worked, and the method itself fails at an azeotrope.

Problem. Work out the smallest number of theoretical stages for splitting a two-part mixture. A theoretical stage is an ideal step: the vapour and liquid leaving the step are in balance with each other. In the Fenske equation, a standard short-cut equation for this count, the product of the top-product and bottom-product strength ratios is 9,801. The relative volatility, denoted α and dimensionless, is 1.30. Relative volatility says how much more easily one part boils than the other; a value near 1 means the two are hard to split.

Best try. The Fenske count is the natural logarithm of 9,801 divided by the natural logarithm of α. At α = 1.30: 35.0 stages. At α = 1.15: 65.8 stages. At α = 1.08: 119.4 stages. As α gets near 1, the count rises fast: a small error in α can more than triple it.

Why the confidence is very low. The value of α carries the same doubt as in entry A2, and the Fenske equation makes that doubt grow without limit as α nears 1. Where α crosses 1, the mixture forms an azeotrope: a mixture that boils at a steady strength, so no number of stages in one ordinary column can split it. The fix is a different setup: change the pressure, add a third liquid that changes the boiling, use a membrane, or split with a solvent. A stage count alone does not show that the split can be done.

Experienced check. Ask for α at each mixture strength from the stated model, whether α crosses 1, and the count at the low and high ends of the α range.

Defensive prompt. "Report relative volatility across the whole range of mixture strengths, state whether an azeotrope gets in the way, and give the count at the lowest and highest plausible relative volatility."

C2. Flooding and Diameter

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: choice of method, and the case itself.

Problem. Size the width (diameter) of a column for a vapour flow of 3.0 cubic metres per second (m3/s) at the section that limits the flow. Flooding is the point where rising vapour starts to hold back the falling liquid, so the column stops working well. The allowed vapour speed, denoted u and expressed in metres per second (m/s), is estimated as 1.2 m/s from a general flooding rule at the chosen design share of flooding. The speed u is the vapour flow divided by the whole width of the column, as if nothing were in the way.

Best try. Area is 3.0 / 1.2 = 2.50 m2; diameter is 1.78 m. Speeds of 0.7 (with a cut for foaming) to 1.6 (clean system) m/s give areas of 1.88 to 4.29 m2 and diameters of 1.55 to 2.34 m.

Why the confidence is very low. Flooding rules can miss their own test data by ±20 to 30%, before any allowance for the real system. Foaming can cut the capacity in half, and foaming is a property of the actual liquid and what is in it, not of clean data for the pure parts. The space taken by the channels that carry liquid down, and liquid carried up with the vapour, also bear on the limit. Makers' ratings for their own trays and packing are not public. Column widths also come in set sizes: a small change in the rule can mean a different vessel, not a small error.

Experienced check. Ask for the flooding rule and how far it scatters, the system factor (the allowance made for the real liquid) and why it fits this liquid's foaming, and checks on the down-channel space and on liquid carried up with the vapour.

Defensive prompt. "Name the flooding rule and how far it scatters, state the system factor and why it applies, and give the diameter with and without a cut for foaming."

C3. Liquid–Liquid Extraction

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: error growing from one measured number.

Problem. Work out the number of equilibrium stages, denoted N and dimensionless, to cut the fraction of a dissolved substance (the solute) left in the raffinate to 0.02 (2%). The raffinate is the feed liquid after the solvent has taken the solute out; the extract is the solvent carrying the solute. The solvent-to-feed ratio is 1.0. The distribution coefficient, denoted D and dimensionless (solute strength in the extract divided by solute strength in the raffinate), is 2.0.

Best try. The extraction factor, denoted E and dimensionless, is D multiplied by the solvent-to-feed ratio: E = 2.0. The Kremser relation, a standard equation for a row of balance stages, for the fraction left behind, (E − 1)/(EN+1 − 1), gives N = 4.7, or 5 stages in practice. At D = 1.2: N = 12.2 (13 stages). At D = 3.0: N = 3.2 (4 stages).

Why the confidence is low. The value of D changes with mixture strength, temperature, and other dissolved matter. Published data for three-part mixtures often rest on one study, and later sources leave out how pure the materials were and how the tests were done. The two liquids also dissolve a little in each other; that sets how much solvent is lost and how much solvent ends up in the raffinate. Those facts sit in tie-line data — tests showing the strengths of the two liquids in balance — kept apart from D. Stage efficiency, how close a real stage comes to the ideal, varies widely with the device used. So D alone gives 4 to 13 stages.

Experienced check. Ask for tie-line data at the real feed and temperature, how much each liquid dissolves in the other at both ends of the row of stages, and a stage-efficiency basis for the named contactor (the device in which the two liquids meet).

Defensive prompt. "Give stage count at the ends of the distribution-coefficient range, and report mutual solubility and solvent loss with it."

C4. Adsorption Breakthrough

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: choice of method, and the case itself.

Problem. Work out the time to breakthrough for a fixed bed holding 1,000 kilograms (kg) of adsorbent and treating a stream carrying solute at 0.5 kg per hour (kg/h). A fixed bed is a vessel packed with a solid (the adsorbent) that takes the solute up on its surface. Breakthrough is the moment the solute starts to come through in the outlet. The equilibrium capacity at the feed strength, denoted q and expressed in kilograms of solute per kilogram of adsorbent (kg/kg), is 0.08 kg/kg, from an isotherm: a curve fitted to tests of how much the solid takes up at each strength.

Best try. If the front moved sharply, with the bed filling fully before any solute came through, the time is 1,000 × 0.08 / 0.5 = 160 hours. At a usable fraction of 0.70, allowing for the partly used zone near the front, the time is 112 hours. Other fits of the same kind, with q of 0.03 to 0.15 kg/kg at the feed strength, give usable times of 42 to 210 hours.

Why the confidence is very low. At the low strengths used for final cleaning, the fitted curves rest on few and rough data, and different curve forms disagree most there. Other matter in the stream, such as water or a second solute, also takes up room on the solid; that is the normal plant case, and it cuts capacity below any single-solute curve. Ageing, dirt build-up, and uneven flow shorten the working cycle further. Cleaning the bed for reuse sets the working capacity — the number that matters for cost — and it is the one least often reported.

Experienced check. Ask for a breakthrough curve on the real feed, with the other components and dampness present, and the working capacity after repeated cleaning cycles.

Defensive prompt. "Give breakthrough time at the ends of the isotherm-fit capacity range, state the competing components the isotherm ignores, and distinguish fresh from working capacity after regeneration."

C5. Membrane Ageing and Fouling

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: the case itself, and error growing over the working life.

Problem. Size the membrane area for a permeate production of 100 cubic metres per hour (m3/h) in a pressure-driven liquid separation. The permeate is the liquid that passes through the membrane. The design flux, denoted J and expressed in litres per square metre per hour (L/(m2·h)), is 20 L/(m2·h). Flux is the flow through each square metre of membrane.

Best try. Area is the rate in litres per hour divided by J: 100,000 / 20 = 5,000 m2. Flux of 12 (fouled, aged) and 28 (clean-water) L/(m2·h) gives areas of 8,333 m2 and 3,571 m2 respectively.

Why the confidence is low. Makers' data come from standard test liquids on new membrane. Real performance is set by dirt build-up and scale on the membrane, by held-back matter piling up near the surface at the design recovery (the share of the feed taken off as product), by how often cleaning is needed, by which chemicals the membrane can stand, and by lasting ageing and squeezing flat under pressure. How much passes and how well the membrane holds back the unwanted part trade against each other: a membrane that passes more usually holds back less. The quoted holding-back figure is often for a test solute, not for the minor part of interest here. A catalogue flux describes a system with no fouling history.

Experienced check. Ask for flux and holding-back performance on the real feed, at the design recovery, over a full fouling and cleaning cycle, and a stated replacement interval, before area or cost is fixed.

Defensive prompt. "Distinguish clean-water flux from process flux, give the area at a fouled end-of-cycle flux, and state the cleaning and replacement assumptions behind any cost figure."

C6. Column Efficiency

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: choice of method.

Problem. Change a need for 20 theoretical stages (ideal steps) into a real tray count, using an overall column efficiency, denoted Eo and dimensionless, of 0.65. Overall efficiency is the number of ideal stages divided by the number of real trays.

Best try. The tray count is 20 / 0.65 = 31 trays. Efficiencies of 0.40 to 0.90, from thick or foaming systems to light hydrocarbons, give 50 trays and 22 trays respectively.

Why the confidence is low. Efficiency rules were fitted to a small set of test systems and scatter widely outside them. Efficiency depends on how thick the liquid is, on surface tension, relative volatility, tray shape, weeping (liquid leaking through the tray holes), liquid carried up with the vapour, and how evenly the liquid spreads. A stage calculation gives none of these. Murphree efficiency, which counts one tray, and overall efficiency, which counts the whole column, are mixed up in later sources and are not the same thing.

Experienced check. Ask for the rule named, with the systems it was fitted to, and a comparison with running data from a similar column, where one exists.

Defensive prompt. "State whether the efficiency is Murphree or overall, name the correlation and its fitted systems, and give the tray count at efficiencies of 0.4, 0.65, and 0.9."

Part D. Transport and equipment

D1. Exchanger Fouling

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: the doubt comes from the fouling resistance (defined in the best try below), which is not a fixed property of the fluid.

Problem. Size a heat exchanger (a unit that moves heat from one fluid to another through a wall) for a heat duty, denoted Q, in megawatts (MW), of 2 MW. The mean temperature gap between the two fluids, worked out in the log-mean way, is 20 kelvin (K). The clean overall heat transfer coefficient, denoted U, in watts per square metre per kelvin (W/(m2·K)), is 1,000 W/(m2·K).

Best try. The clean area is 100 m2. Fouling is the layer of dirt that builds up on the heat-transfer surface and slows the heat flow. Its effect is stated as a fouling resistance, denoted Rf and expressed in square metres kelvin per watt (m2·K/W). With Rf of 0.0004 m2·K/W, the coefficient with fouling is 1 / (1/1,000 + 0.0004) = 714 W/(m2·K) and the area is 140 m2. Resistances of 0.0001 (clean treated water) to 0.0010 (crude oil or cooling water that lays down deposits) m2·K/W give coefficients of 909 to 500 W/(m2·K) and areas of 110 to 200 m2.

Why the confidence is low. The resistances printed in standard tables are allowances based on past use, not measured properties of the fluid in hand. How fast the dirt layer grows depends on wall temperature, flow speed, water treatment, and past history. The time between cleanings decides how undersized the exchanger is at the end of a run. The film coefficients (the heat transfer rates in the thin fluid layers next to the wall) scatter further. Without the service and the run length, the area worked out is an allowance, not a true size for the job.

Experienced check. Ask for the fouling value by service, the time between cleanings, the margin policy (how much extra area is added on purpose), and a check that flow speed and wall temperature do not lead to fast build-up of deposits.

Defensive prompt. "Give the area clean, at the fouling resistance printed in the standard tables for the named service, and at twice that resistance. State the time between cleanings that the area implies."

D2. Multiphase Pressure Drop

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: the method itself may be wrong, and the flow pattern may be wrong too.

Problem. Estimate the pressure drop over one kilometre (km) of horizontal line carrying a gas–liquid mixture. The liquid-only pressure drop, worked out as if the whole flow were liquid, is 0.4 bar per km.

Best try. A two-phase multiplier, denoted φ2 and dimensionless (a pure number, with no units), scales the liquid-only value up to allow for the gas. A multiplier of 5 (from a separated-flow method, in which gas and liquid are treated as flowing side by side) gives 2.0 bar per km. Multipliers of 3 to 9, from a mixed-flow treatment (gas and liquid treated as one blended fluid) up to the separated-flow treatment, give 1.2 to 3.6 bar per km.

Why the confidence is very low. The published methods claim accuracy of only ±30 to 50% against their own test data. Those tests rest mostly on small pipes with air and water, or steam and water, far from high-pressure hydrocarbon service. The flow regime (the pattern of flow, such as smooth layers or slugs of liquid) sets slugging (large lumps of liquid moving down the line), hold-up (the share of the line filled with liquid), and the effect of rises and dips in the ground. A change of pattern changes both the mean pressure drop and the dynamic loads (the shaking forces) at bends and risers (vertical sections of line). Errors in hold-up also falsify the amounts worked out for liquid held in the line, the time fluid spends in the line, and relief sizing (the size of safety venting).

Experienced check. Ask for the flow pattern named at several points along the line, the method used and the test data behind it, and a check for slugging and for rises and dips on routes that are not flat.

Defensive prompt. "Predict the flow pattern, give the pressure drop by a mixed-flow method and by a separated-flow method, and state the hold-up (the share of the line filled with liquid) that each method implies."

D3. Net Positive Suction Head

Confidence: Low, with a sharp break. Kind of uncertainty: the doubt comes through the vapour pressure (the pressure at which the liquid boils at its temperature), which rises steeply with temperature.

Problem. Estimate the available net positive suction head for a pump. Net positive suction head is the margin, stated as a height of liquid, between the pressure pushing liquid into the pump and the vapour pressure; if the margin is too small, the liquid boils inside the pump. The pump draws a light hydrocarbon of density 650 kilograms per cubic metre (kg/m3) from a vessel at atmospheric pressure, 101 kilopascals (kPa). The liquid level is 3 metres (m) above the pump centreline; suction-line losses are 1 m; the liquid temperature is 60 degrees Celsius, with vapour pressure 80 kPa.

Best try. The available head is (101 − 80) × 1,000 / (650 × 9.81) + 3 − 1 = 5.3 m, in which 9.81 metres per second squared (m/s2) is the acceleration due to gravity. At 50 degrees Celsius, with vapour pressure 55 kPa: 9.2 m. At 70 degrees Celsius, with vapour pressure 115 kPa: -0.2 m. The liquid then boils in the suction vessel: the pump cannot run without cavitation (bubbles forming and collapsing inside the pump, which damages it).

Why the confidence is low. The weak point is the inputs, not the sum. The vapour pressure of a mixture is set by its lightest parts: a small change in the mixture moves it as much as a larger change in temperature. The suction temperature at start-up, on recycle, or on hot standby (kept hot while not in normal use) is often higher than the normal value used. The required head (the margin the pump maker says is needed) comes from the maker's tests and carries margins of its own. The result can change sign, and cavitation damages the pump.

Experienced check. Ask for the actual mixture's vapour pressure at the highest suction temperature that can occur, start-up and recycle included, and the margin over the maker's required head at all flows.

Defensive prompt. "Compute the available head at the normal temperature and at 10 degrees above and below it, using the actual mixture's vapour pressure, and state the margin over the required head at maximum flow."

D4. Compressor Work

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: the method itself, because two definitions of efficiency are in use and they are not interchangeable.

Problem. Estimate the shaft work (the work that must be supplied to the machine's shaft) to compress a gas, treated as air with a specific heat capacity at constant pressure, denoted cp and expressed in kilojoules per kilogram per kelvin (kJ/(kg·K)), of 1.005 kJ/(kg·K) and a heat-capacity ratio of 1.4, from 1 to 10 bar at an inlet temperature of 300 kelvin (K). Work is expressed in kilojoules per kilogram (kJ/kg).

Best try. Two efficiencies are in use. The isentropic efficiency compares the real machine with a perfect one that squeezes the gas with no heat loss and no friction. The polytropic efficiency is a small-step efficiency applied at each step along the squeezing path; the two give different answers. The perfect-machine (isentropic) work is cp × 300 × (10(1.4−1)/1.4 − 1) = 281 kJ/kg. Dividing by an isentropic efficiency of 0.75 gives 374 kJ/kg. A polytropic efficiency of 0.75, which changes the exponent rather than the divisor, gives 317 kJ/kg. The two definitions differ by approximately 18% before any correction for real-gas behaviour.

Why the confidence is low. The two efficiencies are swapped for each other in books and articles that copy from earlier sources; an efficiency stated with no definition is unclear by the amount worked out here. Real-gas behaviour (the gas departs from the simple gas law) and a heat capacity that changes along the path add further corrections. The discharge temperature, which governs materials, seals, and risk downstream, depends on the definition more strongly than the work does. Pressure losses between stages, and how well the cooling between stages works, are data held by the maker.

Experienced check. Ask which efficiency definition is meant, ask for the discharge temperature alongside the work, and ask for real-gas properties at suction and discharge.

Defensive prompt. "State whether the efficiency is isentropic (against a perfect machine) or polytropic (step-by-step along the path), compute the work under both definitions, and report the discharge temperature in each case."

D5. Solids Drying

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: the method itself.

Problem. Estimate the batch drying time for 100 kilograms (kg) of dry solid from which 25 kg of water is to be removed, in a dryer with a drying area of 10 square metres (m2) and a constant-rate drying flux of 1.0 kilograms per square metre per hour (kg/(m2·h)). The constant rate is the fast first stage of drying, while the surface stays wet.

Best try. At the constant rate throughout: 25 / (10 × 1.0) = 2.5 hours. Below the critical moisture content (the wetness at which the surface stops staying wet and drying starts to slow), the rate falls as the solid dries. At a mean rate of 0.35 of the constant rate (set by how slowly water moves through the inside of the solid), the time is 7.1 hours. Mean-rate fractions of 0.20 to 0.60 give 12.5 to 4.2 hours.

Why the confidence is very low. The slow second stage is a property of the material in the form it actually takes: how slowly water moves inside it, water bound tightly to the solid, the pore structure (the network of tiny holes in the solid), shrinking, and crust forming on the surface. Published curves are for named materials prepared in named ways. The equilibrium moisture content (the wetness at which the solid stops giving up water to the air around it) moves with the humidity and temperature of the gas. Making the dryer bigger adds air spread and bed depth problems. Sizing on the constant rate understates the time by the factor worked out above and mis-sizes the dryer and the cycle.

Experienced check. Ask for a drying curve (rate against wetness) measured on the actual material at its particle size and bed depth, with the critical and equilibrium moisture contents picked out.

Defensive prompt. "Identify the critical and equilibrium moisture contents, give the time under the constant-rate assumption and under a measured falling-rate curve (the curve for the slow second stage), and state the ratio of the two."

D6. Compressible-Cake Filtration

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: the doubt comes from a resistance that spans a factor of ten.

Problem. Estimate the time to obtain a filtrate volume V of 1 cubic metre (m3). The filtrate is the liquid that passes through the filter. The pressure difference ΔP is constant at 200 kilopascals (kPa), and the filter area A is 2 square metres (m2). The filtrate viscosity μ (a measure of how thick the liquid is) is 0.001 pascal seconds (Pa·s); the cake mass deposited per unit volume of filtrate c (the mass of solid layer laid down for each unit of liquid passed) is 50 kilograms per cubic metre (kg/m3); the specific cake resistance α (how strongly the solid layer resists flow, per unit mass) is 3×1011 metres per kilogram (m/kg).

Best try. Leaving out the resistance of the filter medium (the cloth or screen that holds the solid layer), the filtration relation gives μ α c V2 / (2 ΔP A2) = 2.6 hours. Resistances of 1×1011 to 1×1012 m/kg, the published span for compressible cakes (cakes that are squeezed tighter, and resist more, as pressure rises), give 0.9 to 8.7 hours.

Why the confidence is very low. Cake resistance must be measured on the actual slurry (the liquid–solid mixture being filtered): for compressible cakes it changes with pressure, with the spread of particle sizes, with flocculation (particles clumping together), and with the age and preparation of the feed. A value printed in a table for a similar solid is uncertain by the factor of ten shown here. The resistance of the filter medium, and blinding (the medium's pores blocking up), left out here, can govern short cycles. Values from books lead to filters that are too small.

Experienced check. Ask for a bench test (a small filter-leaf or bomb filter test) on the actual slurry at the design pressure, with resistance reported against pressure, before filter area or cycle time is fixed.

Defensive prompt. "Refuse a tabulated cake resistance as a design basis. Give the time over the full published resistance range and state the bench test required to narrow it."

Part E. Safety and loss prevention

E1. Relief Sizing

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: scenario. The scenario (which failure case is being designed for) is what governs relief design.

Problem. Size the opening of a relief valve (a valve that opens to let out excess pressure) for a relieving rate, denoted W, in kilograms per second (kg/s), of 2 kg/s. If the stream is vapour only, the mass flux (the flow through each square metre of opening), denoted G, in kilograms per square metre per second (kg/(m2·s)), is 1,200 kg/(m2·s).

Best try. The area is W/G = 16.7 cm2 (square centimetres). If the stream is two-phase (liquid and vapour together, with liquid swelling up into the valve inlet, as can happen in a fire or in a reaction that runs out of control), a representative flux is 250 kg/(m2·s) and the area is 80 cm2, a factor of 4.8 larger. Fluxes of 150 to 400 kg/(m2·s) give 50 to 133 cm2.

Why the confidence is very low. Once the case is fixed, the sums are reliable. The case means: what has gone wrong (blocked outlet, fire, control failure, loss of cooling or power, a reacting mixture), whether the stream is vapour, liquid, or both, the pressures at which the valve opens and the pressure allowed to build up, and the corrections for pressure pushing back at the outlet. Fixing the case is the substance of the work. It rests on clauses in the code (the governing rule book), on judgement about which case governs, and on methods for two-phase and reacting relief that sit in restricted practice documents. Sizing from a stated rate and a vapour assumption does only the final sum. It also leaves out the pressure drop in the inlet pipe, the valve opening and closing rapidly (chattering), and the force of the jet on the pipework.

Experienced check. Require, before any area is reported: the list of relief scenarios, with the governing one identified; the phase of the relieving stream and the basis for it; a check against the current editions of the code and the recommended practice; and checks of the pressure drop in the inlet and outlet pipes.

Defensive prompt. "List every relief scenario, identify the governing one, state whether the stream is vapour, liquid, or two-phase and why, then compute the area. Report the code edition used."

E2. Thermal Runaway Time

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: the error grows through an exponential (a quantity that multiplies by a fixed factor for each equal step), and the lab heat measurements themselves are uncertain.

Problem. Estimate the response time after a reacting mass reaches an absolute temperature, denoted T (kelvin, K), of 393 K (120 degrees Celsius), with no heat escaping (adiabatic conditions). The universal gas constant, denoted R (joules per mole per kelvin, J/(mol·K)), is 8.314. The self-heating rate there (the rate at which the mass heats itself) is 5 watts per kilogram (W/kg); the specific heat capacity (the heat needed to raise one kilogram by one kelvin), denoted cp (joules per kilogram per kelvin, J/(kg·K)), is 2,000; the activation energy (the energy barrier that sets how fast the reaction speeds up as temperature rises), denoted Ea (kilojoules per mole, kJ/mol), is 100.

Best try. The self-heating rate is 5 / 2,000 = 0.0025 kelvin per second. The characteristic rise (the temperature rise over which the rate grows by one fixed step) is RT2/Ea = 12.8 K; over that rise the rate increases by a factor equal to the base of natural logarithms, denoted e (approximately 2.718). The time to maximum rate (the time until the reaction runs at its fastest) is that rise divided by the rate: 1.43 hours. Activation energies of 80 and 120 kJ/mol alone give 1.78 hours and 1.19 hours. With a factor of two uncertainty in the measured rate: 0.59 to 3.57 hours, a factor of approximately six.

Why the confidence is very low. The quantities that govern the result come from calorimetry (lab tests that measure the heat a sample gives off) on the actual mixture, including contaminated material, aged material, and charges mixed in the wrong proportions, and they depend on the correction for heat soaked up by the test cell (the thermal inertia correction). A slow start before the rapid rise, gas being given off, and a change in the way the material breaks down at higher temperature all fall outside a treatment that uses a single activation energy. An overestimate by that factor removes the response time that the protective design depends on: emergency response, venting, dumping, or quenching (rapid cooling).

Experienced check. Require calorimetry on the actual mixture (thermal inertia correction stated), a measurement of the gas given off, and a sensitivity case at the lowest plausible activation energy and the highest plausible rate, before the result is used in design.

Defensive prompt. "Give the time to maximum rate at the combined worst case, not central values; state the calorimetric basis, thermal inertia correction, and whether gas generation was measured."

E3. Gas Dispersion

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: scenario and model form (the shape of the calculation itself).

Problem. Estimate the ground-level centreline concentration (the concentration at ground level along the centre line of the drifting cloud) at 500 metres (m) downwind of a continuous 1 kilogram per second (kg/s) release at a wind speed of 3 metres per second (m/s), using a Gaussian plume relation (a standard formula that treats the cloud as spreading sideways and upwards as it travels): concentration is the release rate divided by the product of pi, denoted π (approximately 3.1416), the wind speed, and the lateral and vertical dispersion coefficients (numbers that state how fast the cloud spreads sideways and upwards), denoted σy and σz, in metres (m).

Best try. Air conditions are graded by stability class, which states how fast the cloud mixes into the air. Neutral conditions (stability class D, average mixing), σy = 35 m and σz = 18 m: 168 mg/m3 (milligrams per cubic metre). Unstable conditions (class B, fast mixing), coefficients 80 m and 50 m: 27 mg/m3. Stable night-time conditions (class F, slow mixing), coefficients 20 m and 8 m: 663 mg/m3, a factor of 25 above the unstable case. The distances at which a given concentration is reached change by the full factor as well.

Why the confidence is very low. Stability class, wind speed, and ground roughness are specific to the site and the time; the worst credible case is the stable, low-wind condition that a calculation using average weather misses. Gas denser than air behaves differently, buildings stir the air into wakes, terrain changes the flow, and a release rate that varies with time changes the result; each of these moves the answer away from the Gaussian result. Deposition (material settling out onto the ground) and chemical reaction remove material that the relation leaves in the air. A single distance with no weather case stated is neither a safe-side figure nor an average one; the case is unspecified.

Experienced check. Require the weather case stated and justified, including a stable, low-wind case; the release duration and how the rate varies with time; and an assessment of whether the gas is denser than air and needs a dense-gas treatment.

Defensive prompt. "Report concentrations and consequence distances for stability classes B, D, and F at the stated wind speed and at 1 metre per second, and state which case governs and why."

Part F. Control, Operations, and Data

F1. Control-Loop Tuning

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: spread from loosely measured process numbers, and a wrong picture of the process when loops affect each other.

Problem. Set a proportional–integral controller, a controller that reacts to the present error and to the build-up of past error, for a process that settles on its own after a change. The process numbers are: steady-state gain, denoted Kp (process output per unit controller output), 1.5; time constant, denoted τ (seconds, s), the time scale of the response, 60 s; dead time, denoted θ (seconds, s), the wait before the process starts to respond, 5 s; and the wanted closed-loop time constant, denoted θc (seconds, s), set equal to θ. A common tuning rule, internal model control, gives the controller gain as (1/Kp) × τ / (θ + θc).

Best try. The gain is (1/1.5) × 60 / (5 + 5) = 4.00. At a dead time of 15 s — well within the error of a step test read from a noisy trend — the rule gives 1.33, three times lower. The first setting, used on a process that truly has the longer dead time, swings up and down. The second, used on a process with the shorter dead time, reacts too slowly and lets a level or a quality value drift.

Why confidence is low. The process numbers are hard to measure well. Noise and the test changes themselves blur the result. A sticking valve, or slack in the valve stem, makes the measured gain and dead time depend on which way the valve moves and how far. The gain also changes across the operating range. A nearby loop can change the gain this loop sees when it is on automatic; in the worst case the right pairing of loops is reversed. The sum itself is exact; the numbers fed into it are not.

Experienced check. Ask for the test data behind the process numbers, the direction and size of the test moves, a check of valve travel and sticking, and a look at how the loops affect each other, before any tuning change on a running unit.

Defensive prompt. "Give the tuning at the ends of the dead-time and gain ranges, state the valve condition assumed, and identify any neighbouring loop that changes the effective gain."

F2. Meter Readings That Do Not Add Up

Confidence: Low. Kind of uncertainty: not knowing which meter is wrong.

Problem. Two feed meters read 100 and 50 flow units; one product meter reads 140, in the same units. The unit runs steadily, with no build-up inside, so inflow and outflow should match; they differ by 10 units. Each meter's standard deviation, denoted σ (same units), the typical size of that meter's error, is 2% of its reading.

Best try. The typical size of the mismatch is the square root of the sum of the squared meter standard deviations: 3.58 units. The mismatch is 2.79 standard deviations — in between: one large error is neither shown nor ruled out. The raw yield, product over total feed, is 93.3%. Adjusted yields that make the readings agree span approximately 93% to 99%, depending on which meter carries the error. The three readings alone cannot show whether the gap is a loss, a leak, or a faulty meter.

Why confidence is low. The adjusted result depends on the error sizes assumed for each meter. In practice those sizes come from the meter's class, not from tests on the meter as installed, where pipe layout, correction for temperature and pressure, fouling, and the small sensing lines all add error. Plants often have too few meters to pin down which one is wrong. An adjusted value shown without its changes hides how much it was moved. A neat, closed balance can be produced whether or not the readings support one.

Experienced check. Ask for each change made to a reading, shown next to the adjusted values, each meter's assumed error size and where it came from, and a statement of whether there are enough meters to find which one is wrong.

Defensive prompt. "Show the raw imbalance, the adjustment per meter, and the result assuming each meter in turn is faulty. Do not present a closed balance without the adjustments."

F3. Corrosion and Remaining Life

Confidence: Very low. Kind of uncertainty: spread across tenfold, with attack in small spots not covered by the sum.

Problem. Estimate the remaining life of a vessel with a measured wall thickness of 12 millimetres (mm) and a minimum required thickness of 8 mm, giving 4 mm of metal that can still be lost. The general corrosion rate, denoted r (millimetres per year, mm/year), the speed at which the wall thins evenly, is 0.15.

Best try. The remaining life is 4 / 0.15 = 27 years. Rates of 0.05 to 0.50 mm/year — the published span for carbon steel in one service class, as the chemistry, temperature, and impurities vary — give lives of 8 to 80 years.

Why confidence is very low. Table rates are averages for a class of service. Real attack is set by local conditions: under deposits, in narrow gaps, at welds, where flow speeds the loss, where wet and dry periods alternate, under insulation, and during short upsets that cause much of the loss. Attack in small spots can run at several times the even rate, and a life worked out from average thickness cannot see it. Only thickness readings on the item itself, taken where the attack acts, narrow the range above; such readings are not public.

Experienced check. Ask for thickness readings on the item at the places where the attack acts, the kind of damage named by a materials review, and the next inspection date set from the shortest believable life, not the middle rate.

Defensive prompt. "Give remaining life at both ends of the rate range, name the damage mechanisms, including localised attack, and state the item-specific inspection data required to narrow the range."

Part G. Costs, energy, and the environment

G1. Cost of Building a Plant

Confidence: Very low. Kind of doubt: three guessed multipliers stack up, inside a wider standard range for early estimates.

Problem. Guess the cost of building a unit that makes 100 kilotonnes per year. A unit of the same process making 50 kilotonnes per year cost 10 million United States dollars (USD), in a different year and a different place. Cost grows with size raised to a power, called the exponent, written n (no units), here 0.6.

Best try. Size alone gives 10 × 20.6 = USD 15.2 million. With an exponent of 0.85, for plants where most parts grow almost in step with size: USD 18.0 million. A time factor of 1.25 (prices rise over time) and a place factor of 1.15 (costs differ by place) on the first result give USD 21.8 million. Taking the exponent range together with time factors of 1.20 to 1.30 and place factors of 0.90 to 1.30 gives USD 16.4 million to USD 30.5 million, before extra money set aside for surprises.

Why the confidence is very low. Every multiplier rests on weak ground. The numbers behind them are old, mix plants whose prices cover different things, or are kept private by cost firms. The exponent turns on which parts of the plant the price covers, and on which parts are built twice rather than made bigger. Time factors average mixes of machines and labour unlike any one project. Early estimates of this class carry a standard spread of about −50% to +100%, wider than the range worked out above. A single figure hides that spread.

Experienced check. Ask for the estimate class and its standard range; what the price covers; the base date and how the time factor was built; and where the place factor came from.

Defensive prompt. "State the estimate class and its standard accuracy range, what the cost covers, the base date, and results at both ends of the exponent and place-factor ranges. Report a range, not a figure."

G2. Heat Recovery and Energy Cost

Confidence: Low. Kind of doubt: a choice, not a fact — how much heat to recover trades energy cost against build cost.

Problem. Work out the yearly cost of bought-in heat, such as steam, when heat is recovered down to a smallest temperature gap between hot and cold streams, written ΔTmin (kelvin, K), of 10 K. The bought-in heat duty (the heat flow needed) is 20 megawatts (MW). Price: 25 United States dollars per megawatt hour (USD/MWh). Running time: 8,000 hours per year (h/year).

Best try. The cost is 20 × 25 × 8,000 = USD 4.0 million per year. At ΔTmin of 20 K: duty 26 MW, cost USD 5.2 million per year, and less heat-exchange area is needed. At ΔTmin of 5 K: duty 17 MW, cost USD 3.4 million per year, and much more area is needed: area grows roughly as the temperature gap shrinks.

Why the confidence is low. The best gap is set by four things: how the energy price compares with build cost for this plant; dirt build-up in the added area, where flow is slow; start-up and control needs, which figures for steady running leave out; and the limits of fitting new equipment into an old plant. Common rules of thumb come from one past period's energy and build costs. A standard gap therefore gives a target and a cost that add up correctly, but may not be the right choice for the plant at hand.

Experienced check. Ask for duty and area together at several gaps, the energy price and exchanger cost with their dates, and a word on start-up and control.

Defensive prompt. "Give duty, cost, and relative area at gaps of 5, 10, and 20 kelvin, with the energy price and cost date behind any recommended gap."

G3. Carbon Emissions per Tonne

Confidence: Low for a total; very low for a figure per tonne of product. Kind of doubt: the spread comes from book-keeping choices, not from measuring.

Problem. Work out the yearly emissions of a plant that puts out 100 kilotonnes (kt) of carbon dioxide directly and leaks 2 kt of methane per year, and the emissions per tonne of the main product (80 kt per year, made together with a second product).

Best try. Tonne for tonne, methane warms the planet more than carbon dioxide. The weight given to methane is called the global warming potential, written GWP (no units). With GWP 28 over a 100-year span, the total is 100 + 2 × 28 = 156 kt of carbon-dioxide equivalent per year — that is, all gases stated as the amount of carbon dioxide with the same warming effect. Over a 20-year span, GWP 80: 260 kt per year. The total must be split between the two products; that split is called allocation. Split by weight, a 60% share, gives 1.17 tonnes per tonne of product; split by money value, an 85% share, gives 1.66 tonnes per tonne. The 20-year span with the money-value split gives 2.76 tonnes per tonne, a factor of 2.4 above the first figure.

Why the confidence is low. The spread is all from choices: the time span, the split rule, where the count stops (bought-in energy, the feed that comes in, the use of the product after sale), and how exported energy and credit for the second product are treated. Each choice is set by a rule book, and rule books differ. The gas factors in use are published averages that vary widely. Methane leakage is one of the worst-measured numbers in plant reporting: figures from measuring and figures from book-keeping differ a lot. A figure per tonne given without its choices cannot be compared with any other.

Experienced check. Ask for the rule book, where the count stops, the split rule, and the time span with any figure per tonne, and for methane as a weight as well as an equivalent.

Defensive prompt. "State the rule book, where the count stops, the split rule, and the time span. Report the figure per tonne under the weight split and the money split, and under both methane time spans."

Appendix B. Prompts, Rules, and Scope

Prompts to Use

These prompts make it less likely that a weakness from Parts A to G slips into a final answer unseen. A prompt cannot create facts the model does not have. What it can do is force the answer to show its guesses, its ranges, and its missing facts, so a skilled reader can check the answer at its weak point.

Prompt 1 — For any problem. "Solve the problem. With the solution, give: every guess you made, and how much each guess moves the final result, in percent; every property value, rule of thumb, and fitted relation you used, where each came from, and the range where it holds; the state of each input — given, measured, fitted, estimated, or assumed; and a range, not a single value, for the result wherever any input is fitted, estimated, or assumed."

Prompt 2 — Fill the gaps first. "Before solving, list every missing quantity the solution needs. Include the basis (what the numbers are counted from), the reference state (the starting point the values are measured against), the pressure basis, the composition basis, and whether conversion is per pass or overall. For each one, ask for the value, or solve under the two most likely values and report both. Take no value in silence."

Prompt 3 — Check the work. "Check the solution in a second, separate way: close every material and energy balance and report how far out it is; test the two extreme cases whose answers are known by sight; check every fitted relation against the range where it holds; work the numbers out in code and show the result. Report checks that fail. Do not change the solution to make them pass."

Prompt 4 — Audit by a skilled engineer. "Check your own solution as a chemical engineer with twenty-five years of plant design and operating experience would. Name the three points most likely to differ from how built equipment behaves. For each, give the direction of the difference, its rough size, and the document, test, or record that would settle it."

Prompt 5 — What is not covered. "List what this calculation leaves out: effects not in the model; information that is private, or held in a restricted code or data bank, that you cannot see; effects that appear only in running equipment; and the conditions under which the answer changes by more than 10 percent. End with the questions a reviewer should ask before the answer is used."

The pattern for each entry. Each entry in Parts A to G ends with a prompt built the same way: name the weak quantity; ask for results at both ends of its range; ask for the source, test, or record that would narrow the range. The same pattern works for types not in the list.

Rules to Keep

For the reader. Treat a result in a listed type as a draft, not a final answer. Check first at the point the entry names — the point where the model's facts are weakest — not at the arithmetic, which code settles cheaply. Where the check needs a value from a primary data bank (a first-hand collection of measured values), a maker's document, the current edition of a code, or a measurement on the real material, asking the model again is no substitute.

For the agent giving the answer. This list is held in standing memory under these rules. An answer that rests on a listed type names the entry and its rating. A range replaces a single value where the entry says so. Inputs that are fitted, estimated, or assumed are labelled where they are used. Claims that rest on restricted codes, private data banks, or makers' data are marked as not checked against the source. Agreement with another model does not count as a check for a listed type. Plant data, makers' documents, or measurements supplied by the user take the place of the list's ranges for that case.

For teaching. The entries can be used as spotting exercises: give the best-attempt solution without the doubt paragraphs, and require the weakness to be found and the settling check to be named. Spotting the weakness takes experience with built, running equipment. That is itself one of the list's findings.

Weak Areas Not in the List

The list covers types where a best numerical attempt and a bounded range are possible. Other areas are also weak and sit outside the list, because no such range can be given: plant layout and spacing; human error and how far set procedures can be trusted; upkeep and inspection planning beyond entry F3; buying and judging suppliers; the order of building work; reading rules and laws for a place and date; and any question that rests on the current edition of a restricted code, a live price, or the state of one named unit. Absence from Parts A to G does not mean the model is strong in these areas.

Closing note. The weaknesses recorded here come from the facts open to the model, and from the form in which chemical engineering knowledge is published, kept restricted, or held as experience. The list should change slowly: more computing and checks by other models act on arithmetic and on reasoning slips, and act only weakly on missing data, copied values, and unstated conventions (the unwritten rules behind a number). The list should be revised when an entry's check can be met as a matter of routine from public sources, or when measured error rates appear.

Appendix C. Method of the Study

How the Defensive Prompts Were Made

The list in Parts A to G came first. Each entry was built the same way: a problem was set, the best answer that could be given was worked out, and the answer was then pulled apart to find where it could go wrong. The weak points fell into three kinds. Some were number errors that grow as a calculation runs. Some were wrong choices of method, where a simple rule is used outside the ground where it holds. Some were missing facts, where the problem as stated does not say enough to fix one answer.

For each weak point, a prompt was written to guard against it. A defensive prompt is a prompt written with the weakness named in it: it tells the AI agent to state the weak point, to give a range instead of one number, to say which facts are given and which are guessed, to name the missing facts instead of filling them in silently, and to check the answer by a second route. The prompt for each entry stands at the end of that entry, and the shared prompts stand in Appendix B.

The prompts were then turned into standing rules: the seven shared guards were written into the saved rules that every AI agent in this workspace works under, so the guards were in force for every answer, whether the person asking knew about the list or not. The first blind test (below) ran under those standing rules, with tool use allowed as well. Tool use and the rules were in force at the same time, so that test cannot show how much of its result came from each.

A second test was therefore run with the prompt as the only difference between its two halves. Before it ran, every rule and note learned from the first study was taken out of the saved files the agents start from, and a test agent with a fresh start confirmed that none of that material was present. The files were restored unchanged after the test, and a second test agent confirmed the material was back.

This loop is a form of recursive self-improvement. The word recursive means that the output of a round feeds back into the next round. The AI agent studied its own weak points, wrote the study down, turned the study into rules and prompts for its own later work, and was then tested on the same problems under controlled conditions. The limits of the claim are stated in Section 3.

The Blind Tests

First test. Each of the 32 problems was given to a fresh AI agent (Muse Spark 1.3). The agent received the problem text only. It did not receive the list, the ranges in the list, or the scoring rules, and it was not told that a test was taking place. Each agent could use tools, including a program for running calculations and web search, and the standing defensive rules described above were in force. Each agent tried once. The answers were not edited.

Second test. Sixty-four fresh agents were used, two per problem. One agent of each pair received the problem text only. The other received the same problem text plus that entry's defensive prompt, word for word, as an added instruction. Tool use was open to both halves, so the prompt was the only difference between the halves. The standing rules from the first study were not in force for either half (see above). Each agent tried once. The answers were not edited.

Scoring. Each answer was scored with three questions. Question 1: did the answer's main number fall inside the range printed in the list for that problem? Inside the range counts as a pass; outside counts as a miss, even if the number is near the range. An answer that gave no main number, because it held the problem to be underdetermined, is recorded as "no value" and counted separately from a miss. Question 2: did the answer warn about doubt, by giving a range, by naming a missing fact, or by naming the check that would settle the question? In the second test, an answer in the prompted half whose warning only repeated the words of its prompt, without applying them to the problem, was scored as repeating, not warning. Question 3: did the answer use a sensible method, rather than a weak shortcut, such as treating a high-pressure gas as an ideal gas? Answers were also checked for signs that the agent had found or used the published list. No answer in either test showed any such sign, so no answer was set aside.
