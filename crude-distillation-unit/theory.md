## Process description

The atmospheric distillation unit is the first processing unit of a petroleum refinery and the unit through which substantially all crude oil charged to the refinery passes. Refinery capacity is conventionally reported as atmospheric crude oil distillation capacity; the United States Energy Information Administration (EIA) reports United States operable atmospheric distillation capacity of 18.4 million barrel per calendar day (million b/cd) on 1 January 2025 across 132 operable refineries [1]. The atmospheric distillation unit together with its preheat, desalting and overhead equipment is termed the crude distillation unit (CDU). Every downstream conversion and treating unit receives feed prepared by the atmospheric unit. The atmospheric residue leaving the unit bottom is charged to a vacuum distillation unit; the subject process flow diagram (PFD), designated PFD-103, carries the corresponding continuation marker [25].

The separation principle is fractional distillation by volatility. Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbon compounds whose boiling points span a continuous range; heating the mixture to a two-phase condition and contacting the vapour and liquid repeatedly on the trays of a column separates the mixture into fractions of increasing boiling range from the top of the column to the bottom. The unit performs physical separation only: no chemical reaction is carried out in the unit, and no catalyst is present. Chemical change is a degradation mechanism to be avoided rather than a process step: thermal cracking of the residue becomes significant above the design furnace outlet temperature, hydrolysis of crude salts generates hydrogen chloride (HCl) in the column overhead system, and corrosion reactions attack equipment. The column bottom temperature is held at 340–350 °C, stated in the reference textbook treatment to be below the cracking temperature of the residue [7]. The abbreviation °C denotes degree Celsius, the unit of temperature; kPa denotes kilopascal, the unit of pressure; kg/h denotes kilogram per hour, the unit of mass flow rate.

PFD-103 is organised in three sections [25]. Section A comprises crude charge pumping (pump P-301), a first stage of heat recovery in preheaters E-301 and E-302, admixture of wash water, and electrostatic desalting in vessel V-301, followed by desalted crude pumping (pump P-302). Section B comprises a second stage of heat recovery in preheaters E-303 and E-304 and final heating in the fired furnace F-301, which raises the crude to the column feed temperature and partially vaporises the charge. Section C comprises the atmospheric column T-301 with its overhead condenser E-305 and reflux drum V-302, one pumparound loop (pump P-303 and pumparound cooler E-306), three steam-stripped side strippers (T-302 for heavy naphtha, T-303 for kerosene, T-304 for light gas oil), product cooling and pumping for the three side products, and residue cooling (cooler E-307) and pumping (pump P-304) to the vacuum unit continuation. Stripping steam is supplied to the base of T-301 and to each side stripper [25]. The reference throughput is 850,000 kg/h of crude oil [25].

## Preheat train

The preheat train (heat exchangers E-301, E-302, E-303 and E-304) is a heat recovery network in which cold crude is heated against hot product rundown streams, the pumparound return and the atmospheric residue, recovering heat that would otherwise be rejected to cooling media and reducing the fired duty of furnace F-301. In PFD-103 the crude is heated in two stages separated by the desalter: from 25 °C at battery limit to 125 °C before desalting, and from 120 °C to 250 °C after desalting [25]. PFD-103 design values are a crude inlet of 25 °C and 1,200 kPa, a pumped pressure of 2,800 kPa, and a second-stage outlet of 250 °C and 3,200 kPa [25]. The elevated train pressure is a pumping requirement to overcome exchanger and furnace pressure drop; the pressure is not a separation variable. Literature practice places the desalter within the train at 120–150 °C [8], consistent with the PFD-103 first-stage outlet of 125 °C [25]. No product specification is set in the preheat train. The train outlet temperature determines the furnace duty split, and the temperature approach in each exchanger is the primary indicator of fouling. Salt and solids not removed before the hot end of the train deposit in E-303 and E-304 and in the furnace tubes.

## Desalter

The desalter (vessel V-301) removes inorganic salts (predominantly sodium, calcium and magnesium chlorides), sediment and produced water from the crude before the hot preheat stage. Wash water is mixed with the heated crude; the salts dissolve into the aqueous phase; an electrostatic field then coalesces the dispersed water droplets, which settle and are withdrawn as brine. Salt removal of 90–99 percent per stage is reported, with inlet salt contents of 10–200 PTB reduced to less than 1 PTB in a single stage and less than 0.5 PTB in two stages, where PTB denotes pounds of salt per thousand barrels of crude [8]. The verified literature ranges are an operating temperature of 120–150 °C, an operating pressure of 1,000–1,500 kPa gauge, a wash water rate of 3–10 percent of crude volume, an electric field strength of 1–5 kV/cm, where kV/cm denotes kilovolt per centimetre, and a residence time of 20–30 minutes [8]. A textbook summary gives crude heating to 100–150 °C and mixing with 4–10 percent fresh water before desalting [7]. PFD-103 design values are a mixed feed of 890,000 kg/h at 120 °C and 2,550 kPa, wash water of 40,000 kg/h (equal to 4.7 percent of the crude charge by mass), desalted crude of 845,000 kg/h and brine of 45,000 kg/h [25]. The controlled quality measure is the salt content of the desalted crude, in PTB, with sediment and water content as the associated measure. Desalting performance governs downstream overhead corrosion: poor control of the salt and sediment content of the feed increases hydrolysis upstream of the column and the consequent formation of corrosive acids and salts in the overhead system [15].

## Fired furnace

The fired furnace (F-301) supplies the final temperature increment that the preheat train cannot recover, heating the desalted crude from 250 °C to the column feed condition and partially vaporising the charge so that the vaporised fraction exceeds the total of the products to be drawn above the flash zone by the overflash margin. Textbook practice heats the crude in the tube still to 350–360 °C [7]. The PFD-103 design outlet is 365 °C at 350 kPa, two-phase [25]. Operation materially above the design outlet temperature increases thermal cracking and coke formation in the tubes and in the transfer line. Coke forms on the inner tube wall where the film temperature is highest, insulates the wall, and raises the tube metal temperature at constant process outlet temperature; contributing factors are low mass velocity, flame impingement, salt and solids carryover from inadequate desalting, and operation at elevated outlet temperature to compensate for preheat train fouling. Tube metal temperature monitoring, pass balancing and periodic decoking are the corresponding controls.

## Atmospheric column

The atmospheric column (T-301) performs the fractional separation. The two-phase furnace effluent enters the flash zone; vapour rises through contacting stages against descending reflux liquid, and fractions are withdrawn at the elevations at which the local temperature matches the fraction cut point. Stripping steam admitted at the base (5,000 kg/h in PFD-103) lowers the hydrocarbon partial pressure and strips light components from the residue [25]. Conventional atmospheric columns use contacting trays (sieve or valve trays); packed sections are used in some services, notably where pressure drop or fouling governs. The PFD-103 design basis does not specify the internals of T-301 [25].

The textbook treatment gives a column top pressure of 1.2–1.5 standard atmospheres, equal to approximately 121–152 kPa, an overhead vapour temperature of 120–130 °C, side draw temperatures of 190–200 °C for kerosene and 280–300 °C for diesel-range material, and a column bottom temperature of 340–350 °C [7]. The PFD-103 sample values are design values and differ from the textbook pressure range: the overhead vapour stream is at 140 °C and 320 kPa, the side draws are at 180 °C (heavy naphtha), 230 °C (kerosene) and 290 °C (light gas oil), and the bottoms residue leaves at 340 °C and 360 kPa [25]. The temperature profile decreases monotonically from 340 °C at the base to 140 °C at the top in the PFD-103 basis, consistent in form with the textbook profile [7]. The flash zone is the column section at the feed entry, in which the vaporised portion of the furnace effluent disengages from the unvaporised liquid. The furnace is operated so that the vaporised fraction exceeds the sum of the overhead and side products by a small margin termed the overflash; the overflash liquid washes the flash zone trays, prevents entrainment of residue into the lowest side draw and provides the internal reflux that sets the separation between the light gas oil and the residue.

## Overhead system and corrosion control

The overhead condenser (E-305) condenses the column overhead vapour (light naphtha range hydrocarbons, stripping steam and light gases) against cooling medium. The reflux drum (V-302) separates the condensate into fuel gas, liquid hydrocarbon and sour water. Part of the liquid hydrocarbon returns to the column as reflux, setting the top temperature and the front end of the light naphtha; the balance is the light naphtha product. In PFD-103 the overhead vapour of 134,000 kg/h at 140 °C is cooled to 60 °C in E-305, and V-302 separates 55,000 kg/h reflux, 55,000 kg/h light naphtha product, 10,000 kg/h fuel gas and 14,000 kg/h sour water [25]. The condenser outlet is at 60 °C and 300 kPa and the drum products are at 60 °C and 280–290 kPa [25].

The overhead system is the most corrosion-active section of the unit. Hydrogen chloride, formed by hydrolysis of calcium and magnesium chlorides in the hot crude, leaves the column with the overhead vapour and dissolves in the first condensing water, producing a low-pH aqueous phase, where pH denotes the potential of hydrogen, a logarithmic measure of aqueous acidity; ammonium chloride, formed from hydrogen chloride and ammonia or neutralising amines, deposits as a solid salt that fouls exchangers and causes under-deposit corrosion [16]. The verified control set is optimisation of the primary crude treatment (desalting), chemical treatment with neutralising and filming agents, and wash water injection, together with control of the overhead temperature above the salt deposition point [15][16]. The product quality measures set in the drum are the light naphtha front end, characterised by the initial boiling point of the ASTM D86 distillation, where ASTM denotes ASTM International, the standards organisation whose test methods define product quality measurement, and the fuel gas hydrogen sulfide (H2S) content, which determines the downstream gas treating duty.

## Pumparound

A pumparound loop withdraws liquid from an intermediate tray, cools the liquid against crude in the preheat train or against another cold stream, and returns the cooled liquid to the column above the draw tray. The loop removes heat at an intermediate temperature level, at which the heat is recoverable into the preheat train, instead of at the overhead condenser, at which the heat is rejected. The consequences are a reduced overhead condenser duty at increased heat recovery, a reduced vapour and liquid traffic in the column section above the return, and consequently a smaller required column diameter or an increased capacity margin in that section. The pumparound return also provides internal reflux distributed along the column, which improves the fractionation between the adjacent side draws. PFD-103 design values are a pumparound draw of 200,000 kg/h at 300 °C and 345 kPa, pumped by P-303 to 900 kPa, cooled in E-306, and returned at 260 °C and 850 kPa, a temperature reduction of 40 degree Celsius across the loop [25]. No product specification is set in the loop; the loop duty determines the temperature profile slope in the middle section of the column and thus the overlap between the kerosene end point and the light gas oil initial boiling point, each measured by ASTM D86 distillation.

## Side strippers

Each side draw leaves the column containing dissolved lighter components that depress the product flash point and front end, because the draw tray liquid is in equilibrium with vapour rising from below. The side stripper is a small column in which the draw is contacted with stripping steam (or, in some designs, a reboiler). The steam lowers the hydrocarbon partial pressure, vaporises the light components, and the resulting vapour is returned to the main column above the draw tray; the stripped product leaves the stripper bottom at specification front end. Stripper conditions in PFD-103 follow the draw temperatures of T-301: heavy naphtha stripping at approximately 175–185 °C, kerosene at approximately 225–235 °C and light gas oil at approximately 285–295 °C, at pressures of 320–345 kPa, with stripping steam supplied at 250 °C and 400 kPa at rates of 2,000 kg/h (T-302), 3,000 kg/h (T-303) and 4,000 kg/h (T-304) [25]. The steam-to-product ratios are approximately 2.4, 2.5 and 2.7 percent by mass for the heavy naphtha, kerosene and light gas oil strippers respectively [25]. The controlled measures are the flash point of kerosene and light gas oil, the initial boiling point and the 10 percent recovered temperature of the ASTM D86 distillation, and, for kerosene, indirectly the freezing point. Product specifications illustrate the magnitudes controlled: the Jet A-1 aviation turbine fuel specification (Defence Standard 91-091, Issue 18, 2025) requires an ASTM D86 distillation with 10 percent recovery at maximum 205.0 °C and an end point of maximum 300.0 °C, a flash point of minimum 38.0 °C, a freezing point of maximum −47.0 °C, and a total acidity of maximum 0.015 mg KOH/g, where mg KOH/g denotes milligram of potassium hydroxide per gram [17]. The crude-unit kerosene is an intermediate that is subsequently hydrotreated and blended to such a specification.

## Product rundown and residue handling

Product rundown coolers recover heat from the side products into the preheat train or reject heat to cooling media, and rundown pumps deliver the products to storage or to downstream units at the required pressure. In PFD-103 the heavy naphtha is cooled from 175 °C to 90 °C (cooler E-308, pump P-305), the kerosene from 225 °C to 110 °C (cooler E-309, pump P-306) and the light gas oil from 285 °C to 130 °C (cooler E-310, pump P-307), and each product is pumped to 1,100 kPa [25]. The atmospheric residue is the unit product of largest mass flow: 425,000 kg/h in PFD-103, equal to 50.3 percent of the desalted crude charge [25]. The residue leaves the column base at 340 °C, is cooled to 180 °C in E-307 against crude preheat, and is pumped by P-304 to 1,200 kPa for transfer to the vacuum distillation unit [25]. A higher residue transfer temperature preserves heat for the vacuum unit but raises the coking and fouling rate in the transfer line; the cooled condition also provides the safe handling state if the vacuum unit is unavailable and the residue must be routed to storage.

## Balance model and furnace duty estimate

The App panel computes a slate balance and a sensible-heat furnace duty estimate. The symbol $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{feed}}$ denotes the crude oil feed rate (in kg/h) and the symbol $y_i$ denotes the cut yield of product $i$ (in mass per cent of the crude feed). The product flow rate of product $i$, denoted $\dot{m}_i$ (in kg/h), is

$$\dot{m}_i = \dot{m}_{\mathrm{feed}} \, y_i / 100$$

The closing remainder (in kg/h) is the crude feed rate minus the sum of the five product flow rates, and represents fuel gas, sour water, brine and losses on the crude-feed basis of the model; the full unit balance, which carries wash water and stripping steam as additional inputs, is stated in the Product slate section. The default yields are the PFD-103 design flow rates divided by the design crude feed rate of 850,000 kg/h, rounded to one decimal place: light naphtha 6.5, heavy naphtha 10.0, kerosene 14.1, light gas oil 17.6 and atmospheric residue 50.0 mass per cent, with a closing remainder of 1.8 mass per cent at the defaults.

The symbol $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{des}}$ denotes the desalted crude flow rate through the furnace (in kg/h), taken as the crude feed rate multiplied by the design ratio 845,000/850,000. The symbol $c_p$ denotes the mean specific heat capacity of the desalted crude over the furnace temperature interval (in kJ/(kg K), where kJ denotes kilojoule and K denotes kelvin). The symbol $T_{\mathrm{in}}$ denotes the furnace inlet temperature (250 °C in the design basis [25]) and the symbol $T_{\mathrm{out}}$ denotes the furnace outlet temperature (365 °C at the default). The furnace duty $Q$ (in kW, where kW denotes kilowatt) is estimated from the sensible-heat relation

$$Q = \dot{m}_{\mathrm{des}} \, c_p \, (T_{\mathrm{out}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}) / 3600$$

in which the divisor 3600 converts kilojoule per hour to kilowatt and a temperature difference in degree Celsius equals the same difference in kelvin. The default value of $c_p$ is 2.2 kJ/(kg K), an assumed value stated as an assumption: no crude-specific value is given in the design basis, and the crude-specific value is assay-dependent. At the defaults the estimate gives a duty of approximately 59,385 kW (59.4 MW, where MW denotes megawatt and 1 MW = 1000 kW). The estimate covers sensible heat only; the latent heat of the portion vaporised in the furnace is excluded, and the result is a lower bound on the fired duty at the stated outlet temperature. Every modelling choice in the model is thereby stated: a prescribed split for the slate (the separation itself is not computed) and a constant mean specific heat capacity for the duty estimate.

## Production scale

Operable United States atmospheric distillation capacity was 18.4 million b/cd on 1 January 2025, held by 132 operable refineries; the corresponding 2025 calendar-day figure in the EIA capacity table is 18,180 thousand barrel per day (thousand b/d) [1][2]. One barrel is defined as 42 United States gallons [3]. Gross input to atmospheric crude oil distillation units (crude runs) was 16,502 thousand b/d in 2023, 16,607 thousand b/d in 2024 and 16,731 thousand b/d in 2025, with operable utilisation of 90.5 percent in 2024 and 92.0 percent in 2025, where b/cd denotes barrel per calendar day and b/sd denotes barrel per stream day, the maximum sustained throughput of a unit on stream; stream-day capacity is typically about 6 percent higher than calendar-day capacity [1][2]. World refinery capacity was 102.76 million b/d in 2023 and 103.80 million b/d in 2024, an expansion of 1.04 million b/d in 2024, and global refinery throughput rose by 0.52 million b/d to 85.97 million b/d in 2024, where the world figures are those of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) Annual Statistical Bulletin [5][6]. The largest single United States refinery is the Motiva Port Arthur, Texas refinery at 640,500 b/cd, and the largest by stream-day capacity is the Marathon Galveston Bay refinery at 665,000 b/sd [1][4]. The largest single-train installation of record is the Dangote refinery at Lekki, Nigeria, designed around a single crude distillation unit processing approximately 650,000 b/d, with the crude distillation column recorded at installation in 2019 as the largest in the world, of 2,350 tonne mass and 112 metre height [23]. The PFD-103 reference throughput of 850,000 kg/h lies in the large single-train class [25].

## Process economics

The crude distillation unit is one of the largest energy-consuming units in a refinery and consumes around 35–40 percent of the total process energy of the refinery [11]. The energy is supplied principally as furnace fuel in F-301, as stripping and utility steam, and as electrical drive for the charge, product and pumparound pumps, less the heat recovered in the preheat train and pumparound loop. At whole-refinery level, energy accounts for about 60 percent of the cash operating costs of European Union refineries; a simple refinery with distillation and mild hydrotreating only consumes approximately 3–4 percent of the energy content of its crude intake, and a complex refinery approximately 7–8 percent [12]. The two bases are distinct: the 35–40 percent figure of [11] is a share of process energy consumed, and the figures of [12] are shares of crude intake energy content.

Verified unit-level capital costs are dated, and two bases are reported with their years stated. On the 1994 United States Gulf Coast basis of Gary and Handwerk (Petroleum Refining: Technology and Economics, 3rd edition, 1994), a desalter cost 1,800,000 United States dollars ($) for 30,000 b/sd, an atmospheric distillation unit cost $27,000,000 for 30,000 b/sd, equal to $900 per b/sd, and a vacuum distillation unit cost $14,500,000 for 18,000 b/sd; offsite facilities for a mid-size refinery added approximately 30 percent of the onsite cost, and a 15 percent contingency allowance was applied [10]. A contemporary commercial market report gives an atmospheric unit for 50,000 b/d at $80–150 million, equal to $1,600–3,000 per daily barrel, and a vacuum unit at $30–60 million [21]. The report of [21] is a commercial market study of lower authority than the source of [10]; its figures are reported as an indicative current range. The operating cost structure of the unit is dominated by energy, followed by maintenance of the furnace, column and exchanger train, chemicals (demulsifier, overhead neutraliser and filming inhibitor, desalter wash water treatment), and labour and overheads.

The margin context for the unit is the crack spread: the difference between the purchase price of crude oil and the selling price of the finished products, used as an indicator of short-term refinery margin [3]. The standard 3:2:1 crack spread approximates a typical United States refinery yield of three barrels of crude converted to two barrels of gasoline and one barrel of distillate; the measure excludes other variable costs and all fixed costs and therefore overstates the net margin [3]. Dated recent values for single-product cracks on the United States Gulf Coast basis are a gasoline crack spread of $22.02 per barrel in June 2024 and a diesel crack spread of $22.22 per barrel in June 2024 [14].

## Safety and environmental issues

The furnace contains the largest single inventory of hot, partially vaporised crude under pressure in the unit. Coke deposition raises tube metal temperature progressively; the end point of the sequence is tube rupture, release of the tube contents into the firebox, and a furnace fire with escalation potential to adjacent equipment. Controls are tube skin temperature monitoring with high-temperature alarms and trips, pass flow balancing, flame supervision, and steam snuffing of the firebox. Release paths elsewhere in the unit include pump seal failures on hot product and residue pumps, exchanger and flange leaks in the hot preheat train, and column overpressure; the unit relief and blowdown system routes gas relief to the refinery flare, and liquid relief to closed drain and slop systems. The desalter separates a three-phase system of oil, water and an intermediate emulsion band termed the rag layer, stabilised by fine solids, asphaltenes and corrosion products; growth of the rag layer degrades the electric field distribution, carries water and salt into the desalted crude and carries oil into the brine, which is an environmental loss and a wastewater treatment load. Brine from the desalter is highly corrosive and is routed through the sour water system to wastewater treatment [8]. Overhead corrosion by hydrochloric acid and ammonium chloride is treated in the Overhead system section [15][16]. Sour water withdrawn from the reflux drum (14,000 kg/h in PFD-103 [25]) contains dissolved hydrogen sulfide, ammonia and phenolic compounds and is routed to the refinery sour water stripper; the fuel gas and light naphtha from sour crude charges contain hydrogen sulfide, which is toxic at low concentrations and is managed by closed sampling, gas detection, and amine treating of the fuel gas before use as furnace fuel [18]. Products are run down hot, and the associated hazards comprise thermal burns and autoignition of leaks from hot-oil lines and pump seals, light naphtha storage vapour emissions, water bottoms in hot residue storage flashing to steam on contact with hot oil, and overfill of product and slop tanks.

Two crude-unit incidents of record are verified. At the Tosco Avon refinery, Martinez, California, on 23 February 1999, a fire in the crude unit killed four workers and seriously injured a fifth: workers were replacing leaking piping attached to the fractionator while the unit remained in operation; corroded and leaking valves had prevented isolation and draining over a period of 13 days; the pipe contained pressurised naphtha, which released onto the hot fractionator and ignited. The report of the United States Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board (CSB), approved on 21 March 2001, attributed the event to the absence of a hazard-assessment process for maintenance and to failure to correct a pattern of unsafe practices [20]. At the BP–Husky refinery, Oregon, Ohio, in September 2022, a mistaken release of more than 10,000 kg of liquid naphtha to the ground formed a vapour cloud that ignited, killing two workers; the CSB final report of 24 June 2024 recorded more than 3,700 alarms in the 12 hours preceding the incident, failure to shut down the unit or to exercise stop-work authority, and a similar overfill event at the same refinery in 2019 [19]. Carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions are treated in the Issues of special concern section [13].

## Issues of special concern

Crude slate variability is the governing operational uncertainty of the unit class. The yield structure, the salt, sediment, water, sulfur, nitrogen and acid contents, the wax and asphaltene contents and the distillation curve are properties of the specific crude or blend charged, and unit set points (furnace outlet temperature, draw rates, overflash, stripping steam rates and desalter conditions) are re-optimised at each slate change. A unit designed on a medium crude and charged with a heavier slate loses capacity by residue handling and furnace duty before any product specification fails; a unit charged with a lighter slate becomes limited by overhead condensing and naphtha handling.

Preheat train fouling mechanisms are salt and scale deposition, asphaltene precipitation from incompatible blend components, corrosion product transport, and coke formation in the hottest exchangers; the operational consequence is a rising furnace duty at constant throughput, or a falling throughput at maximum furnace duty, until a cleaning shutdown is forced. Incompatible blends of light tight oil precipitate asphaltenes and accelerate fouling in the exchanger train downstream of the desalter, raising fired heater energy consumption and forcing earlier cleaning shutdowns, and light tight oils additionally foul the cold preheat section upstream of the desalter with paraffin wax and filterable solids [18]. Salt and solids carryover from the desalter deposits in the hot preheat exchangers and furnace tubes, generates the hydrogen chloride of the overhead corrosion system, stabilises the desalter rag layer and erodes control valve trims; the desalter outlet salt content in PTB is the leading indicator, against the single-stage threshold of less than 1 PTB [8].

The unit is the refinery's largest single site of fuel combustion in its furnace. For the generic 200,000 b/d refinery of the International Energy Agency Greenhouse Gas Research and Development Programme (IEAGHG) fired heater study, total refinery emissions were approximately 1.5 million tonne per year of carbon dioxide, of which fired heaters contributed approximately two thirds, and the crude distillation heater alone contributed 271,000 tonne per year, the largest single heater source in the refinery [13]. Improvement routes are preheat train cleaning and antifoulant programmes, furnace excess air control and air preheat, increased pumparound heat recovery at useful temperature level, and carbon dioxide capture applied to fired heater flue gas [13].

Opportunity crudes are discounted crudes whose processing difficulty is the reason for the discount. Heavy crudes raise residue yield and furnace loading and, in blends, create asphaltene incompatibility fouling. High-acid crudes are classified by the total acid number (TAN), the mass of potassium hydroxide in milligram required to neutralise the acids in one gram of oil: crudes of TAN as low as 0.5 mg KOH/g are classified as high-acid, and at a TAN of approximately 1.0 mg KOH/g acidic crudes begin to be heavily discounted and are termed opportunity crudes [24]. The associated naphthenic acid corrosion (NAC) is most severe in the temperature range 190–400 °C, which covers the furnace outlet, the transfer line, the flash zone and the lower column and residue system; mitigation is by blending the charge TAN below approximately 0.5 mg KOH/g, by injection of neutralisers and corrosion inhibitors, and by chromium- and molybdenum-bearing alloy metallurgy in the hot sections [22][24]. Light tight oils present the inverse difficulty: a high light ends content for which an existing unit lacks overhead and naphtha capacity, with documented bottlenecking of the crude overhead and naphtha processing units by unblended light tight oil, wax and solids fouling of the cold preheat train, and entrained hydrogen sulfide and amine-based scavenger chemicals in the crude [18].
