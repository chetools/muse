Findings that matter

Siemens is particularly compelling when chemical engineering involves building and validating custom process models, predicting product quality, resolving equipment-scale transport phenomena, or connecting models to plant automation. It is not automatically the best single supplier for every process, industry, or undergraduate course.

The relevant Siemens portfolio has several distinct layers: gPROMS for process and product modeling; Simcenter STAR-CCM+ for computational fluid dynamics (CFD); Simcenter Flomaster for thermofluid networks; COMOS for plant engineering information; SIMIT for automation simulation and training; SIMATIC process control; and Opcenter for manufacturing execution. Comparing all of these collectively against only Aspen Plus would misrepresent both vendors.

My recommended educational strategy is a complementary stack: one flowsheet simulator, one environment for explicit mathematical modeling, one focused CFD experience, and an accessible programming/data-analysis tool. A department with Siemens licenses can make gPROMS a central modeling platform while retaining another flowsheet tool for comparison and sector exposure.

Evidence and limits

This is a broad portfolio and workflow assessment, not a benchmark of solver speed, prediction accuracy, market share, or employment outcomes. Product descriptions establish advertised scope; they do not independently demonstrate superiority. Pros, cons, and selection recommendations below are engineering judgments drawn from those capabilities. Actual suitability depends on model libraries, property data, version, validation, implementation support, and licensed modules.

Sources include primary vendor documentation, an academic conference paper deposited by UCL, and ABET's published criteria. Siemens customer examples cited here are vendor-hosted. No comparable, current institutional quotations were obtained, so price rankings would be speculative. Coverage spans major process families and industries, with representative specialist tools rather than an exhaustive catalog of every application.

Siemens' most persuasive advantages

  1. Model development tied to experimental evidence. gPROMS supports parameter estimation and model validation alongside simulation and optimization. This helps when a standard reactor block misses the important mechanism.
  2. Product quality as an engineering output. FormulatedProducts targets processes where a particle distribution, impurity profile or formulation outcome matters alongside throughput.
  3. Multiple physical scales. gPROMS and STAR-CCM+ allow a department or company to study both process networks and local transport effects. Transferring information between these levels still requires deliberate modeling choices.
  4. Engineering-to-operations breadth. COMOS, SIMIT, process control and Opcenter offer a route from design information to commissioning and manufacturing workflows.

Siemens' most consequential limitations

  1. Breadth creates implementation work. Common ownership does not remove the need to reconcile units, tags, component definitions, property methods, model versions and interfaces.
  2. Custom-model freedom brings responsibility. A sophisticated solver cannot identify omitted mechanisms or supply missing kinetic data. A converged result can still be physically wrong.
  3. Specialized alternatives can reduce development effort. A purpose-built wastewater, mineral, refinery or exchanger package may encode the exact domain knowledge a project needs.
  4. Teaching time and access can dominate. Equation modeling, CFD and automation are distinct learning demands. Adding all products to compulsory courses can displace fundamental engineering content.
  5. Lock-in extends beyond file format. Custom libraries, scripts, property databases and plant interfaces can all increase switching effort. Require readable equations and neutral data exports where practicable.

These are analytical implications, not evidence that Siemens is uniquely difficult or that competing commercial tools avoid the same issues.

The final row is an application shortlist inferred from physics capabilities, not verification of a complete prebuilt Siemens library for each industry. Some applications require custom models or additional products. Sector labels alone are weak selection criteria.

Specialist evidence: Olga transient multiphase transport, Aspen polymer properties/models, BioWin biological/chemical/physical wastewater models, OLI water chemistry, scaling and corrosion, HSC mineral and metallurgical workflows. These demonstrate why a broad portfolio should sometimes be complemented by a domain specialist.

Across industries, distinguish equipment sizing from equipment rating. A flowsheet heat duty and an assumed heat-transfer coefficient are not a complete exchanger design. Evaluate HTRI Xist or Aspen EDR when thermal/hydraulic rating is the task. Likewise, a safety study needs an appropriate validated transient model and applicable design methods; possessing a simulator does not make an operating procedure safe.

4. Connection to undergraduate chemical engineering

Published educational evidence

Kamel, Tsatse and Badmos (2025), in an ESCAPE 35 proceedings paper, describe UCL's integration of gPROMS, Aspen Plus and GAMS. Their sequence introduces gPROMS for reactor ODEs and GAMS for simulation/optimization in the first year, adds Aspen Plus and advanced reactor/separator modeling in the second year, and applies the tools in a third-year capstone. This is a concrete example of complementary adoption. It does not prove that the same sequence causes better learning outcomes than another curriculum. UCL paper and abstract.

For a US-oriented accreditation connection, ABET's 2026–2027 adopted criteria require modern engineering tools, experimentation and data interpretation, culminating design, and chemical engineering applications to process design, analysis, control and hazards. They do not prescribe Siemens, Aspen, or another brand. The same document includes proposed future criteria; those should not be confused with the adopted requirements. ABET criteria.

Proposed four-year sequence

This sequence is a recommendation, not a description of UCL's three-year structure.

Stage Engineering learning objective Suggested software role Assessable output
Year 1: balances and computation Units, conservation, numerical methods and reproducibility Spreadsheet/Python or MATLAB; optional small gPROMS model Independent material/energy balance and a documented calculation
Year 2: thermodynamics and transport Choose and defend property methods; connect equations to data One flowsheet simulator; gPROMS for a small dynamic model Flash/VLE comparison with experimental or trusted reference data
Year 3: reactions, separations and control Calibrate kinetics, simulate disturbances, examine nonideal equipment gPROMS plus one bounded STAR-CCM+/Fluent/COMSOL exercise Held-out model validation, sensitivity results and a control analysis
Year 4: integrated design Trade off economics, operability, safety and environmental performance Flowsheet core plus targeted custom model; specialist tools as needed Design report, model archive, assumptions, uncertainty and oral defense

For most undergraduates, basic COMOS/P&ID and SIMIT/control demonstrations are sufficient. A full MES, plant engineering or distributed-control-system qualification is better suited to an elective, industrial placement, or sponsored capstone.

Four assignments that make the software educational

1. Thermodynamic disagreement. Simulate the same flash or distillation problem using a shared component set and two justified property methods. Explain differences using phase-equilibrium data. Where two simulators are available, match physical assumptions before comparing answers. Vendor agreement alone is not validation.

2. Nonisothermal reactor model. Derive species and energy balances in gPROMS or a programming environment, estimate kinetic parameters from a supplied dataset, and validate against separate observations. Examine initialization, parameter identifiability, jacket behavior and a feed disturbance. Do not award full credit for fitting the training data alone.

3. Mixing and scale-up. Compare an ideal stirred-tank assumption with a small CFD study of a stirred vessel or static mixer. Use an appropriate mesh study and compare power, residence-time or mixing measurements where available. Explain whether local transport changes selectivity or heat removal enough to justify CFD.

4. Batch manufacture and economics. Use a fermentation or crystallization process to compare mechanism-based predictions with a facility scheduling/economic analysis. A yield improvement may fail to improve annual output if a dryer, cleaning step, or downstream unit remains the bottleneck.

A coherent capstone example

An illustrative renewable-methanol project can connect the tools without requiring students to learn the entire Siemens portfolio:

  1. Define the boundary: purchased or onsite hydrogen; CO2 source and conditioning; synthesis/recycle; purification; utilities.
  2. Build a conventional flowsheet and close balances using defensible properties.
  3. Develop a gPROMS reactor/dynamic model only for the part whose assumptions need refinement.
  4. Use CFD only if a specific heat/mass-transfer or geometry question affects the design.
  5. Compare energy, cost and emissions under explicit electricity and feedstock scenarios.
  6. Examine disturbances, operating limits and a P&ID/control strategy; optionally demonstrate virtual commissioning.

The assignment must supply or require students to source kinetics and property data. No numerical production rate, savings or emissions reduction is assumed here. Purchased hydrogen and onsite electrolysis require different accounting boundaries, so students must state which they use.

Recommended assessment emphasis

An illustrative rubric allocates 25% to formulation and assumptions, 25% to verification/validation, 20% to design decisions, 15% to uncertainty and sensitivity, and 15% to reproducibility and communication. These weights are proposed teaching choices, not researched standards. Require a model file, readable equations or calculation logic, property-method rationale, data provenance and an oral explanation. Interface proficiency should support engineering reasoning rather than dominate the grade.

5. Academic access and procurement

Siemens advertises discounted, full-functionality gPROMS academic licenses; access is institution-dependent. AspenTech also has an academic program. HTRI's educational suite includes selected exchanger modules but excludes some commercial resources. The standard DWSIM release is free and open source, while DWSIM Pro is a separate commercial offering. gPROMS academic program, Aspen academic program, HTRI educational scope, DWSIM.

Siemens has published a free STAR-CCM+ student edition fact sheet describing size/export limitations. That document is dated 2021 despite recent search crawl dates, so confirm current availability and restrictions directly before planning a course around it. A recently crawled page is not necessarily a recently revised policy. Siemens student fact sheet.

Before purchase or curriculum adoption, request written answers on concurrent seats, required model/property modules, student laptop and remote access, research versus teaching rights, commercial project restrictions, compute resources, instructor training, export formats and renewal terms. Calculate total effort and cost over several teaching years, including faculty preparation and IT administration.

Interoperability merits a practical trial. CAPE-OPEN defines interfaces between process-modeling components, but a standard's existence does not establish support for every product/version or guarantee complete model portability. CO-LaN standards.

Recommended selection exercise

Ask shortlisted vendors to solve three department-relevant problems with the actual licensed configuration: a nonideal separation, a calibrated dynamic reactor, and a sector-specific batch or electrolyte case. If CFD is important, add a shared mixing problem. Evaluate documented assumptions, validation data, setup effort, reproducibility, interfaces, classroom reliability and a written price quotation. Treat industrial partner workflows as one input to the decision.

Default recommendation: adopt Siemens gPROMS where equation modeling, experiments, dynamics or product quality are central; retain a conventional flowsheet simulator; provide a limited CFD experience and an accessible computational environment. Favor a single Siemens-centered stack only when faculty expertise, industrial partnerships, licenses and teaching support make that choice practical. For broader student access or constrained budgets, use DWSIM and an open computational environment as the baseline, with commercial tools targeted to their educational value.

6. Research questions worth pursuing

  • Does explicit equation modeling improve students' ability to diagnose a flawed process model compared with interface-led flowsheet teaching?
  • Does experimental calibration followed by held-out validation improve understanding of kinetics, uncertainty and scale-up?
  • Does a focused CFD exercise change students' judgments about ideal reactor assumptions?
  • Can students transfer thermodynamic/modeling reasoning to an unfamiliar simulator?
  • What learning benefit justifies the additional licensing, computing and instructor effort?

A useful study would give groups the same physical problem and datasets, assess reasoning with a common rubric, track prior preparation and instructional time, and test transfer using an unfamiliar tool. Outcomes should include conservation errors, property-method justification, validation quality and design reasoning—not simply successful convergence or software certification. These are proposed research directions; this brief does not claim that comparative educational effectiveness has already been established.
