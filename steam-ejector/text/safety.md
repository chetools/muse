# Safety

A steam ejector vacuum system has no moving parts in the ejector
itself, but its failures are sudden: a stage that breaks down
loses vacuum in seconds, and a condenser that floods backs liquid
into the vessel. The issues below are the ones that recur in
operation [2][4]. Each is stated with its mechanism, its symptom,
and the check that catches it.

## Unstable operation and surging

When the discharge pressure of a stage rises past its critical
value, the shock in the diffuser moves upstream into the mixing
throat and the entrainment rate falls steeply. The suction
pressure then rises, the discharge pressure falls, the shock
moves back downstream, entrainment recovers, and the cycle
repeats. The symptom is a suction pressure that hunts up and down
over seconds. The check is a suction-pressure recorder: steady
operation shows a flat trace, surging shows a regular oscillation.
The remedy is to lower the discharge pressure — open the vent
path, restore cooling water, or reduce the load — not to raise
the motive pressure, which moves the shock only slightly.

## Back-pressure sensitivity

Every stage holds only a limited discharge pressure. A rise in
atmospheric pressure, a blocked vent, or a flooded aftercondenser
raises the back pressure on the last stage first, and the failure
propagates upstream stage by stage. The symptom is a suction
pressure that rises while the motive steam flow is unchanged. The
check is a pressure gauge on each stage discharge: the stage
whose discharge pressure has risen against its design value is
the stage that has broken down.

## Wet steam and erosion

Condensate droplets in the motive steam strike the nozzle throat
at jet velocity and erode it. A worn throat passes more steam at
a lower velocity, so entrainment falls while steam consumption
rises. The symptom is a slow loss of vacuum over months with a
rising motive-steam flow. The check is a steam trap and a
separator on each motive branch, and a periodic measurement of
motive-steam flow against suction pressure. Nozzle throats are
inspected when the measured steam flow at design vacuum exceeds
the design value by more than about 10%.

## Air in-leakage

Air leaking into the vessel through flanges, seals, and valve
stems adds directly to the dry-gas load. Because the first stage
entrains the full load, a leak raises the motive steam demand of
every stage. The symptom is a suction pressure that will not fall
to its design value although the ejectors are sound. The check is
an isolation test: blank the vessel, pull vacuum, isolate the
ejectors, and watch the pressure rise. A steady rise gives the
leak rate, which is compared with the design dry-gas load of
50 kg/h.

## Condenser flooding and hot-well level

If the hot-well level rises, condensate backs up the barometric
legs into the intercondensers. Liquid in a condenser blocks gas
flow, raises the stage discharge pressure, and can be carried
into the next ejector and the vessel. The symptom is a rising
hot-well level with a rising suction pressure. The check is a
level gauge and a high-level alarm on the hot well, and a sight
glass on each barometric leg. The barometric leg must be taller
than the head that balances the condenser vacuum: for a condenser
at 7.40 kPa abs the leg must hold 9.6 m of water against
atmosphere, and for a condenser at 27.38 kPa abs it must hold 7.5
m, and the installed legs carry margin above those heights.

## Freezing

In cold weather, cooling water left standing in an idle condenser
or a barometric leg can freeze and block the leg. The symptom is
a condenser that will not drain on start-up. The check is to drain
idle legs and to keep a small cooling-water flow through idle
condensers in freezing weather.

## Start-up and shut-down

On start-up, the last stage is started first and the stages are
brought in from the discharge end towards the suction end, so
that each stage discharges into a condenser that is already under
vacuum. Starting the first stage first would discharge it against
atmospheric pressure and it would not entrain. On shut-down the
order is reversed, and motive steam is shut off before cooling
water, so that the condensers do not fill with steam that then
condenses and pulls a deeper vacuum than the vessel is rated for.
A vacuum breaker on the vessel protects it against collapse if
the condensers pull the pressure below the vessel design vacuum.

## Noise

The supersonic jet and the shock in the diffuser produce a
high-frequency noise, typically above 85 dB(A) at 1 m from an
unlagged ejector. The check is a sound survey at start-up. Lagging
and an acoustic enclosure on the ejector and its discharge line
are the remedy; ear protection is required in the area while the
survey is open.

## Condensate contamination

Condensate from the hot well contains the condensed process
vapour as well as condensed motive steam, so it carries whatever
the suction load carried. It is not boiler feed water without
treatment and testing. The check is a sample of hot-well
condensate analysed for the process species before any reuse is
approved.
