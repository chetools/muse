# Safety

A steam ejector vacuum system has no moving parts in the ejector
itself, but its failures are sudden: a stage that breaks down
loses vacuum in seconds, and a condenser that floods backs liquid
into the vessel. The issues below are the ones that recur in
operation [2][4]. Each is stated with its mechanism, its symptom,
and the check that catches it.

## Unstable operation and surging

When the discharge pressure of a stage rises past its critical value —
the onset of single-choking described in §T12 of the Theory panel, at
which only the motive nozzle remains choked — the shock in the
diffuser moves upstream into the mixing throat and the entrainment
rate falls steeply. The suction pressure then rises, the discharge
pressure falls, the shock moves back downstream, entrainment recovers,
and the cycle repeats. As its symptom, operators observe a suction
pressure that oscillates over seconds. For the check, a
suction-pressure recorder separates the two states: under steady
operation its trace stays constant, while surging shows a regular
oscillation. Lowering the discharge pressure is the remedy — open the
vent path, restore cooling water, or reduce the load — rather than
raising the motive pressure, which moves the shock only slightly.

## Back-pressure sensitivity

Every stage holds only a limited discharge pressure. The back
pressure on the last stage rises first — from a rise in
atmospheric pressure, a blocked vent, or a flooded
aftercondenser — and the failure propagates upstream stage by
stage. With motive steam flow unchanged, a rising suction
pressure signals this failure. Pressure gauges on each stage
discharge identify the broken-down stage, namely a stage whose
discharge pressure has risen against its design value.

## Wet steam and erosion

The condensate droplets in the motive steam strike the nozzle throat
at jet velocity and erode it. A worn throat passes more steam at
a lower velocity, so entrainment falls while steam consumption
rises. Slow vacuum loss over months, with a rising motive-steam
flow, is the symptom. For the check, each motive branch has a
steam trap and a separator, and motive-steam flow is measured
periodically against suction pressure. Nozzle throats are
inspected when the measured steam flow at design vacuum exceeds
the design value by more than about 10%.

## Air in-leakage

Air leaking into the vessel through flanges, seals, and valve
stems adds directly to the dry-gas load. Because the first stage
entrains the full load, a leak raises the motive steam demand of
every stage. Suction pressure that will not fall to its design
value, although the ejectors are sound, is the symptom. An
isolation test measures the leak: blank the vessel, pull vacuum,
isolate the ejectors, and watch the pressure rise. A steady rise
gives the leak rate, which is compared with the design dry-gas
load of 50 kg/h.

## Condenser flooding and hot-well level

If the hot-well level rises, condensate backs up the barometric
legs into the intercondensers. Liquid in a condenser blocks gas
flow, raises the stage discharge pressure, and can be carried
into the next ejector and the vessel. Hot-well level and suction
pressure rising together signal flooding. On the hot well, a
level gauge and a high-level alarm provide the check, with a
sight glass on each barometric leg. Installed barometric legs
carry margin above the heights derived in the Process panel
(First barometric intercondenser and Second barometric
intercondenser): 9.6 m of water for a condenser at 7.40 kPa abs
and 7.5 m for a condenser at 27.38 kPa abs.

## Freezing

In cold weather, cooling water left standing in an idle condenser
or a barometric leg can freeze and block the leg. A condenser
that will not drain on start-up is the symptom. As the check
against freezing, idle legs are drained and a small cooling-water
flow is kept through idle condensers in freezing weather.

## Start-up and shut-down

On start-up, the last stage is started first and the stages are
brought in from the discharge end towards the suction end, so
that each stage discharges into a condenser that is already under
vacuum. Starting the first stage first would discharge it against
atmospheric pressure and it would not entrain. Shut-down
reverses the order, and motive steam is shut off before cooling
water, so that the condensers do not fill with steam that then
condenses and pulls a deeper vacuum than the vessel is rated for.
A vacuum breaker on the vessel protects it against collapse if
the condensers pull the pressure below the vessel design vacuum.

## Noise

High-frequency noise from the supersonic jet and the shock in the
diffuser is typically above 85 dB(A) at 1 m from an unlagged
ejector, where dB(A) is the sound pressure level in A-weighted
decibels. At start-up, a sound survey checks the noise level.
Lagging and an acoustic enclosure on the ejector and its discharge
line are the remedy; ear protection is required in the area while
the survey is open.

## Condensate contamination

The condensate from the hot well contains the condensed process
vapour as well as condensed motive steam, so it carries whatever
the suction load carried. It is not boiler feed water without
treatment and testing. Sampling hot-well condensate and analysing
it for the process species is the check before any reuse is
approved.
