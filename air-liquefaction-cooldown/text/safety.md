# Safety

## Hazards of the cool-down state

The hazards of a liquefaction loop are at their least familiar
during start-up. The plant is in motion between two states its
operators know well — warm and shut down, or cold and steady —
and inventories, temperatures and compositions are all changing
at once. The hazards below are those of cryogenic air separation
generally, stated for the cool-down condition; the industry
guidance is collected in the EIGA safe practices guide for
cryogenic air separation plants [4].

## Oxygen enrichment

Liquid air is not a fixed-composition liquid. Nitrogen is the
more volatile component of air, so liquid that stands, boils or
leaks loses nitrogen first and grows richer in oxygen. An
oxygen-enriched liquid is a fire hazard of a different class
from air: oxygen enrichment raises the flammability of materials
generally, including materials that do not burn in air [6].
Liquid spilled during the first hours of operation — when levels
are being established and connections are being checked — must
be treated as an oxidiser spill, not as cold water. The same
enrichment acts inside the equipment: the liquid in the
separator of this loop, and in the sumps of the columns it
feeds, is oxygen-enriched relative to air, and every hydrocarbon
that reaches it concentrates there. The enrichment is a property
of real air: the model of this page treats air as one fluid, so
the liquid in its separator has the feed composition, and the
hazard stated here is that of the real equipment the loop
represents.

## Hydrocarbon accumulation in liquid oxygen

Feed air carries trace hydrocarbons, and the cold end of the
plant collects them. Acetylene is the controlling case: it is
sparingly soluble in liquid oxygen and precipitates as a
shock-sensitive solid at 4 to 6 parts per million in liquid
oxygen [5]. The industry limits are far below the precipitation
point — the maximum allowable concentration of acetylene in
liquid oxygen at a reboiler is 0.5 parts per million by volume,
with total hydrocarbons limited to 500 parts per million as
methane [5]. Two operating rules follow. The feed purification
that the Process panel places before the cold box is a safety
system, not a product-quality refinement [4]. And a liquid
inventory must never be boiled to dryness: dry boiling
concentrates every contaminant in the last of the liquid [5].
During cool-down, when the first liquid is forming in a warm,
newly cleaned plant, both rules apply from the first kilogram.

## Cold contact and brittle fracture

Equipment in the loop runs from 293 K down to about 84 K.
Contact with cold metal or liquid at those temperatures freezes
tissue on contact; gloves, face protection and loose-fitting
cryogenic clothing are the standard controls, and they matter
most during start-up, when manual checks at cold equipment are
frequent. Materials selection is the second control: plain
carbon steels lose ductility at low temperature and can fail by
brittle fracture, so cold equipment is built from stainless
steels, aluminium alloys or copper alloys rated for the service.
A warm plant being cooled is also a plant under thermal stress:
cool-down rates are limited — a vendor start-up procedure gives
a typical rate of 30 to 50 K per hour, set by the exchanger
design [14] — and exceeding the limit risks piping stress and
cold box structural damage rather than a faster start.

## Pressure from trapped liquid

A volume of liquid trapped between two closed valves warms
toward ambient temperature and vaporises. The liquid-to-gas
expansion ratio is about 700 for the components of air — 696
for nitrogen and 862 for oxygen, volumes of gas per volume of
liquid at ambient conditions [13] — so a trapped volume of
liquid becomes several hundred times that volume of gas in a
fixed volume, and the pressure
rise bursts the line unless a relief device is fitted. Every
section of cold piping that can be isolated with liquid in it
requires thermal relief. The risk is specific to the transient:
at steady state the liquid levels are known, while during
cool-down liquid is appearing in lines that were vapour-filled
an hour earlier.

## Deficient and enriched atmospheres

The loop's return stream and its vents discharge nitrogen-rich
gas. In a pit, a trench or an enclosed cold box, that gas
displaces air without any warning property: oxygen-deficient
atmospheres are a standing hazard around nitrogen vents and
cold equipment [8]. The accepted window for confined-space
entry is 19.5 to 23.5 percent oxygen by volume [7]; the upper
bound guards the enrichment side, where clothing and materials
saturated in oxygen-enriched air burn readily [6]. Start-up
venting is heavier and less steady than normal operation, so
vent locations and entry controls are checked before the
cool-down begins, not after the plant is cold.
