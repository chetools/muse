Air liquefaction: the cool-down

How a liquefaction unit gets cold from a warm start: one expansion makes the first cold, a regenerative heat exchanger banks it, and the cold end ratchets down over hours until the first liquid forms.

Expansion split. The turbine fraction is the share of the cold-end flow that expands through the turbine; the rest throttles through the valve. Experiment 1 of the Theory panel: set the fraction to 0 at 568 kPa and look for the cold end stalling near 282 K with no liquid. Experiment 3: run 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0 and look for the threshold between stall and liquefaction.

Feed pressure. The feed pressure sets the pressure ratio of both expansion devices and the size of the throttling effect. Experiment 2: with the turbine fraction at 0, set 20 000 kPa and look for first liquid at about 0.55 h and a cold end settling near 171 K — the historical operating point of the valve-only process.

Heat leak. Heat leaking into the cold box is a direct charge on the refrigeration surplus. Experiment 4: at the design basis, raise the leak in steps and look for the liquid rate falling, and liquefaction failing at 10 kW.

Exchanger size. The size factor scales the exchanger conductance. A smaller exchanger leaves a larger warm-end approach, and the cold carried out by the return stream is lost. Experiment 5: set 0.4 and look for the warm-end approach growing to about 18 K; then raise the heat leak to 6 kW and look for the stall.

Run length. The duration sets how much of the transient is shown. The design basis needs about 4 h to settle and 3.1 h to first liquid; a stalled run is visible within the first hours. Longer runs show the inventory reaching its 400 kg working level, after which liquid is drawn as product at the formation rate.
