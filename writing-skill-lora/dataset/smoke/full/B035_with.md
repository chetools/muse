A zeolite membrane gave a higher separation factor and an unchanged flux over 500 h than a polymer membrane in pervaporation of an ethanol-water feed, at a lower flux. Pervaporation is a membrane separation in which a liquid feed contacts one side of a membrane and vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through as vapour. Flux is the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour (kg m-2 h-1), and the separation factor is the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed (dimensionless). Feed was 85 wt% ethanol in water, at 60 °C; one feed composition was tested and no test ran beyond 500 h. The polymer membrane gave a flux of 1.5 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 120, and flux fell 7% over 500 h. The zeolite membrane gave a flux of 1.3 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 320, and flux was unchanged over 500 h within measurement error. Flux, separation factor and stability stand together for each membrane: the polymer membrane's flux advantage (1.5 against 1.3 kg m-2 h-1) is paired with the lower separation factor (120 against 320) and with the only flux decline reported (7% over 500 h). Flux, selectivity and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane. At the one composition tested (85 wt% ethanol in water, 60 °C, up to 500 h), the zeolite membrane holds the higher separation factor and the stable flux, and the polymer membrane holds the higher flux.
