Pervaporation is a membrane separation technique in which a liquid feed is placed in contact with one side of a membrane, while a vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through the membrane as a vapour. In this study, two membrane types were compared for the separation of isopropanol and water, using a feed containing 85 wt% isopropanol in water at 60 °C. Two key performance measures were used throughout the work. Flux is defined as the mass of material permeating per unit membrane area per hour, expressed in kg m-2 h-1, while the separation factor is defined as the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed, and is dimensionless.

The polymer membrane achieved a flux of 1.9 kg m-2 h-1, which was higher than the flux of 1.2 kg m-2 h-1 obtained with the zeolite membrane. However, the zeolite membrane gave a much higher separation factor of 560, compared with a value of only 120 for the polymer membrane, showing a clear trade-off between flux and selectivity in the results. In terms of stability, the flux of the polymer membrane fell by 12% over 500 h of operation, whereas the flux of the zeolite membrane remained unchanged over the same period, within measurement error.

It is important to note that only one feed composition was tested in this work, and no test was run for longer than 500 h, so the longer-term behaviour of both membranes remains unknown. Overall, the results show that the polymer membrane offers higher throughput, while the zeolite membrane offers better separation and better stability. Flux, selectivity and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane, and reporting all three here helps to give a more complete picture of how the two membrane types compare in practice for this particular separation task.
