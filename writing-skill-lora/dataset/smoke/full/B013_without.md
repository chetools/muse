Pervaporation is a membrane separation process in which a liquid feed is placed in contact with one side of a membrane, and a vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through the membrane as a vapour. In the present study, two membranes were tested using a feed consisting of 85 wt% n-butanol in water at a temperature of 60 °C. The performance of the membranes was characterised by the flux, which is the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour, in kg m-2 h-1, and by the separation factor, which is defined as the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed, and is dimensionless. Only one feed composition was tested, and no test was run for longer than 500 h. The polymer membrane produced a flux of 1.7 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 180, but its flux fell by 14% over 500 h. The zeolite membrane produced a flux of 1.1 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 560, and its flux remained unchanged over 500 h within measurement error. These results show that the polymer membrane provides the higher flux, whereas the zeolite membrane provides the higher separation factor together with better stability. Flux, selectivity and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane, so reporting all three here is valuable.
