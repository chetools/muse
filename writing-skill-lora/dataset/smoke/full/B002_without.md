Pervaporation is a membrane separation technique in which a liquid feed contacts one side of a membrane, while vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through as a vapour. In this study, two membranes were evaluated on a feed containing 85 wt% isopropanol in water at 60 °C, and their performance was compared using two key measures. The first measure is the flux, which is defined as the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour, expressed in kg m-2 h-1. The second measure is the separation factor, defined as the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed, and it is a dimensionless quantity. Only one feed composition was tested in this work, and no test ran for longer than 500 h. The polymer membrane achieved a flux of 1.5 kg m-2 h-1 together with a separation factor of 120, although its flux fell by 15% over the course of 500 h. In contrast, the zeolite membrane showed a lower flux of 0.9 kg m-2 h-1 but a much higher separation factor of 560, and its flux remained unchanged over 500 h within measurement error. Overall, these results demonstrate a clear trade-off: the polymer membrane offers higher flux, and the zeolite membrane offers higher selectivity along with better stability. Interestingly, flux, selectivity and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane, which makes this direct comparison particularly valuable.
