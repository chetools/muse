Pervaporation is a membrane separation process in which a liquid feed contacts one side of a membrane while vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through as a vapour. In this study, two membranes were compared for the separation of a feed containing 85 wt% ethanol in water at 60 °C. Performance is described by the flux, which is the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour, and by the separation factor, which is the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed.

The polymer membrane gave a flux of 2.1 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 240. Over 500 h, however, its flux fell by 9%. The zeolite membrane gave a lower flux of 0.9 kg m-2 h-1, but a much higher separation factor of 560, and its flux remained unchanged over 500 h within measurement error.

Overall, the two membranes offer a trade-off: the polymer membrane permeates faster, while the zeolite membrane separates more sharply and is more stable over time. It is important to note that only one feed composition was tested, and no test ran beyond 500 h, so the longer-term behaviour of either membrane remains unknown. Flux, selectivity and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane, which makes it difficult to compare membranes on all three aspects at once, and reporting all three for both membranes at the same feed composition gives a more complete picture of the difference between them than any one measure alone would provide in practice.
