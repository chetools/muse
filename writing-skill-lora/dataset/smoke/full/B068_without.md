This work investigates the use of pervaporation for separating ethanol and water, comparing two different membrane types under the same conditions. Pervaporation is a membrane separation process in which a liquid feed contacts one side of a membrane, while a vacuum applied on the other side draws the permeating component through the membrane as a vapour. Two key performance measures are considered here: the flux, which is the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour, in kg m-2 h-1, and the separation factor, which is defined as the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed, and is dimensionless.

The feed used in the experiments contained 85 wt% ethanol in water, and all tests were carried out at 60 °C. Only one feed composition was tested, and no test was run beyond 500 h, which frames the scope of the results presented.

The polymer membrane achieved a flux of 2.1 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 240, although its flux fell by 15% over 500 h of operation. The zeolite membrane, in contrast, gave a lower flux of 1.1 kg m-2 h-1 but a higher separation factor of 420, and its flux remained unchanged over 500 h within measurement error.

Taken together, these results illustrate the classic trade-off between flux and selectivity: the polymer membrane offers higher throughput, while the zeolite membrane offers greater selectivity and stability. It is also notable that flux, selectivity, and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane, making this combined comparison especially useful. In summary, for a feed of 85 wt% ethanol in water at 60 °C, the polymer membrane recorded a flux of 2.1 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 240 with a 15% fall in flux over 500 h, whereas the zeolite membrane recorded a flux of 1.1 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 420 with no change in flux over 500 h within measurement error, based on a single feed composition and tests lasting no longer than 500 h in this pervaporation study.
