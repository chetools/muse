In this worked example, we will look at a first-order reaction and calculate two important quantities: the half-life of the reaction and the fraction of the reactant that remains after 60 minutes have passed. For a first-order reaction, the rate constant k, which is expressed in min-1, determines the half-life t1/2 in min through the relationship t1/2 = ln(2) / k. In addition, the fraction of the reactant remaining after a time t in min is given by the expression exp(-k t), which shows how the amount decreases exponentially over time.

In the case considered here, the measured rate constant is k = 0.069 min-1 at a temperature of 323 K. This value was obtained from concentration measurements that were taken every 5 min over a period of 90 min, giving a good picture of how the concentration changed during the reaction.

Substituting the value of k into the half-life equation gives t1/2 = ln(2) / 0.069, which works out to a half-life of 10 min. This means that every 10 minutes, half of the reactant that is present at that point is consumed. Using the second expression, the fraction remaining after 60 min is exp(-0.069 x 60), which gives a value of 0.02, so only a very small proportion of the original reactant is left after that time, as one would expect after six half-lives have gone by.

It is important to remember, however, that these relationships are valid only for a first-order reaction. A reaction that follows a different order would behave in a different way and would follow a different decay law, so the same equations could not simply be applied to it. Both of the results reported here, the half-life of 10 min and the fraction remaining of 0.02 after 60 min, were computed directly from the stated relations using the measured rate constant, and together they give a clear summary of how quickly this particular reaction proceeds at 323 K under the conditions of the measurements described above.
