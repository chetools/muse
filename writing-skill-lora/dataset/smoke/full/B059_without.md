The rate constant k, expressed in s-1, of a first-order liquid-phase reaction was measured at two different temperatures in order to see how the rate of the reaction changes with temperature. At the lower temperature of 300 K, the rate constant was found to be k = 0.019 s-1. At the higher temperature of 340 K, the rate constant was much larger, at k = 0.541 s-1. This second value was not measured directly in the same way, but was computed from the Arrhenius relation using an activation energy of 71 kJ mol-1, so it should be understood as a derived value based on that relation and on the stated activation energy.

Each of the temperatures was measured in triplicate, which helps to give confidence in the results, and the relative standard deviation of k was 4%. This is a fairly small spread, indicating that the measurements were reasonably consistent with each other.

It is also worth mentioning that no rate constant is reported for temperatures above 340 K. The reason for this is that above 340 K the solvent started to evaporate while the samples were being taken, which meant that reliable measurements could not be made in that range. As a result, the data reported here cover only the interval from 300 K to 340 K, and the large increase in the rate constant between those two temperatures is the main finding that comes out of the measurements and the calculation described above.
