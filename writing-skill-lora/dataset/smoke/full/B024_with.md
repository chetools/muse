Pervaporation of an 85 wt% n-butanol feed in water at 60 °C gave a higher flux through a polymer membrane and a higher separation factor through a zeolite membrane, with flux, selectivity and stability reported together for the same membrane. Pervaporation is a membrane separation in which a liquid feed contacts one side of a membrane and vacuum on the other side draws the permeating component through as vapour. Flux is the mass permeating per unit membrane area per hour (kg m-2 h-1). The separation factor is the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed (dimensionless). The polymer membrane gave a flux of 1.8 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 120, and flux fell 6% over 500 h. The zeolite membrane gave a flux of 1 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 420, and flux was unchanged over 500 h within measurement error. Flux and separation factor rank the two membranes in opposite orders: the polymer membrane passes more mass per unit area per hour (1.8 against 1 kg m-2 h-1), and the zeolite membrane separates more strongly (a separation factor of 420 against 120). Stability over the test duration also differs, with a 6% fall in flux for the polymer membrane against no change within measurement error for the zeolite membrane. One feed composition was tested, and no test ran beyond 500 h. Behaviour at other feed compositions, and behaviour beyond 500 h, was not measured for either membrane. The feed is the same for both membranes, 85 wt% n-butanol in water at 60 °C, and the test duration is the same, 500 h; flux, separation factor and flux change over the duration are each stated for the membrane they describe.
