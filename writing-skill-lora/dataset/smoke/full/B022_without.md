In this example we will work out the half-life of a first-order reaction, and then find out what fraction of the reactant is still remaining after 60 minutes have passed. For a reaction that is first order, the half-life is directly connected to the rate constant through the relation t1/2 = ln(2) / k, where t1/2 is the half-life in minutes and k is the rate constant in min-1. In a similar way, the fraction of the reactant remaining after a time t is given by the exponential expression exp(-k t), which describes how the amount decreases as time goes on. The rate constant for the reaction considered here was measured as k = 0.069 min-1 at a temperature of 323 K. This value was obtained from concentration measurements that were taken every 5 min over a period of 90 min, so it is based on a whole series of measurements rather than on a single observation. Using the half-life relation, t1/2 = ln(2) / 0.069, which gives a half-life of 10 min. Using the fraction relation at t = 60 min, the fraction remaining is exp(-0.069 x 60), which comes out as 0.02, a dimensionless number meaning that only a small part of the original material is left after an hour. It is important to remember that these relations apply to a first-order reaction only. A reaction that follows a different order would decay in a different way and would be described by a different decay law, so the same formulas could not simply be carried across. The data behind the example are the measured rate constant of 0.069 min-1 at 323 K, taken from concentration measurements every 5 min over 90 min, and the two answers are a half-life of 10 min and a fraction remaining of 0.02 after 60 min.
