Pervaporation is a membrane separation technique in which a liquid feed is placed in contact with one side of a membrane, while a vacuum on the other side draws the component that permeates through the membrane as a vapour. Two key quantities are commonly used to describe the performance of a membrane in this process. The first is the flux, which is the mass of material permeating per unit membrane area per hour, in kg m-2 h-1. The second is the separation factor, which is defined as the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed, and is dimensionless.

In the tests described here, the feed was 85 wt% ethanol in water at a temperature of 60 °C. Only one feed composition was tested, and no test was run for longer than 500 h. The polymer membrane gave a flux of 1.9 kg m-2 h-1 and a separation factor of 180, but its flux fell by 14% over 500 h. The zeolite membrane, by contrast, gave a lower flux of 1.2 kg m-2 h-1 but a much higher separation factor of 560, and its flux remained unchanged over 500 h within measurement error.

Overall, these results show an interesting trade-off between the two membranes, and it is worth noting that flux, selectivity and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane, which makes comparisons of this kind particularly useful.
