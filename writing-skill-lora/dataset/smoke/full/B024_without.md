Pervaporation is a membrane separation technique in which a liquid feed is placed in contact with one side of a membrane, while a vacuum on the other side draws the component that permeates through the membrane as a vapour. Two measures are used to describe the performance of the membranes in this study. The first is the flux, which is the mass that permeates per unit membrane area per hour, in kg m-2 h-1. The second is the separation factor, defined as the water-to-alcohol ratio in the permeate divided by the same ratio in the feed, which is a dimensionless quantity. The feed used in the tests was 85 wt% n-butanol in water, and the tests were run at 60 °C. Only one feed composition was tested, and no test was allowed to run beyond 500 h. The polymer membrane produced a flux of 1.8 kg m-2 h-1 with a separation factor of 120, although its flux fell by 6% over the course of 500 h. The zeolite membrane produced a lower flux of 1 kg m-2 h-1, but its separation factor was much higher at 420, and its flux remained unchanged over 500 h within measurement error. It is frequently the case that flux, selectivity and stability are seldom reported together for the same membrane, so reporting all three together, as is done here, allows the two membranes to be compared across flux, selectivity and stability at the same time, with the polymer membrane offering the higher flux and the zeolite membrane offering the higher separation factor and the steadier performance over the full test duration of 500 h. In summary, the polymer membrane is the faster of the two membranes, and the zeolite membrane is the more selective and the more stable of the two over the 500 h for which it was tested at 60 °C.
