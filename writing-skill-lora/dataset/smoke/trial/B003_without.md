Carbon capture from industrial sources such as cement kilns is widely seen as an important part of efforts to reduce carbon dioxide emissions. In this work, an amine absorption unit was tested at pilot scale on gas taken from a cement kiln, in order to see how the technology performs outside the laboratory.

The pilot unit was operated over a period of 60 days, treating gas at a rate corresponding to 13 t of carbon dioxide per day. Over this period, the unit captured 90% of the carbon dioxide in the treated gas, which is an encouraging result for a pilot-scale system. The energy requirement of the process was also measured: the steam demand was 3.2 gigajoules per tonne of carbon dioxide captured. In addition, the solvent loss was 2 kg per tonne captured, which is relevant to the operating cost of the process.

Overall, the results show that the unit could sustain a high capture rate over two months of operation while consuming steam and solvent at the levels reported above. However, some questions remain open. In particular, beyond 60 days, the effect of kiln dust on the solvent is untested, so longer-term operation could still reveal problems that did not appear during the trial. Further work over longer periods would therefore be valuable to confirm that the performance seen here can be maintained.
